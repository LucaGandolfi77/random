**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1, calculus of variations, vector calculus  
**Objective:** To derive Newton's law of universal gravitation from first observational principles, to construct the gravitational potential, and to solve the two-body problem exactly — yielding Kepler's laws as consequences, not postulates.

## 2.1 Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

The gravitational force between two point masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \) separated by a distance \( r \) is directed along the line connecting them, attractive, and proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the separation:

\[
\mathbf{F}_{12} = - G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}
\]

where \( \hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r \) is the unit vector directed from \( m_1 \) to \( m_2 \), and \( G = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11} \, \mathrm{m^3 \cdot kg^{-1} \cdot s^{-2}} \) is the gravitational constant. The negative sign indicates attraction.

The derivation of this law from observational data (Kepler's laws) is instructive. Consider a body of mass \( m \) orbiting a central mass \( M \). The force is central:

\[
\mathbf{F} = F(r) \hat{\mathbf{r}} = - \frac{G M m}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}
\]

Because the force is central and conservative, the angular momentum \( \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} \) is conserved. In the plane of motion (\( \theta = \pi/2 \)):

\[
L = m r^2 \dot{\phi} = \mathrm{constant}
\]

The radial equation of motion is:

\[
m \left( \ddot{r} - r \dot{\phi}^2 \right) = - \frac{G M m}{r^2}
\]

Substituting \( \dot{\phi} = L/(m r^2) \):

\[
\ddot{r} - \frac{L^2}{m^2 r^3} = - \frac{G M}{r^2}
\]

This is a second-order nonlinear ordinary differential equation. To solve it, introduce the substitution \( u = 1/r \). Then:

\[
\dot{r} = - \frac{L}{m} \frac{du}{d\phi}
\]

and

\[
\ddot{r} = - \frac{L}{m} \frac{d}{d\phi} \left( \dot{r} \right) = - \frac{L^2}{m^2} u^2 \frac{d^2 u}{d\phi^2}
\]

Substituting into the radial equation and simplifying yields:

\[
\frac{d^2 u}{d\phi^2} + u = \frac{G M m^2}{L^2}
\]

This is the Binet equation for an inverse-square law. Its solution is a conic section:

\[
u(\phi) = \frac{G M m^2}{L^2} \left( 1 + e \cos(\phi - \phi_0) \right)
\]

or, in terms of \( r \):

\[
r(\phi) = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos(\phi - \phi_0)}
\]

where \( p = L^2/(G M m^2) \) is the semi-latus rectum and \( e \) is the eccentricity. If \( 0 \leq e < 1 \), the orbit is an ellipse; \( e = 0 \) is a circle; \( e = 1 \) is a parabola; \( e > 1 \) is a hyperbola.

The derivation is complete. Kepler's first law (elliptical orbits) and second law (equal areas in equal times) follow directly. The third law (\( T^2 \propto a^3 \)) is obtained by integrating the area:

\[
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\phi} = \frac{L}{2m} = \mathrm{constant}
\]

For one complete orbit:

\[
A = \pi a b = \frac{L}{2m} T
\]

Using \( b = a \sqrt{1 - e^2} \) and \( p = a(1 - e^2) = L^2/(G M m^2) \), one finds:

\[
T^2 = \frac{4 \pi^2}{G M} a^3
\]

This is Kepler's third law, derived without approximation.

## 2.2 The Gravitational Potential and Potential Energy

The potential energy \( U \) associated with the gravitational force is defined by:

\[
\mathbf{F} = - \nabla U
\]

For the inverse-square force:

\[
U(r) = - \frac{G M m}{r}
\]

The gravitational potential \( \Phi \) (potential per unit mass) is:

\[
\Phi(r) = - \frac{G M}{r}
\]

This potential satisfies Poisson's equation:

\[
\nabla^2 \Phi = 4 \pi G \rho
\]

where \( \rho \) is the mass density. In vacuum (\( \rho = 0 \)), this reduces to Laplace's equation \( \nabla^2 \Phi = 0 \
\). The potential is singular at \( r = 0 \), which is the mathematical statement that point masses produce singular gravitational fields. In physical reality, extended bodies smooth this singularity over their volume.

The total mechanical energy of an orbiting body is:

\[
E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + U(r) = - \frac{G M m}{2a}
\]

where \( a \) is the semi-major axis. This is the virial theorem applied to gravitational systems: the time-averaged kinetic energy is half the magnitude of the time-averaged potential energy, so \( \langle T \rangle = - \frac{1}{2} \langle U \rangle \) and \( E = \langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle = - \langle T \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \langle U \rangle \).

## 2.3 The Two-Body Problem: Reduced Mass and Center of Mass

For two masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \), the equations of motion are:

\[
m_1 \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_1 = - G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|^3} (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)
\]

\[
m_2 \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = + G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|^3} (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)
\]

Define the center-of-mass position:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \frac{m_1 \mathbf{r}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{r}_2}{m_1 + m_2}
\]

and the relative coordinate:

\[
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2
\]

Then \( \ddot{\mathbf{R}} = 0 \), so the center of mass moves with constant velocity. The equation for \( \mathbf{r} \) becomes:

\[
\mu \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = - G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^3} \mathbf{r}
\]

where \( \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \) is the reduced mass. This is identical to the one-body problem with total mass \( M = m_1 + m_2 \). All results derived above apply directly, with \( \mu \) replacing \( m \) and \( M \) replacing the central mass.

This reduction is not an approximation. It is exact. It demonstrates that the gravitational interaction of two bodies is geometrically equivalent to the motion of a single particle of reduced mass in the potential of a fixed central mass.

## 2.4 The Virial Theorem and Its Astrophysical Applications

The virial theorem relates the time-averaged kinetic and potential energies of a system in steady state. For a system of \( N \) particles interacting via gravity:

\[
2 \langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle = 0
\]

The proof uses the identity:

\[
\frac{d}{dt} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{p}_i \cdot \mathbf{r}_i = 2 \langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle
\]

and assumes that the left-hand side is bounded in time (i.e., the system does not expand or collapse to infinity). For a self-gravitating system with total potential energy \( U \) and total kinetic energy \( T \), the virial theorem implies:

\[
E = \langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle = - \langle T \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \langle U \rangle
\]

This result is fundamental to the study of stellar clusters, galaxies, and the universe as a whole. It provides an independent estimate of mass from velocity dispersion measurements — the original evidence for dark matter in galaxy clusters.

## 2.7 Note on the Measure

The gravitational law is not a simple claim. It is the claim that connects the observer to the star, the star to the galaxy, the galaxy to the universe. It measures not only distance, but the relationship between all things that have mass. And mass, as we have seen, is the measure that counts not the objects alone, but the space between them. The claim is settled when the orbit closes. The universe is settled when every orbit is understood. This is the work of astrophysics.

## 2.12 Literary Reflection: The Underground Orbit — Dostoevsky and the Freedom of the Claim

Dostoevsky's underground man asks whether freedom is worth the suffering it brings. The gravitational claim asks the same question, but in the language of orbits. A free orbit — an unbound hyperbolic trajectory — is the orbit of freedom. It does not return. It does not settle. It asks forever, moving away from the center, refusing to be captured. The bound orbit — the ellipse — is the claim that has accepted its limit. It returns. It repeats. It settles. But the settlement is not defeat. It is the choice of the claim to remain in relationship with the center.

The virial theorem is the underground man's confession: the kinetic energy and the potential energy are not enemies. They are the two sides of the same claim. The claim is not solved by destroying one side. It is solved by recognizing that both sides must exist, must measure each other, must keep each other in balance. The claim is not false when it is in tension. It is false only when it denies the tension — when it claims to be only kinetic, or only potential, or only free, or only bound.

And so the orbit is not only a mathematical curve. It is the shape of a life. The elliptical life is the life that returns, that remembers its origin, that is bound by love (the inverse-square law). The hyperbolic life is the life that escapes, that asks the question forever, that refuses the settlement but pays the price of isolation. Both are true. Both are claims. And the astrophysicist, reading the orbit, reads not only the mass and the velocity, but the choice of the claim — to remain, or to leave, to remember, or to forget.

## 2.8 Advanced Derivation: The Energy of an Elliptical Orbit

The total mechanical energy of a body of reduced mass \( \mu \) in an orbit with semi-major axis \( a \) and eccentricity \( e \) is constant and equal to:

\[
E = - \frac{G M \mu}{2a}
\]

To derive this explicitly, begin with the kinetic energy in terms of radial and tangential components:

\[
T = \frac{1}{2} \mu \left( \dot{r}^2 + r^2 \dot{\phi}^2 \right)
\]

Using \( \dot{\phi} = L/\mu r^2 \) and the radial equation:

\[
\dot{r}^2 = \frac{2}{\mu} \left( E - U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) \right)
\]

where the effective potential is:

\[
U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = - \frac{G M \mu}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}
\]

The turning points of the orbit occur where \( \dot{r} = 0 \), i.e., where \( E = U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) \). Solving the quadratic equation in \( 1/r \) yields the two roots \( r_{\mathrm{min}} \) and \( r_{\mathrm{max}} \), which are the periapsis and apoapsis:

\[
r_{\mathrm{min}} = a(1 - e), \quad r_{\mathrm{max}} = a(1 + e)
\]

Substituting these into \( E = U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_{\mathrm{min}}) \) and using \( L^2 = G M \mu^2 a (1 - e^2) \) yields the result \( E = -G M \mu / (2a) \). This derivation is exact and requires no approximation other than the point-mass assumption.

The time-averaged kinetic energy is:

\[
\langle T \rangle = -E = \frac{G M \mu}{2a}
\]

and the time-averaged potential energy is:

\[
\langle U \rangle = 2E = - \frac{G M \mu}{a}
\]

This confirms the virial theorem \( 2\langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle = 0 \) for this specific potential.

## 2.9 The Perihelion Precession: First Relativistic Correction

For a nearly circular orbit, the first-order relativistic correction to the orbital equation introduces an additional term to the Binet equation:

\[
\frac{d^2 u}{d\phi^2} + u = \frac{G M m^2}{L^2} + \frac{3 G M}{c^2} u^2
\]

The solution is not a closed ellipse but a precessing ellipse. The precessional rate per orbit is:

\[
\Delta \phi = \frac{6 \pi G M}{c^2 a (1 - e^2)}
\]

For Mercury (\( a \approx 5.79 \times 10^{10} \, \mathrm{m} \), \( e \approx 0.206 \)), this yields \( \Delta \phi \approx 43 \) arcseconds per century, in agreement with observation and providing one of the classical tests of general relativity.

## 2.10 Historical Note: From Kepler to Newton

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) derived his three laws from the meticulous observations of Tycho Brahe (1546–1601). The laws were empirical. Isaac Newton (1643–1727) derived them from a single physical principle: the inverse-square law of gravitation. The derivation shown in Section 2.1 is the mathematical bridge between observation and theory. It demonstrates that the claim of the universe — the claim that holds the planets in their orbits — is not an arbitrary rule, but the necessary consequence of the geometry of space and the conservation of angular momentum.

The modern astrophysicist does not merely observe orbits. The astrophysicist derives them, measures them, and uses their deviations to discover new claims — the claims of dark matter, of relativistic corrections, of the curvature of spacetime itself.

## 2.11 Note on the Dynamics of N-Body Systems

For \( N > 2 \) bodies, the problem has no closed-form solution (the three-body problem is non-integrable in general). Numerical integration using symplectic integrators (e.g., the Verlet algorithm or Hamiltonian splitting methods) is required. The symplectic property ensures that the numerical trajectory conserves energy and angular momentum over long integration times, which is essential for studies of planetary dynamics, star cluster evolution, and galaxy mergers.

The claim of gravity is not settled for two bodies alone. It is settled for every body, in every orbit, in every cluster, in every galaxy. The measure is the same: the mass, the distance, the constant \( G \). The universe is the accumulation of these claims.
