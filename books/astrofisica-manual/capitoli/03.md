# Chapter 3 — Stellar Structure: Hydrostatic Equilibrium and the Equation of State

**Level:** Intermediate  
**Objective:** To derive the equations of stellar structure from first principles: mass conservation, hydrostatic equilibrium, and energy transport.

---

## 3.1 Mass Conservation

Consider a spherical shell of radius \( r \) and thickness \( dr \) within a star. The mass contained in the shell is:

\[
dM = 4\pi r^2 \rho(r) \, dr
\]

where \( \rho(r) \) is the density profile. Integrating from the center (\( r = 0 \)) to the surface (\( r = R \)) gives the total stellar mass:

\[
M = \int_0^R 4\pi r^2 \rho(r) \, dr
\]

This is the equation of continuity in spherical symmetry.

---

## 3.2 Hydrostatic Equilibrium

For a star in equilibrium, the outward pressure force balances the inward gravitational force on each shell. The pressure at radius \( r \) is \( P(r) \), and the gravitational acceleration is \( g(r) = G M(r)/r^2 \). Force balance yields:

\[
\frac{dP}{dr} = - \rho(r) g(r) = - \frac{G M(r) \rho(r)}{r^2}
\]

This is the equation of hydrostatic equilibrium. It must be solved together with the equation of state \( P = P(\rho, T) \), the energy equation, and the opacity law. The system is a coupled set of nonlinear ordinary differential equations, solved numerically in modern stellar evolution codes.

---

## 3.3 The Equation of State

For an ideal gas (appropriate for the outer layers of main-sequence stars):

\[
P = \frac{\rho k_B T}{\mu m_H}
\]

where \( \mu \) is the mean molecular weight (\( \mu \approx 0.6 \) for fully ionized hydrogen-helium mixtures) and \( m_H = 1.673 \times 10^{-27} \, \mathrm{kg} \) is the mass of the hydrogen atom.

For degenerate electron gas (appropriate for white dwarfs):

The pressure is given by the Fermi-Dirac integral. In the non-relativistic limit:

\[
P = K_1 \left( \frac{\rho}{\mu_e} \right)^{5/3}
\]

where \( \mu_e \approx 2 \) for carbon-oxygen white dwarfs and \( K_1 = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{2/3} \hbar^2}{5 m_e m_u^{5/3}} \approx 9.91 \times 10^6 \, \mathrm{dyn \cdot cm^2 \cdot g^{-5/3}} \).

In the ultra-relativistic limit:

\[
P = K_2 \left( \frac{\rho}{\mu_e} \right)^{4/3}
\]

with \( K_2 = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{1/3} \hbar c}{4 m_u^{4/3}} \). This transition determines the Chandrasekhar limit.

---

## 3.4 The Lane-Emden Equation and Polytropic Models

For a polytropic equation of state \( P = K \rho^{1 + 1/n} \), the hydrostatic equilibrium equation becomes the Lane-Emden equation. Define \( \theta(\xi) \) such that \( \rho = \rho_c \theta^n \) and \( r = \alpha \xi \), where \( \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{(n+1) K}{4\pi G} \rho_c^{(1-n)/n}} \). Substituting yields:

\[
\frac{1}{\xi^2} \frac{d}{d\xi} \left( \xi^2 \frac{d\theta}{d\xi} \right) = - \theta^n
\]

With boundary conditions \( \theta(0) = 1 \) and \( \theta'(0) = 0 \). For \( n = 0 \) (uniform density), \( \theta(\xi) = 1 - \xi^2/6 \), giving \( R = \alpha \sqrt{6} \). For \( n = 1 \), \( \theta(\xi) = \sin(\xi)/\xi \), with the first root at \( \xi_1 = \pi \). For \( n = 3 \) (ultra-relativistic degenerate gas), no analytical solution exists; numerical integration gives \( \xi_1 \approx 6.897 \), which determines the Chandrasekhar limit when combined with the equation of state.

## 3.5 Energy Transport: Radiative and Convective Equilibrium

The temperature gradient in a star is determined by the mode of energy transport. In radiative equilibrium:

\[
\frac{dT}{dr} = - \frac{3 \kappa \rho}{16 \sigma_{\mathrm{SB}} T^3} \frac{L(r)}{4\pi r^2}
\]

where \( \kappa \) is the opacity (in \( \mathrm{m^2 \cdot kg^{-1}} \)), \( L(r) \) is the luminosity interior to \( r \), and \( \sigma_{\mathrm{SB}} \) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.

If the temperature gradient exceeds the adiabatic gradient:

\[
\left| \frac{dT}{dr} \right|_{\mathrm{rad}} > \left| \frac{dT}{dr} \right|_{\mathrm{ad}} = \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \right) \frac{T}{P} \frac{dP}{dr}
\]

then convection occurs. The Schwarzschild criterion for convective instability is:

\[
\nabla_{\mathrm{rad}} > \nabla_{\mathrm{ad}}
\]

where \( \nabla = d\ln T / d\ln P \). In the Sun, convection dominates in the outer \( \sim 30\% \) by radius; in massive stars, the core is convective due to the steep temperature dependence of the CNO cycle.

The opacity \( \kappa \) is a complex function of density, temperature, and composition. The Kramers opacity law (approximate):

\[
\kappa \approx \kappa_0 \rho T^{-3.5}
\]

is useful for analytical estimates in the stellar envelope, but modern stellar evolution uses tabulated opacities (e.g., OPAL or OP tables) that include bound-free, free-free, and electron scattering contributions.

## 3.6 Note: The Structure of a Star as a System of Equations

The complete system of stellar structure consists of four coupled first-order ordinary differential equations:

\[
\frac{dM}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho
\]

\[
\frac{dP}{dr} = - \frac{G M \rho}{r^2}
\]

\[
\frac{dL}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho \epsilon
\]

\[
\frac{dT}{dr} = - \frac{3 \kappa \rho L}{16 \pi a c r^2 T^3}
\]

with \( \epsilon \) the nuclear energy generation rate and \( a = 4\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}/c \) the radiation constant. Solving this system requires numerical integration from the center (with boundary conditions \( M(0) = 0 \), \( L(0) = 0 \), and a specified central temperature \( T_c \)) to the surface (where \( P = 0 \), \( T = T_{\mathrm{eff}} \), and \( L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma_{\mathrm{SB}} T_{\mathrm{eff}}^4 \)).

This system is not a simple claim. It is the claim that connects the observer's measurement of light to the physical conditions at the center of the star — conditions of temperature, density, and pressure that exceed anything achievable in terrestrial laboratories. The star is the claim that holds itself together by its own weight. And the equation that describes it is the equation that holds the universe together.

## 3.7 Literary Reflection: Tolstoy's Garden and the Layered Star

Tolstoy writes that the garden grows whether we count it or not. The star is the same. It does not ask whether we observe its layers — the core, the radiative zone, the convective envelope. It holds them regardless. The equation of hydrostatic equilibrium is not a command; it is the garden's habit. It grows from the inside, not because it is ordered to grow, but because the small things — the pressure, the density, the temperature — accumulate their attention, and the accumulation becomes the star.

The polytropic index is not a number. It is the measure of the star's patience. The Lane-Emden equation is not a differential equation alone. It is the question that the star asks of itself: how much can I hold before I must change? The answer is not in the equation. It is in the solution — the root \( \xi_1 \), the surface, the boundary where the claim is settled and the garden ends. The star does not solve the equation. It lives it. And the astrophysicist, reading the equation, reads not only physics, but the patience of the star — the patience of all things that grow in layers, that hold themselves together by attention, that become large not by force, but by the accumulation of small truths.
