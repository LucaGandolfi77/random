# Chapter 4 — The Gloves

**POV:** Ciro · **Place:** Milan (the warehouse of the Germoglio, the gardens) · **Story time:** winter–spring 2024
**Words:** ~1.150 / translation of `il-permesso/capitoli/04.md` ✅

---

## Draft

Planting began in March, and planting, in March, is the thing you don't think: you do it. The plants in pots waited in the warehouse, and the plants that wait, in the morning, are the plants you don't buy: you plant them. We planted them in the gardens, one by one, and planted plants, in the morning, are the plants you don't see: you see them in spring.

Vanda said that plants planted in March aren't seen in March: they're seen in spring. And spring, in Milan, comes the way true things come: all at once, and without being asked.

The gloves were the usual gloves: the ones for those who carry. Hands, in gloves, are the hands of someone who does everything: they're kept. The work with the hands, in the morning, is the work you don't think: you do it. And the work you do, in the morning, holds your life. It was the first thing in five years that didn't make fear. I didn't know it, and I knew it in books: on an estate, once, a man who had everything discovered that the only thing that doesn't make fear is the work with the hands (Tolstoy, *War and Peace*). The work with the hands, in the morning, is the work you don't think: you do it. And the not thinking, at thirty-five, was the thing you don't buy: you receive it.

At noon I ate standing with the two-euro sandwich, and the sandwich, in the garden, is the sandwich of those who come back: you eat standing. Vanda drank the coffee from the machine, and the coffee, at noon, is the coffee you don't choose: it takes you. In the books Adele had given me there was a coffee like that, in a factory of voices: long, lukewarm, nobody chose them. It was the same coffee. The world, in the morning, has one coffee, and nobody chooses it: and the things nobody chooses, in the morning, are the things that hold you.

Vanda asked me a thing, once, without my asking anything like it:

— And what do you plant? —

It was Vanda's question, and the question, in the morning, was the question I didn't answer: I didn't know. The true answer was one: nothing. I planted nothing. I planted the cooperative's plants, and the cooperative's plants weren't mine: they were plants. The true answer was the other: I planted time. Ten hours a day, or nine and a half, which is the same account. And planted time, in the morning, you don't see: you see it at the end of the sentence.

— The plants — I said, in the end, with the voice of someone who doesn't complain. — And time. —

It was the first time I answered that question, and the answer, at thirty-five, was the smallest answer in the world: the plants, and time. Small answers, at thirty-five, are the answers you keep: the big ones are said, and stay in your hand. Vanda didn't ask anything. Heavy things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

I made the first request in May, to the educator, with the voice of someone who doesn't complain. The educator was called Nunzio, and Nunzio was forty-five, and he signed the sheets: he signed them and didn't look at them, as is their habit, sheets. I asked for a book, and the request, to the educator, is the request that isn't made: it's made, at thirty-five, when books are home.

— Which book? — Nunzio said, and the question was the rule: the rule you answer with a number.

— One that waits — I said.

It was the answer that wasn't in the form, and the answer outside the form, to the educator, is the thing you keep: you keep it the way you keep doors that close. Nunzio signed, and Nunzio's hands, on the sheet, were the hands of someone who gives: they give without asking. The book arrived on Thursday, from the library, and the book was for me, and the book was also for someone who didn't ask: Salvatore, the boy in the class.

Hands, in the morning, asked: and the question, in the morning, isn't contested: it's listened to.
