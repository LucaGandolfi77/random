# Chapter 7 — Sunday

**POV:** Ciro · **Place:** Milan (cell 23, the phone) · **Story time:** 2025, spring
**Words:** ~1.200 / translation of `il-permesso/capitoli/07.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

The Sunday calls I made at noon, and noon, in Milan, is noon in the barra too: the same sun, the same clock, and no time difference. Time difference, in the books of the girl I read, was the thing you feel: you're together and you're not together. I had no time difference: I had the same sun. And the same sun, on Sunday, is the thing you don't buy: you have it, or you don't, and I had it.

Assunta answered always, and Assunta didn't ask *are you well?*. The mothers of the barra don't ask: they know. The answer, on the phone, isn't said: it's sent in instalments. In Milan, in the books of the girl I read, the mother asked *but at least you're well?*, and the question was the true one. Questions, in Milan, are the things you make: mothers ask. In the barra mothers don't ask: they know. It's the reversal, and the reversal, at thirty-six, is the thing you feel in your legs.

— Hi, mum — I said, with the voice that doesn't complain.

— Hi, my love — Assunta said.

It was the first lie, and the first, usually, is the time you learn to say it. I didn't say it well. Lies, when they begin, you hear: you hear them the way you feel damp in walls. Assunta heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

The Sunday calls lasted ten minutes, and the ten minutes, on Sunday, are the longest ten minutes in the world: they're kept. But the calls, at thirty-six, were the calls that changed: the voice, on the phone, was the new voice. The subjunctive, on the phone, bent: *if I could, if I were*. And Assunta felt it, and Assunta didn't ask: she knew. Heavy things aren't asked: they're listened to.

— But who are you? — Assunta said, one of those Sundays, with the voice of someone who says the thing she never said.

It was the question nobody asked me, and the question, on the phone, was the truest question of the month: you feel that the other knows. Assunta knew it: she knew the voice had changed, and the changed voice, on the phone, is the thing you notice: you notice it the way you notice damp in walls. I didn't answer. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the phone, the way you keep the things that aren't needed.

— It's me — I said, in the end, with the voice of someone who doesn't complain.

— It's me — Assunta said. — For six years. —

It was the truest thing of the Sunday, and the true thing, on the phone, isn't contested: it's listened to. Assunta hadn't asked *are you well?* for six years: she knew the answer, and the answer, in the barra, isn't said: it's built. And the answer, at thirty-six, I was building it: ten hours a day, with the garden, with the books, with the subjunctive.

— And the book? — Assunta said, the Sunday after, with the voice of someone who says the thing she wants to say. — Your aunt says you study. —

It was the second true thing of the Sunday, and the second, usually, is the time you learn to stay. Assunta knew about the study, and she knew it from outside: things are known, in the barra, without being said. — They study Leopardi — I said. — And Leopardi, on the phone, isn't explained: it's read. —

— And does he read to you? — Assunta said, and the question was the true one: the question nobody asked me.

— I read to him — I said. — And he reads to me. —

It was the truest thing of the Sunday, and the true thing, on the phone, isn't contested: it's listened to. Assunta hadn't asked *are you well?* for six years: she knew the answer, and the answer, in the barra, isn't said: it's built. And the answer, at thirty-six, I was building it: ten hours a day, with the garden, with the books, with the subjunctive.

In the evening, in the cell, I looked at the phone. The phone was the usual one, and the phone, at night, is the phone you don't buy: you keep it. The lies, at night, are kept: they're kept at the bottom of the drawer, the way you keep the things that aren't needed. But the lies, at thirty-six, were the lies that stop: they stop the way true things stop, and true things, at thirty-six, were beginning to arrive.

My mother knew it, and my mother didn't ask: mothers who don't ask, usually, know the answer. And the answer, at thirty-six, I was building it.
