# Prologue — Ten Hours


> "Man is a being that gets used to everything."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Memories from the House of the Dead*

The gate opens at half past seven, and the gate, at half past seven, opens to make you come back in. I didn't know it the first time I went out, on the first of March: I believed it opened to let you go. It doesn't open to let you go. It opens to make you come back in: it's the law of the gate, and I learned the law in six years, and I learned it well, because it's the only school I had.

The gate was iron, and the iron, in the morning, was freezing: cold, in Milan, is the cold you don't expect: you put up with it. I stood at the stop with the folded sheet in my hand, the way you hold a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of her own life, and that the numbers are never good. The sheet said my name pronounced wrong, my face printed, and the writing: *work release*. Work release. At thirty-four, work release is the thing you keep: you keep it the way you keep the things you don't buy.

Ten hours of freedom a day. Not ten hours: the hours outside are nine and a half, and the half is the bus's, and the bus is the system's. The ten hours are said to queues, to visits, on the phone: *ten hours a day outside*. It isn't like that. The ten hours are nine and a half, and the nine and a half divide like this: the bus two, the garden seven, and the rest is the rest. And the rest, in the morning, is the coffee at the stop: fifty cents, short, lukewarm, nobody chooses it. It takes you. In the books of the girl I read there was a coffee like that, in a factory of voices: long, lukewarm, nobody chose them. It was the same coffee. The world, in the morning, has one coffee, and nobody chooses it: and the things nobody chooses are the things that hold you.

The count is at six. I return at half past five, and the half is the gate's, and the gate is the system's. Lateness, at the return, is the revocation: the rule says so, and the rule isn't contested. It's taken. The code of the barra said one thing only: don't speak. The rule of the prison says one thing only: come back. And the two rules are the same rule, said from two sides: don't speak, come back. You don't come back, you speak. You can live on two rules for six years, and it felt like the opposite of freedom. It was the opposite of freedom.

Milan didn't look at me. The city, in the morning, walked without looking, and people without the sheet walked the way things walk: for real. I walked with the sheet, and the sheet was the thing that made me see: not the city. The sheet. In the barra the code wasn't carried in the hand: it was carried in the mouth, and in the mouth it wasn't said. Heavy things aren't said. They're kept, and you cross with them.

This is the story of two years, and of how a man comes back to the straight path ten hours a day — or nine and a half, which is the same account. The straight path, in the barra, was a line: people spoke of it, and didn't take it. I take it for two years, ten hours a day, and I haven't found it yet: the garden remakes it, the sheet remakes it, the books remake it.

I begin at the beginning, which was a gate, and a gate opens to make you come back in.

## A note on the allusions

The protagonist's reflections contain subtle allusions to Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Proust and Leopardi, marked in parentheses: they are not quotations — they are ways of thinking borrowed, the way languages are borrowed. The arc of the voice is the arc of the book: the first reflections are the street's, and the allusions enter one by one, the way light enters hedges. The epigraphs at the start of the chapters are real quotations: Fyodor Dostoevsky (*Memories from the House of the Dead*, *The Brothers Karamazov*), Leo Tolstoy (*War and Peace*, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*), Giacomo Leopardi (*L'infinito*, *A Silvia*, *The Village Saturday*, *Zibaldone*, *La ginestra*).
