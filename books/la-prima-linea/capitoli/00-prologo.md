## Draft

> "It seemed to them that very little was missing before a new, marvelous life would begin…"
>
> — Anton Chekhov, *The Lady with the Dog*

My father's overalls are still in the wardrobe, and the wardrobe stands in the room that was his, and the room has been mine for ten years.

The overalls are blue, and the blue is grey now: the foundry's colour eats it, and it eats it slowly, the way dust has its habit — it doesn't shout, it settles. In the right pocket there's the bottle, the plastic kind, and the bottle has been empty for ten years, and the empty things, in my house, are the things you don't throw away: you keep them. You keep them the way you keep bottles, which were once full and are now glass, and glass, in the light, does what glass does: it shines. My father used to say the foundry is beautiful at night, and that being beautiful at night is not a merit: it's a shift.

I work the second shift at Fonderie Valsassina, and Fonderie Valsassina makes aluminium, and aluminium melts at seven hundred degrees, and seven hundred degrees, in winter, are the only thing that keeps you warm. The shift starts at two and ends at ten, and my body learned the shift the way you learn a language: slowly, and without wanting to. At the pour you put the mask on, and the mask is a mask that breathes: the glass, after ten years, has taken the film that bar windows take after a hundred years. You look through it, that's all. The helmet goes on at the pour, always: the head is the only thing the foundry protects. Outside the foundry, nobody ever cared about my head.

My father was a foundryman for thirty years, all of them on the pour line, and the pour line is where the white comes out: molten aluminium is white, and white is the only thing at the foundry that isn't tired. Thirty years of white in the eyes, and the dusts you don't weigh: you measure them in years, not kilos. You breathe them.

Rino Viganò, fifty-four years old, broad shoulders, black hands that soap wouldn't take. My father's hands were the foundry's hands, and the foundry stays in the hands: the heat doesn't leave, the cold doesn't arrive. I have his hands. I look at them sometimes, when I'm holding the glass, and the glass is where hands show: they show, that's all.

In 2014 they told him the thing, and the thing had a name he didn't tell me. I learned it through cousin Franca, who learned it from the third department's wife, who learned it from the file. At the foundry everyone knows the things except the ones who should.

He and I had been alone since 2006, when my mother left, and leaving, in our house, was a trade: it took two. The two of us who stayed learned one thing only: don't ask each other anything. The heavy things you don't ask. You carry them. You carry them the way you carry a shift: you show up at two, you close at ten.

The night he got worse, I didn't go. I was supposed to go and I didn't go. I was at the bar, and there was a match on, and that November the match didn't matter. I learned it that night from a message I didn't read until late, and late, on a bar night, is a place you don't go back to: you stay.

I found him at the department the next morning, and he was standing the way he always stood: straight, hands in the pockets of the overalls, the mask on his shoulder. He looked at me the way that was his habit: looking and saying nothing. He said nothing. I said nothing. We were two men who had missed each other by one night, and a missed night, between two men who say nothing, doesn't come back: it gets rounded off.

He died in March 2016. The foundry was at the funeral: three coaches, the ceremony overalls, the department heads in jackets. The priest said the things they say, and the things they say at foundry funerals are the things you don't hear: you nod. I nodded. I was twenty-three, with a house, a shift, and nothing else. The house was his house, the shift was his department, and the nothing else was the nothing else: you look at it, that's all.

I drank the first bottle the evening after the funeral, alone, in the kitchen, and it wasn't a big one: one of the ones you open and don't finish. I drank it to sleep, and sleep came at four, when it was already morning, and the morning, after a night of not sleeping, is the morning of a man with a shift. I got up, went to the shift, and the shift was as always. From then on, a bottle a night. A bottle a night, and a bottle a night, and bottles, in the long run, are nights: you count them, and you don't tell them. I did the count once, alone, the way the things you count alone have their habit: three thousand nights, about.

I don't call myself an alcoholic. The alcoholic is someone else: he's the one who drinks in the morning, and I don't drink in the morning, because in the morning there's the foundry, and at the foundry hands shake, and hands that shake don't hold the crucible. I drink at night, and at night, after the second shift, it's already somebody else's morning. The bottle, for me, isn't a vice: it's hygiene. It's for sleeping. Sleep, at the foundry, you don't buy: you pour it.

What's left, at the end of the month, is two hundred euros, and the two hundred euros, for ten years, stayed two hundred euros: the money of an escape that didn't happen. I've been on a temp contract for six years: the payslip comes from another firm, and the other firm doesn't know who I am, it knows what I produce. I produce the way a tool produces: well, without complaining, and without expecting anything back. The line boss tells me *Ettore, you're reliable*, and reliability, at the foundry, is the virtue of tools: you have it, and you don't choose it.

And INAIL, in 2017, wrote the thing it writes: *no causal link*. The link, at the foundry, you breathe it. But a report is a report: you sign it, you file it, and the archive, like the dust, doesn't shout: it settles.

Now I know this story by heart, the way stories have their habit: you learn them after. And the thing I learned last is the thing that was there from the start, and that I didn't look at, because not looking is the trade you learn first: the foundry that killed my father pays my wages.

The overalls are still in the wardrobe. The bottle too. The bottles, in the long run, I've started throwing away, but that's another story: and the other stories, in my house, begin in September. In front of a field I didn't know.
