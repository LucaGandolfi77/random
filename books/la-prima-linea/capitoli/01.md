## Draft

> "O nature, O nature, / why do you not deliver / what you promise in that hour?"
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *A Silvia* (translation)

The September shift started at two, and at two, in September, the heat was already inside: you carried it from home, the way you carry a coat in December. I came into the department with the bottle full of water and the overalls open, and open overalls, at the foundry, are overalls that don't work: you close them at the first splash. The furnace stood at the end of the department, and the furnace, from far away, you don't hear: you see it. You see it in the reflection on the ceiling, and the reflection is orange, and orange, in the department, is the only thing that moves by itself: everything else moves because you move it.

You go to the pour in pairs, and in pairs you stand the way the things done in pairs have their habit: without speaking. The crucible tips, the white comes out, and the white is the only thing at the foundry that isn't tired. Watch the white for ten years, the white of the pour: it stays in your eyes, and then you find it in places where it doesn't belong: in the January fog, in the milk of the coffee, in the eyes of a woman who looks at you and says nothing. The white, at the foundry, is a trade: you learn it, and you don't unlearn it.

The sound of the pour isn't a sound: it's a weight. The metal coming out makes a low noise you don't hear with your ears: your ribs hear it. After ten years my body knows the metal's shift better than I know the calendar's: at two it's hungry, at four it's thirsty, at ten it's sleepy. And the sleep, at ten, I don't give it: I give it at one, and the distance between ten and one is the distance of the bottle, and the distance of the bottle, in Sesto, is always the same: from the department to the bar, from the bar to home.

Next to me at the department there was Gino. Sixty-one years old, thirty-two of foundry, the cough that doesn't leave him even when he sleeps — the whole line hears it. Gino has a theory of the shift, which he repeats every evening with the seriousness of a man who had thirty-two years to think about it:

"The shift doesn't pass," he said. "The shift melts. And once it's melted you don't go back: you pour."

He had a theory of coffee too, which said it was the only real break because it lasts three minutes, and a theory of holidays, which said they were the prize the foundry gives to the ones who stay. He didn't have a theory of retirement. He said he couldn't afford to think about that one any more, and he laughed, and the laugh ended in the cough, and the cough took it back. On his bench there was a photo of his grandchildren and a small bottle of oil, and the oil, at the department, nobody knew what it was for: the line had wondered, and nobody had asked. The useless things, at the foundry, you don't ask: you keep.

On the other side of the line there was Toni, the delegate. Toni has a broken voice and the stubbornness of a man who has signed thirty years of requests, and the requests, at the foundry, are the things you write and don't read: you file them. Every so often Toni stopped the line with a sentence:

"The company put up the detectors," he said. "The detectors say what the company needs to hear."

And the company, in the end, sent a communiqué, and the communiqué said *values within the limits*, and limits are limits: they're written, and the written things, at the foundry, are the things you don't touch. The dust doesn't unionize. The dust gets breathed. It gets breathed slowly, the way it has its habit: it doesn't shout, it doesn't ask, it settles. And it settles where it can, and where it could, in my family, was the lung.

I've been on a temp contract for six years. The payslip comes from another firm, and the other firm doesn't know who I am: it knows what I produce. I produce the way a tool produces: well, without complaining, and without expecting anything. The line boss tells me *Ettore, you're reliable*, and reliability, at the foundry, is the virtue of tools: you have it, and you don't choose it.

On Tuesday evening Vera called.

Vera has been my woman for two years, and two years, in my life, are the years in which I had a person who called me on Tuesdays. Vera is a schoolteacher at the De Cristoforis school, and Vera's school lets out at four, and at four she's free, and Vera's freedom is the freedom of a woman who knows what to do with it: the school, the shopping, the post, the phone call to me.

"Hi," she said. "Sunday, lunch at mine? I'm making risotto."

"I have the shift."

"You have the shift on Sunday too?"

"We're stacking the sacks."

"Ettore."

"What."

"Nothing."

And she added nothing, and I didn't ask. The sentence that adds nothing is the sentence that says everything, and the sentence that says everything, on the phone with Vera, I heard it without hearing it: the way the things that aren't said have their habit — you don't hear them, and they weigh. Me hanging up, in the kitchen, with the bottle already open, and the bottle open at nine at night is the bottle you don't justify: you drink it.

I didn't go on Sunday. Not because I had the shift: the Sunday shift was a lie, I said it and I knew it. I didn't go because on Saturday night I had drunk until three, and three, on a Sunday morning, is the nine of a man with a lunch that isn't coming. Asleep until noon, phone off, the house making its usual noise. When I turned the phone on there was Vera's message, arrived at eleven: *that's fine*. Two words. The two words, with Vera, are the two words that scare the most: they don't shout, they don't ask, they close.

I never made her pay for the missed lunch, and she never made me pay for it: the missed meals, in couples that are wearing out, are the things you don't make each other pay for: you add them up. And the things that add up, in the long run, are the account, and the account, sooner or later, presents itself. The way accounts have their habit.

On Tuesday the shift was the usual shift: the furnace, the white, the sacks, Gino's cough at the end. At ten I went out, and at the bar on the corner there was Sandro, and Sandro knows my wine and my hours, and my hours are his hours: for ten years the bar on the corner has closed at half past ten, and at half past ten I'm the last one, and the last one, at the bar, is the last one. I don't call myself an alcoholic. The alcoholic is the one who drinks in the morning. I drink at night, and I drink my wine in small glasses, and the small glasses are the only measure I've given myself: two, and the two, in the long run, are four, and the four are the two of another bottle. The mathematics of the bottle is a mathematics you learn alone, and alone, in the long run, you teach it.

At home, the bookcase had four shelves, and the four shelves had the books from school, my mother's books, an atlas come off its spine and three novels I hadn't opened. On the nightstand there was the INAIL report, folded in four, and the fold was the fold of the day I read it, and I read it once: *no causal link*. The link, at the foundry, you breathe it. But a report is a report: you fold it, you put it away, and the drawer is where reports sleep: they sleep, that's all.

On Thursday evening I took the long way back from the bar, and the long way passes in front of the Amatori Nord Bassa field. The field has been in front of me for ten years, and in ten years I had never gone in: you pass, that's all. That Thursday there was a training, and through the wire you could see the bodies: forty or so, in shirts, and among the forty a thing I didn't know — eight men bound together, shoulder to shoulder, back against back, pushing.

The push, from outside, you feel. It isn't a noise: it's a weight, like the metal, but it's a weight of people. Eight men pushing together make a sound the foundry doesn't make: the foundry makes the sound of metal, and the sound of metal asks nothing of anyone. Those made the sound of people: you could hear that they pushed for each other, and that the others pushed for them, and that nobody, in the middle, could let go. If one lets go, all fall. I felt it from outside, and the thing you feel from outside, sometimes, is the thing you can't say: I stopped at the wire for ten minutes, and in ten minutes I didn't understand the rules. I understood one thing only: that they were forty, and that forty pushing together were a thing that had never been in my life.

The broad shoulders I have from my father, and broad shoulders, at the foundry, are a defect: they take more load. From outside the wire, that Thursday in September, for the first time, they looked like a virtue.

I went back the next day. And the next day, the next day.
