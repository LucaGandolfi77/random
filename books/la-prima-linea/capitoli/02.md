## Draft

> "…and shipwreck is sweet to me in this sea."
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *L'infinito* (translation)

The next evening I went back, and the next evening there was the training, and the training was at nine, because until a quarter to nine the field belonged to the kids. I arrived straight from the shift, with the foundry under my jacket, and the foundry under my jacket is a smell that doesn't ask permission: it arrives, and it stays. In the changing room there were forty men, and the forty men were forty smells: the grass, the muscle spray, the sweat of the ones who had come from work like me. Nobody asked my name. They asked my position.

"Front row?" said the one with the whistle.

"Whatever you need."

"Prop. You look like a prop."

The one with the whistle was Bepi Cestonaro, and Bepi is the coach, and Bepi is fifty-eight, with a belly that is a curriculum and a bicycle he rides to every training, rain included, and he had been a prop for twenty years, and the props, he says, are the men who play so that the others can play. He looked at my shoulders the way you look at a tool:

"You've carried something in your life."

"Aluminium."

"Aluminium will do."

Then I asked him the thing I had decided to ask on the way, and the thing was the shift. The morning shift, at the foundry, is the shift of the lucky ones: it starts at six, it ends at two, and the evening is yours. Bepi looked at me and didn't understand, and I didn't explain, because explaining, at thirty-one, was a thing I didn't know how to do.

"Not me," he said. "The foundry."

"The foundry," I said.

The line boss, on Wednesday, said from January. And January, in October, is far: you wait. In the meantime the training was the second shift, and the second shift of rugby was the shift that didn't pay: it paid in a thing I didn't know how to name. The bar on the corner lost its Thursday, and the loss, for Sandro, was one glass: he didn't say anything. The barkeeps, in the long run, don't say anything: they know.

The warm-up was a run around the field, and the run was the first thing that hurt, because ten years of second shift teach the shoulders and not the lungs. Around me the forty ran, and the forty were workers, students, a dentist, two young Tunisians who spoke between them a language that wasn't mine, and nobody explained anything, because in the third division nobody explains: they show.

Then they made the scrum. Bepi put me in the front row, head against the shoulder of a man I didn't know, and the man's shoulder was a shoulder like mine: broad, consumed, twenty years older. Eight men, bent, bound, and the eight were one body, and the body had no name. In the middle there was the ball, and around the ball there was the law: you push together, or you don't push.

"Crouch," said Bepi. "Touch. Set."

And we set, and the ground pushed back, and the push of the ground was the push I knew: the weight of the metal, in the ribs. But in front of the weight there were seven men pushing with me, and the seven were pushing the way you push at the pour: without speaking, all the way, and the push that is all the way, in the middle, is a thing that holds you up. If one lets go, all fall. Nobody let go. The scrum ended, and the scrum ended the way the true things end: you get up, and you don't understand what happened, and you want it again.

Next to me, in the second row, there was Giorgi. Giorgi Kapanadze, thirty years old, Georgian, a head taller than me, with the hands of a man who has done everything and the Italian of a man who says few words, all nails. Giorgi fixed my binding without asking, and the binding was wrong, and the wrong binding, in the scrum, is the thing that breaks the neck:

"Arm inside," he said. "The arm inside. The scrum is not strength. The scrum is agreement."

He said it like a sentence he had said before, in another language, to other men, and the sentence, in his mouth, was the sentence of his country. He told me, while we waited for the next scrum, that in Georgia they had pushed like this forever, that the Caucasus was a place of mountains and tables, and that when two villages fought, afterwards, they ate. I didn't understand the eating. I understood it after.

Then the tackle. Bepi put the bags down and said the thing he says: the tackle is the only moment in which you give your body to a man you don't know, and the man gives you his, and the giving, in rugby, is the only giving that is regulated: you give, and you get up, and the other gets up. I looked for the hit the way you look for a punishment: I found it. I took the bag the way you take a bottle: all the way, and without breathing. Bepi stopped me after the fourth, and he said the sentence I didn't expect:

"You tackle like a man who owes something."

And I didn't answer, and the not answering was the answer, and Bepi knew it, and Bepi, in the long run, was the first man who knew it without asking.

"Rugby," he said, "is a debt you pay together. Not alone."

On Sunday there was the first match, a friendly against a team from Brianza, and Bepi put me on for the last twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes were the twenty minutes in which I understood the thing I had felt from outside the wire: that the body, when it has a reason, doesn't ask for the bottle. We lost 38 to 12, and the loss, in the third division, is a loss you make together: nobody carries it alone. In the changing room nobody spoke. In the clubhouse, after, everybody spoke.

The third half was a long table, and the long table had the two teams on the same side, and the pasta of the club's ladies, and the water, and the bread. The men of Brianza, who had tackled us for eighty minutes, passed the bread to the men of Sesto, and the bread, at the third half, is the law: you pass it, you don't ask. We toasted with water, because in the third division you toast with water: the wine costs, and Wednesday is near. The captain of Brianza said the thing they say at the third halves, and the thing they say is always the same and is always new: to the ones who fell.

Bepi, at the end, said his, and his was the sentence I carry:

"First you push, then you eat. A man who doesn't eat with the ones he tackled hasn't understood anything."

I had spent ten years working next to men I never ate with. At the foundry the second shift eats at the bench, alone, and the bench, at the foundry, is the bench of the ones who eat alone: you eat, and you don't taste. At the long table of the third half I ate with thirty men, and the thirty were the men I had pushed with and the men I had lost with, and the eating, for the first time in ten years, had a taste. It seemed to me, that evening, that very little was missing before something began (Chekhov). The thing that was missing, I didn't know. I know it now.

I went home late, and at home, for the first time in eight years, I didn't drink. Not for virtue: for fullness. The bottle is for the empty, and the empty, that evening, wasn't there. The night was long, and the long night, that Sunday, didn't ask me for the bottle: it asked me for nothing. It just stayed.
