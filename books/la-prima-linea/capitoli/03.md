## Chapter objective

- **What must happen**: **first turning point.** Vera leaves. The last Italian anchor breaks; Ettore sinks back into the bottle and makes the club the only home.
- **What must emerge of the character**: Vera doesn't leave him for the rugby: she leaves him for the bottle. Ettore understands that staying in Sesto is drinking until the end. The shame becomes stronger than the fear.

## Draft

> "Everything waits for the Sunday; the waiting for the usual pleasure is itself a pleasure."
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *Il sabato del villaggio* (translation, to verify against edition)

The Thursday after the friendly, Vera called, and the call was the call of the things that have to be said in person. She made the risotto again, and the risotto, that night, was the risotto of the things you make when you have something to say: too much butter, and the butter doesn't hide.

Her flat was a third floor in via Roma, and the third floor of Vera was the third floor of the things that work: the plants that grow, the books that stand, the table set for two. The table set for two, that night, was the table of a question, and the question, before the risotto, came out the way the questions come out when they've waited: without a preamble.

"Are you drinking every day?"

And the question was the question I asked myself every morning, and every morning I answered it the way I answered it to her: with the thing that isn't a lie and isn't the truth.

"I drink like everyone. A glass at dinner."

"Like everyone," she said. "And the bottle on your kitchen table, at nine at night, is dinner?"

"The bottle is for sleeping."

"Ettore. My father slept too."

Then the story came out, and the story was the story I had never asked, because in our houses the heavy things don't get asked. Vera's father had worked the presses in Cinisello, and the presses, he said, were the only thing that made him tired enough to sleep. He didn't drink in the morning. At night he drank, and he called it hygiene. The bottle was for sleeping. He died at fifty-one, and at fifty-one my mother was forty-nine, and my mother had spent twenty years being the one who waits.

"I don't want to spend twenty years being the one who waits," said Vera. "I'm telling you because you're the only man I know who calls the bottle hygiene."

And the sentence was the sentence that costs, and the cost, that night, was paid the way it's paid: nobody raised the voice. We ate the risotto. The butter didn't hide. We did the dishes, and the doing of the dishes, between two people who have said the thing, is the doing of the dishes: you wash, you dry, and nobody says the thing again.

She came to take her things the following Sunday. The things were a mug, two books, the toothbrush and the spare key, and the taking of the things was done the way Vera did everything: with care, and without noise. The mug was on the shelf, and the mug, in the shelf, had left its ring, and the ring of the mug stayed: the rings of the things that leave, stay. The two books she took. The toothbrush went in the bin, because the toothbrush, in a bathroom that isn't yours any more, is a thing that doesn't come back. The key she left on the table, and the key, on the table, was the key of the things that are finished: it doesn't open anything any more, and it weighs.

"Take care, Ettore."

"Take care."

And the door closed the way the doors close when there's nothing to decide: quietly, and for good. I sat in the kitchen with the key on the table and the ring on the shelf, and the flat, for the first time in two years, was the flat of one person. The flat of one person, at thirty-one, is a flat you know by heart: it was my flat from before, and I had spent two years forgetting it.

That night I drank the bottle, and the bottle, that night, was two, and the two were four, and the four were the two of the second. Then I opened the wardrobe, and I took the overalls, and I put them on, and the overalls fit me the way they had fit my father: they fit. I sat in the kitchen with the overalls on and the glasses on the table, and the overalls, on me, were the overalls of a man who was fifty-four and had died of the lung, and I was thirty-one, and the lung, in the overalls, was my lung. I drank to the father. I drank to the lung. I drank the way you drink when the shame is bigger than the fear: without counting, and without sleeping.

The sleep came at five, and the five, that morning, was the morning of a man with a shift, and the shift was the shift, and the shift didn't ask anything. The shift never asks anything. That's why it's a shift.

The trainings became daily. Tuesday and Thursday there was the training, and Monday, Wednesday, Friday there was the field with nobody on it, and the field with nobody on it was the field I ran on alone, and the running, alone, at thirty-one, in November, was the running of a man who has something to outrun. Saturday there was the match or the gym. Sunday there was the match. The week of Ettore, in November, had a shape, and the shape was the shape the bottle had left: you run from it, and the running is the shape.

The club didn't ask. The club was the only place where nobody asked me anything: not how I was, not how it ended, not why I ran. And the not asking, at thirty-one, was the closest thing I had to mercy. Bepi saw the running, and the running of every day, and Bepi said one thing, and the thing was the thing of a man who has seen thirty years of men:

"Whatever you're running from," he said, "the field doesn't outrun it. The field only gives you somewhere to be while you don't."

And the sentence was the true sentence, and the true sentences, at thirty-one, are the sentences you don't want: you take them, and you keep them.

I stayed in Sesto. The December came, and the December was the December of the things you decide: the days are short, the evenings are long, and the long evenings are the evenings in which you count. I counted, and the counting was the disease (Dostoevsky): the bottles of November, the glasses of the years, the nights of the father, the twenty years of Vera's mother waiting. The arithmetic of the bottle is an arithmetic that goes backwards: it starts from the end, and the end, at fifty-one, had a face I had seen at the dinner, and the face was Vera's, and the face of Vera was the face of her mother, and the mother of Vera had waited twenty years, and I was going to be the one who is waited for.

In the changing room, Giorgi's locker had a postcard pinned inside, and the postcard was mountains: the mountains of Georgia, blue on blue, and the blue was the blue of the overalls in my wardrobe. I had seen it every Thursday for two months, and every Thursday I had seen it without seeing it. That night I saw it.

And the thing I saw, I didn't know the name of. I knew the name of it after.
