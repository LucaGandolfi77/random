## Draft

Giorgi took the postcard out of the locker the night I saw it, and the taking was the taking of a man who has been waiting for the seeing.

"Kazbegi," he said. "In Georgia. But the mountains, Ettore, are not Georgia. The mountains are everywhere you haven't been."

Then he told me the thing he had never told me, and the thing was the thing of a man who says few words, all nails: Giorgi had played two seasons for the Kasbek, in Makhachkala, before the money of Italian rugby came for him. Two seasons of earth, and the earth, in Makhachkala, was the earth of the training at six in the morning, because in Makhachkala the training is at six: the sun, the Caspian, and the men who train at six are the men who have somewhere to be.

"The coach," said Giorgi. "Murat Ibragimov. Fifty years old. He was a prop. He doesn't speak Italian, and he doesn't speak English, and he doesn't need to: the scrum is a language, and the language is the same everywhere."

Then the photos, and the photos were the photos of the training at six: forty men on a dirt field, the sea behind, the mountains behind the sea, and in the middle of the forty a man with a whistle who stood the way my father stood: straight, hands in the pockets, looking and not saying. I looked at the man with the whistle, and the looking, that night, was the looking of a man who has found something: I didn't know what I had found. I knew it after.

"We need a prop," said Giorgi. "The front row. The man who was there went back to the village. And the club doesn't pay. You train, you work, you live with a family. Murat finds the work. Murat finds the house."

"Why me?"

"Because you're a prop," said Giorgi. "And because you push like a man who owes something, and in the Caucasus everybody pushes like a man who owes something."

The call happened at Giorgi's, on the speakerphone, a Sunday, and the call was the call in two languages: Giorgi spoke Russian, and Russian, from him, was a language I heard for the first time from the inside — the sounds didn't ask me anything, they went where they went. Murat's voice was a low voice, and the low voice, on the phone, was the voice of the metal: you felt it in the ribs. Giorgi translated the parts that needed translating, and the parts that needed translating were the conditions:

"You train with us. You work. You don't drink here — here you don't drink. You respect the house. The house gives you the bread, and the bread is a debt: you don't refuse it, and you don't forget it."

And I said yes, and the yes, at thirty-one, was the first yes of my life that didn't have a but.

The letter arrived on Thursday, and the letter was the letter of invitation, printed, stamped, with the club's name at the top and my name in the middle, and the name was written the way names are written by somebody who wants you to come: right. I read the letter at the bar on the corner, and the letter, at the bar, was the letter of the things that are real: the visa, the dates, the address. Sandro looked at the letter and didn't ask, and the not asking of Sandro was the not asking of ten years: he knew.

Before the letter I had done the thing I had to do, and the thing was the file. I went to the INAIL, and at the INAIL there was a queue, and the queue was the queue of the things that are owed: I waited two hours, and the two hours were the two hours of the man before me, who was there for the shoulder. The clerk gave me the file of my father, and the file was a folder of forty pages, and the forty pages had the numbers: the dust measurements, year by year, the years my father was on the pour line. 1994: 4.2. 1999: 5.6. 2003: 5.1. The numbers didn't say anything. Numbers don't say anything: they stay. The report, at the end, said the thing it says: *no causal link*. The link, at the foundry, you breathe it. But the numbers were the numbers, and the numbers, in the folder, were the years of my father's lung, and the lung was the lung of the man who had died at fifty-four, and the fifty-four didn't argue either.

I put the folder in the drawer, and the drawer, for the first time in ten years, closed on something I had read. The reading didn't change the thing. The reading changed me, and the changing of me, that December, was the changing of a man who has stopped arguing with the numbers: you take them, and you go.

The farewell to the club happened the way the farewells happen at the third division: without a speech, and with the pasta. Bepi told the team at the training, and the team did the thing the teams do: they said nothing, and on Thursday they made the last third half, and the last third half was the long table with the pasta, and the long table had the bread, and the bread was the law: you pass it, you don't ask.

Before the pasta, Bepi made the scrum. One scrum, the whole team, and the scrum was the scrum of the farewells: eight men, and in the eight there was me, and the eight pushed the way they had pushed the first time, and the push was the push of the things you do together: nobody lets go. If one lets go, all fall. Nobody fell.

After the scrum Bepi gave me the shirt, and the shirt was the shirt of the club, with the number 3, and the number 3 is the number of the props: the men who play so that the others can play. Bepi said the law, and the law was the law of a man who has seen thirty years of fields:

"Wherever you go, there's a field. And the field is the same everywhere: you push, you get up, and after, you eat. Push first, Ettore. Then eat. And wherever you eat, remember the table."

Youssef and Karim, the two young Tunisians, didn't say anything, and the not saying, from them, was saying: they knew the trade of leaving. They knew it the way you know the trade of leaving: from inside. Karim shook my hand at the end, and the handshake was the handshake of the ones who stay: it says come back, and it doesn't.

The flat I closed on the twenty-seventh. The deposit stayed with the landlord, and the landlord, in the long run, keeps the deposits: it's his trade. The overalls stayed in the wardrobe, and the wardrobe closed, and the closing of the wardrobe, for the first time in ten years, was the closing on a decision: the decision of a man who doesn't carry his father's lung into a country he hasn't seen. I took the bottle, the empty one, the plastic one of my father's pocket, and the empty bottle, in my bag, was the only thing I carried that wasn't new: it was the thing of the man who had called the foundry beautiful at night. The books stayed. The report stayed. The key of Vera was still on the table, and the key stayed too: the keys of the things that are finished don't travel.

At the airport there was the fear, and the fear, at the gate, is the fear of the ones who leave: it asks if you're sure, and it doesn't ask twice. The flight had a stop in Istanbul, and the stop was four hours, and the four hours were the four hours of a man who sits with a bag and a letter and doesn't know a word of the language of the place he's going to. I had the bottle in the bag, the shirt in the bag, the letter in the bag, and the bag was the bag of a man who is thirty-one and has nothing else: and the nothing else, this time, was different from the nothing else of the funeral. The nothing else of the funeral was empty. This one was full of the things I had chosen, and the things you choose, in the long run, are the things that weigh the right way.

I boarded at eleven at night. The plane took off, and from the window, before the clouds, there was the Po valley: the lights, the factories, the field of the Amatori Nord Bassa that I couldn't see and knew where it was. And then the clouds, and the clouds, at night, were white, and the white was the white of the pour: the only thing that isn't tired.

The letter was in my pocket. It was the first thing in my life that had my name written by somebody who wanted me to come.
