## Draft

The plane landed at Uytash at four in the afternoon, and at four in the afternoon, in January, the light was already going: it went the way the light goes in a foundry, from the edges, and the centre stayed. The cold of Sesto is a cold that enters: it comes through the coat, the collar, the years. The cold of Makhachkala was a cold that waited: it stood outside the door of the plane and didn't come in until you opened, and when you opened, it took everything at once, politely.

The signs were in Cyrillic, and the Cyrillic, from the plane, was the scaffolding of a city: the letters were there, and the streets had no names yet. I had one word, and the word was the word I had learned on the flight from Giorgi's book: *spasibo*. Thank you. One word, at thirty-one, in a country where the letters didn't open: it was the word of the ones who arrive.

Ruslan was at the barrier, and the barrier of Uytash is the barrier of the airports of everywhere: the glass, the queue, the signs. I recognized him by the way he stood — straight, hands in the pockets, looking and not saying — and the standing, at the barrier, was the standing of my father, and the standing of Murat in the photos, and I understood, later, that in the Caucasus all the men stand that way: it's the standing of the ones who don't waste.

The car was a Niva the colour of dust, and the Niva, in the parking lot, had the dust of the roads of a whole country: you don't wash it, and the not washing, in a country of dust, is a form of respect. Ruslan didn't speak. He took the bag, and the taking of the bag was done the way you take a bag from a man who has travelled twenty hours: without asking if it's heavy. We drove, and the drive was the drive of the first days: the Caspian on one side, grey like the Po, and the mountains far, white on the tops, and the city in between — the buildings that were built and the buildings that weren't finished, and the unfinished, in Makhachkala, is not a defect: it's a state.

The house was behind a gate, and the gate was a gate of iron, and the iron, at the gate, opens for the ones who are expected: Ruslan opened it without getting off the car, and the opening without getting off was the opening of the house of a man who knows his gate. The courtyard was a courtyard of concrete, and the concrete had a wall of stone behind, and the wall of stone was the wall that had been there before everything: the house, the car, the country. In the courtyard there was a dog, and the dog didn't bark. The dogs of the houses of the Caucasus don't bark at the guests: the guests are expected.

Patimat was at the door. Patimat is the wife of Ruslan, and Patimat is forty-three, and Patimat had the look of the women who cook: the look that measures you at the door, and measures the bag, and measures the tiredness, and decides, in the measuring, what there will be for dinner. She didn't say anything. She took the bag with one hand, and the bag, in her hand, weighed nothing: the hands of Patimat were hands that carry.

The table was a low table, and the low table had the bread and the salt, and the bread and the salt, in the Caucasus, are the welcome: they are put in front of the guest, and the guest doesn't refuse them, and the guest doesn't pay for them, and the guest doesn't ask what they are. The bread was the bread of the house: dark, heavy, the bread of the ones who work. I broke it, and the breaking of the bread, that evening, was the breaking of the first bread that had been put in front of me by somebody who was not paid: it was put by somebody who had decided, and the decision, in the bread, is the thing you taste.

I put the money on the table after the dinner, and the money on the table was an offense. I didn't know it. The not knowing was written on Ruslan's face the way things are written: without a word. Ruslan took the money and put it back in my hand, and the putting back was done gently, and the gentle, that evening, was worse than the angry. Patimat took the bread away and brought it back with more. And the more, in the bread, was the answer: you don't pay in a house where you are expected. You work.

"You work," said Ruslan, and it was the first complete thing he said to me, and I understood it because the working is a language that doesn't translate. "The guest who doesn't work is a guest for a night. The guest who works is a guest for a winter."

Timur is the son of Ruslan, and Timur is twenty-two, and Timur had the look of the sons of the houses: the look that watches. At the table he asked the question the house didn't ask, and the asking, from the son, was the asking that matters: the house has the law of not asking; the son has the right.

"Why here?" he asked, in English, and the English, from him, was the English of the films: clean, fast.

"For the field," I said. "For the rugby."

"There is no rugby here," said Timur.

"There is," I said. "I've seen the photos."

Timur looked at me, and the looking, from Timur, was the looking of a man of twenty-two who has seen the foreigners come and go: they come for the mountains, they come for the soul, they take the photos, and they go. And the looking said: you too. And the not saying, from Timur, was the saying: prove it.

The teacher was a woman that Ruslan knew, and the knowing of Ruslan was the knowing of the ones who know the city: she lived in the building behind the mosque, and she was Russian, and she had stayed when the others went. Her name was Nina Petrovna, and Nina Petrovna was sixty-eight, and Nina Petrovna was the widow of a colonel who had been at the garrison, and the colonel was dead, and the woman stayed: and the staying, in a city that empties, is the staying of the ones who have nowhere else, or the staying of the ones who have decided. Nina Petrovna was the second kind. You saw it in the way she opened the door: like the gate, without getting off.

The flat was a flat of books, and the books went from the floor to the ceiling, and the ceiling, in the flat of Nina Petrovna, was the ceiling of a library: the books held it up. The samovar was on the table, and the lamp, and the lamp was the lamp of the lessons: it lit the page and not the room.

The alphabet, she said, is a city you enter by night: the letters are there, and the streets have no names yet. She taught me the letters the way you teach a child, and the teaching, from her, was done without mercy and without irony: a letter, the sound, the repetition. The repetition, in her kitchen, was the repetition of the prayers of somebody else's church: you say, and you don't understand, and you say again, and one day the saying becomes the understanding, and you don't see the day it happens.

At the end of the hour she took a book from the shelf — a thin book, a reader of a school, with the pages yellow — and she read me a page of Chekhov, aloud, and the reading was done with a voice that didn't give: a voice that has read aloud for forty years, to students, to sons, to an empty flat. I didn't understand the words. I understood the voice. And the voice, that evening, was the first thing of Makhachkala that was not a cold, or a wall, or a law: it was an offer.

"You Europeans come looking for our soul," she said, in Italian — she had the Italian of the Union, the Italian of the ones who studied it when it was useful. "The soul, my dear, is not a souvenir."

And she closed the book, and the closing of the book was the closing of the lesson: you come back Thursday.

The first training I did on the Monday, and the training was at six, and the six, in Makhachkala, is the six of the ones who have somewhere to be. Murat Ibragimov was at the field, and the field was a field of earth, and the earth, at six, was frozen at the edges and alive in the middle, and the middle, in the fields of the Caucasus, is where the men are. Murat looked at my shoulders and didn't say the thing Bepi said. He said the other thing, and the other thing was the law:

"Here you don't drink," he said, in Russian, and my one word was not enough, and I understood it anyway: some things don't need the language. "The body is already drunk enough with fatigue."

And I said the word I had: *spasibo*. Thank you. And the thank you, at the field of the Kasbek, at six in the morning, was the thank you of a man who had brought nothing to drink and had nothing to refuse: the point of the law is that you don't refuse. You arrive empty.

The nights were the nights of the silence, and the silence of the house of Ruslan is a silence that doesn't ask anything: it stays. The body asked for the glass, and the body asks at one, at two, at four, and the asking, at four, in the dark of a courtyard of the Caucasus, is the asking of a man who has arrived empty on purpose and discovers that empty is a country too. I walked the courtyard at four in the morning, and the ones who walk at night, in the books of Petersburg, are called dreamers (Dostoevsky). I was not a dreamer. I was a man whose body was asking, and the body, when it asks, doesn't lie: it was the first honest thing of the year, and the honest thing, at thirty-one, was mine: I had chosen it.

At six the training began.
