## Draft

> "…truth and beauty, which had guided human life there, in the garden and in the court of the high priest, continued unbroken to this day, and were evidently always the chief thing in human life, and on earth in general."
>
> — Anton Chekhov, "The Student" (translation, to verify)

The field of the Kasbek was a field of earth, and the earth, at six in the morning, in February, had the edges frozen and the middle alive, and the middle was where the men were. The men of the Kasbek were twenty-two, and the twenty-two were the twenty-two of the ones who train at six: Arsen, Ismail, Khalid, Gamid, Apti, and the others, and the names, in the beginning, were the names of a language I didn't have: I learned them the way you learn the streets of a city by night, one at a time, and the learning of the names, at the Kasbek, was the learning of the men: a name a week.

Suleyman Gadzhiev was the captain, and Suleyman is thirty-four, and Suleyman drives a taxi, and the taxi, in Makhachkala, is the taxi of the ones who work: he trained at six and drove from eight, and the driving, from eight, was the driving of a man who has a family and a field and doesn't sleep more than the field asks. Suleyman didn't speak. He said the things that need saying: the calls of the training, the names of the moves, the one word he said to me the first week, and the word was the word of the captains:

"You push," he said, pointing at the front row, "for the eight. Not for you."

And the sentence was the sentence I had heard at the foundry, said by nobody: at the foundry you push for the ones who pay. At the Kasbek you push for the eight. The difference, in the push, is the difference of the two lives: one is a job, and one is a thing you choose, and the thing you choose weighs less even when it weighs more.

Ali Kurbanov was the talent, and Ali is nineteen, and Ali had the speed of the nineteen and the hunger of the ones who want out: he wanted France, he said, the rugby of France, the money of France, and the wanting, in Ali, was the wanting I had had in reverse: he was inside and wanted out, and I was outside and had wanted in. We didn't speak. We didn't need to: the mirror doesn't need the language.

The training was the training of the Caucasus, and the training of the Caucasus is the training of the ones who don't have the machines: you push, you tackle, you run on the earth, and the earth, at six, gives back what it takes: it's hard, and the hardness, in the legs, is the hardness of a country that doesn't lie. Murat fixed my binding by hand, and the fixing by hand was done without words, the way you fix the things of the ones who don't speak: he took my arm, he put it inside, he pressed, and the pressing was the pressing of the man who knows where the arm goes. The hands of Murat were the hands of the coaches of the ones who don't pay: they work.

The work was at the bazaar, and the bazaar Murat found, and the bazaar was the bazaar of the things that are immediate: the fruits of the season, the nuts, the dried fruits, the prices that are shouted and the prices that are whispered. I worked for Gadzhi, and Gadzhi is sixty, and Gadzhi has the stall of the walnuts and the dried apricots, and the stall of Gadzhi was the stall of a man who has been at the bazaar for forty years: it had the weight of the things that stay. I loaded the crates from eight to two, and the loading was the loading of the foundry, said in another language: the sack, the shoulder, the back. The hands knew it. The hands, at the bazaar, were the hands of my father, and the father, at the bazaar, was not dead: he was the loading.

Gadzhi paid me eight hundred rubles a day, in cash, and the cash, at the bazaar, is the cash of the things that don't wait: it goes to the bread, and the bread goes to the house, and the house is the house of Ruslan, and the house doesn't take the cash: the house takes the work. The cash, at the end of the day, stayed in my pocket, and the pocket, at the end of the day, was the pocket of a man who has a day with a shape: the training at six, the bazaar at eight, the table at seven in the evening. The shape was the only thing I had carried with me from the foundry, and the shape, in Makhachkala, was the shape of a life: it was the first time the shape was mine.

On Sunday there was the friendly against Kaspiysk, at home, and the friendly was the friendly of a country that doesn't have a third division: it had the earth, the cold, the two teams, and the eighty minutes. I played the first half, and the first half was the first half of my life in another language: the calls were calls I didn't understand, and the not understanding, in the scrum, doesn't matter — the scrum is a language, and the language is the push. We lost 21 to 9, and the loss was the loss of the ones who lose together: nobody carried it alone.

The third half was the third half of the Caucasus, and the third half of the Caucasus is the khinkal: the long table of the café of the market, and the long table had the two teams on the same side, and the khinkal, and the broth, and the bread, and the tea. The men of Kaspiysk, who had tackled us for eighty minutes, passed the bread to the men of Makhachkala, and the bread was the law: you pass it, you don't ask. The toasts were toasts with tea, and the toasts with tea are the toasts of the house: they say the same things as the toasts with wine, and they say them clear like the morning. The captain of Kaspiysk raised the glass of tea and said the thing they say at the third halves, and the thing is always the same and is always new: to the ones who fell. And the falling, in the Caucasus, is not a shame: it's the half of the work.

I ate the khinkal with thirty men, and the eating was the eating of the third half of Sesto, said in another language: the same table, the same law, the same bread. The table, in the Caucasus, was the table of Bepi: you push, you get up, and after, you eat. And wherever you eat, remember the table. I remembered it. The remembering was the remembering of a man who is carrying a law in a language he doesn't have: the law weighs nothing, and it holds.

The injury was Ismail's, and the injury happened in the training of the Tuesday, and the training of the Tuesday was the training of the things that happen: a tackle that goes wrong, the ankle that doesn't go with the rest, the cry that the men of the Caucasus don't let out — the cry, from Ismail, was one, and it was short, and it was enough. I carried him off the field, and the carrying was the carrying of the ones who are near: you take the arm, you take the weight, and the weight, in the carrying, is the weight of a man who is twenty-six and has a family that will ask. The carrying didn't have a language. The carrying was the language.

The hospital was the hospital of Makhachkala, and the hospital of Makhachkala is the hospital of the cities that work: the corridor, the queue, the neon, the forms. I waited with Ismail three hours, and the three hours were the three hours of the queues of the things that are owed: the hospitals, in every country, are the queues of the bodies. The corridor had a bench, and the bench had the alphabet: I had the reader of Nina Petrovna in my pocket, and I studied the letters the way you study the streets: one at a time, without understanding, and the studying, in a corridor of a hospital, at thirty-one, was the studying of a man who has decided something.

She came at the fourth hour. She had the folder of Ismail in her hand and the look of the doctors who work: the look that reads the body before the paper. She looked at the ankle, and she touched it, and the touching was done without asking, and the not asking, from a doctor, is the not asking of the ones who know: the body doesn't answer questions.

"It's broken," she said, in English, and the English was the English of the films of Timur: clean, fast. "Not dislocated. The dislocation is what your coach said."

"Murat is a coach," I said.

"Murat is a coach," she said. "I'm a doctor. It's broken."

Her name was Zarema, and Zarema is twenty-seven, and Zarema had the frankness of the ones who say the things: it's her luxury, and the luxury, in a woman of the Caucasus, is a thing that costs. She saw my hands while I filled the forms, and the seeing of the hands was the seeing of a doctor: the hands of the foundry have the burns and the calluses of the foundry, and the calluses don't lie. She looked at the hands, and she looked at the reader in my pocket, and the looking, from her, was the looking of the first person who had read me in a year.

"You are not a rugbist," she said. "You are a worker."

"Both," I said.

"And the alphabet?"

"For the language."

"You came here to learn?" she said, and the question was the question of Timur, said in another way, and the answer was the same: yes. She looked at me, and the looking lasted a second more than the looking of the doctors, and the second more was the second of the things that begin.

At the end of the shift she took a book from the bag of the corridor — the bag of the things that doctors carry — and the book was a thin volume, a Chekhov, the stories, with the pages that had been read by somebody before me: the pencil, in the pages, was the pencil of the ones who read with a pencil. She put the book in my hands, and the putting was done the way Bepi put the shirt: as a thing that is given, and the given, in the Caucasus, is a debt.

"This is for after the alphabet," she said. "Chekhov. The stories are short. The alphabet is long."

And she went back into the corridor, and the corridor, behind her, was the corridor of the things that continue: the queue, the neon, the bodies.

That evening, in the courtyard, under the lamp of the wall, I read the first lines. I didn't understand them. I understood one thing, and the thing was the thing the book did: it didn't ask me anything. The pages were the pages of a man who had written them for the ones who read, and the reading, at thirty-one, in a courtyard of the Caucasus, with a language of one word and a book of three hundred pages, was the reading of a man who is not only a body: the body was the thing that had carried me there, and the body was tired, and the thing that wasn't tired was the thing in my hands.

The letters were the letters of a city by night. And the city, that evening, had one lit street, and the street was the street of Chekhov, and the street was mine to walk.
