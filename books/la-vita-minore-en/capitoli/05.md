# Chapter 5 — Lucia

**POV:** Teo · **Place:** Turin (the fourth floor, my mother's house) · **Story time:** November–December 2023
**Words:** ~1.300 / translation of `la-vita-minore/capitoli/05.md` ✅

---

## Draft

Nora came to know my mother on an afternoon in November, at four, outside the visiting hours, because at that hour the fourth floor was Nora's and the rules were hers.

My mother watched her come in and did the thing she did with people: she looked her in the face, from the hands to the hair, the way you look at fabrics. Fabrics are looked at from the reverse, but people are looked at from the front, and my mother knew both things.

— You're the one of the coffee — she said.

— I'm the one of almost everything, in here — Nora said. — But the coffee, yes. —

My mother laughed softly, with the laugh she had learned in the ateliers, in a low voice, as is the habit of those who work in rooms where little is said, and made Nora a place next to the bed.

She had brought up two cups, one in each hand, as was her habit. My mother took hers with two hands, and the hands of those who keep things are always visible: short nails, the hump of the fabric, the wrist that doesn't shake. They talked about the things that are talked about on fourth floors at four, which are the small things, and they were the true ones. Then my mother called me next to the bed and gave me the thimble.

She kept it in the bedside drawer, with the things that were needed. It was brass, worn, with the hump marked by forty years of needle pricks. — It's for you — she said. — You're a man who throws away too much, and the things that are kept must be kept. The thimble serves one thing only: not letting yourself be pricked by the things you keep. —

I put it in my pocket. Small things are kept in the pocket, when you're afraid of losing them.

— Now tell me yours — my mother then said to Nora, with the voice she used with new fabrics. — The small things. The true ones. —

And Nora told her: of the tomatoes from stall three, which lasted a whole week; of the machine that spits coffee and never gets the hour wrong; of the patients who kept their eyes closed and breathed slowly, as if the book were a medicine taken with the ears. She spoke softly, as is her habit, and my mother listened with her hands: she kept them on the blanket, still, and every so often they moved, the way the hands of those who brood move. For the first time in years I saw my mother keep a thing that wasn't sewn by her: she kept someone else's voice.

And the voice, as is its habit, wasn't lost.

The pathology came the way true things come: with its week, without knocking. It said the knot wasn't alone. My mother learned it from the doctor and told me from her, in the face, as was her habit:

— It has spread. Now we see. —

*Now we see*. It's the thing you say when seeing doesn't help anymore. I said it too, to myself, for weeks: *now we see*. Seeing didn't help. We both knew it, and we didn't say it: it was the only thing we had left not to say, and we kept it with care, as is the habit of those who have always kept things in the needle drawer.

It was in the last weeks that my mother stopped lying to herself.

The last weeks were the weeks of December, and December, in a hospital, is the month of the things that are counted: the shifts, the operations, the discharges. My mother was on the fourth floor, and the fourth floor was the floor of the things that are counted, and my mother was counted: the nurses counted her, the machines counted her, and the counting was the thing she didn't do. She did the other thing: she sewed. She sewed in the air, above the blanket, the way she sewed at the atelier, and the sewing was the thing she did for the last time: it was the thing she was finishing.

— I'm finishing the seams — she said, the last Sunday, with the voice of the sewing room. — The knots are out. The seams remain. —

And the seams, at fifty-nine, were the things she had sewn for forty years: the hems, the linings, the silences. And the silences, the last Sunday, she unsewed: she said the things she hadn't said. She said them to me, and to Nora, and to the room 412, and the saying was the thing she did for the last time: it was her last sewing, and it was made of words.

— Your father — she said, the last Sunday —, he underlined one thing only. You know it. I knew it, and I didn't say it. —

— I know — I said.

— I kept it — she said. — Eleven years. And the things that are kept, Teo, they're not kept for ever: they're kept until you can say them. —

And the saying, at fifty-nine, was her last sewing, and it was the truest: it was made of the things that are kept, and it was finished in a week, and the week, in December, was the week of the things that aren't counted.

My mother stopped lying to herself the last Sunday, and the stopping was the thing I saw: it was in the hands, and the hands, on the blanket, were still. She wasn't frightened. She was the one who had put away the fear — folded, put in the needle drawer — and the drawer, the last Sunday, she opened: she took out the things, and she said them.

And the things, said, were the things that stay: they stay, and the staying, at thirty-three, is the thing I learned. My mother said them, and the saying was her last sewing, and the sewing was the truest thing of the room 412: it was made of words, and the words, said, were the things that stay.

Nora was there, the last Sunday, and Nora was the one of the coffee: she had brought up two cups, one in each hand, and the cups were the things that are kept. My mother took hers with two hands, and the hands were still, and the staying of the hands was the thing I saw: it was the thing that didn't shake, and the thing that doesn't shake, in a hospital, is the only thing that holds.

My mother died on the Tuesday, at three in the morning, and the three in the morning, in a hospital, is the hour of the things that aren't counted: Nora was there, and Nora was the one of the things that aren't counted, and the things that aren't counted, at three in the morning, were the things that hold.
