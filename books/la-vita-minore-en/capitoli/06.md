# Chapter 6 — Everything Else Is Noise

**POV:** Teo · **Place:** Turin (my mother's house, the church, the fourth floor) · **Story time:** December 2023
**Words:** ~1.300 / translation of `la-vita-minore/capitoli/06.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "the only true paradise is the paradise we have lost."
>
> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

Telling the death to everyone else is a thing you don't learn. You do it, and it stays.

I said it to the relatives on the phone, one by one, with the voice I had learned from my mother — not the cheerful one, the soft one. The aunts said the things people say, and they said them with the voices you use at death feasts: the ones that don't shake, because shaking is for people who are close, and the aunts hadn't been close for years. One aunt — the one in Turin, the only one who ever called me — said: — Teo, if you need anything. — And she added nothing, and never called. It's the thing that's said and not done, as is their habit, the things said at death feasts: they aren't done, they're said, and that's it.

I said it to the colleagues of the seventh floor, and Ivo was the only one who didn't say the things people say: he put on my desk a coffee from the machine — long, lukewarm — and said:

— I won't tell you the things people say. I've left you the coffee. —

He had thirty years of headset and a theory for everything, except for death. For that he had only the coffee, and he left it without looking at me, as is the habit of kind gestures: you don't see them while they happen, you see them after.

Dario called me on Thursday, and for the first time he organised nothing.

— Come to dinner — he said. — But if you don't feel like it, you don't feel like it. —

I went, and the lasagna was there, and Chiara cried at the first bite, as is their habit, true things: other people's tears are the only thing you can give at dinner. Dario said nothing all evening, and it was the truest thing he could say. At the end of dinner, at the door, he held my shoulder for a second, as is their habit, heavy things: they aren't said, they're kept.

The funeral was on Thursday, in a neighbourhood church that smelled of wax and school desks. The seamstresses of the ateliers came, all of them, in a line, with hands that didn't know what to do — and it's the first time a seamstress's hands don't know what to do: you see it, and it hurts more than a cry. The relatives who hadn't been seen for years came, uniform, as is their habit, mourning: in a church all unhappy families are alike, and unhappiness is the only thing that makes uniform. The Ladies of the courtyard came, with flowers that weren't from Porta Palazzo and were fine anyway. And the woman of stall three came, with a basket of tomatoes nobody knew where to put, and she put it at the foot of the coffin, and it was the truest thing of the church.

The coffin was of dark wood. I thought, with the thought that mourning makes — which doesn't ask permission to be absurd — that a coffin is the only thing my mother couldn't redo. You don't take it out, you don't iron it again, you don't put it in the needle drawer. You keep it. It's the only thing that's really kept, in mourning: you keep it, and it's no use.

After a death everything else is noise. The phones that ring in bursts, the messages of the far relatives, the silent television in a house full of relatives, the sentences said when someone dies: *I'm sorry*, *I'm with you*, *I'll miss you*. Noise. Nobody says it: you feel it, the way you feel damp in walls. The only thing that wasn't noise was Nora, who didn't say the sentences. She stayed, as is her habit: the things that are kept aren't said, they're kept.

We emptied the house on corso Traiano in two Sundays, Nora and I.

The house, when it empties, smells more than when it was full: you open a wardrobe and the smell of fabric and hot iron took you back — to ten to five in the morning, to my father's thermal bag, to the Sunday dinners, to the whole childhood. I read later, in a book, that the lost paradise is recognised only afterwards, and that the after is made of smells. I already knew it. I was living it, smell by smell, drawer by drawer.

We opened the sewing room's wardrobe, where my father's thermal bag was, closed for fifteen years. The things of the dead are kept closed: they open when the other dies. Inside there was the coat of the shift, a clean and folded handkerchief, and the smell — the smell of coffee and work, which was the smell of his mornings, at ten to five. Nora held the bag open in front of me, without saying anything, and I held the smell for a whole minute, as is their habit, things: they're kept, and they don't give you back.

My mother's garments, which she had sewn for others, went to the others: in the good sacks, to the shops that redistribute them. It's the only thing a seamstress can ask, that the things made for others continue to dress the others. The rest we kept: the photos, the thermal bag, the needle drawer, the sewing machine.

The last thing was the iron. The iron was on the chair, and the iron, in a house that empties, is the thing that stays: the iron doesn't go. It was the thing my mother had used for forty years, and the iron, in the empty house, was the thing I kept: I kept it, and the keeping was the thing I did for the first time — the keeping of the things that don't go. Nora held the iron for a minute, the way you hold the things you don't know, and she said:

— It's still warm. —

It wasn't warm. It was cold. But Nora said *it's still warm*, and the saying was the thing I kept: the things that are kept, when they're said by someone who stays, are warm. It's their secret: they're kept, and the keeping warms them.

The house emptied in two Sundays, and the empty house, in January, was the house that smelled: it smelled of fabric, and of iron, and of the things that are kept. And the smell was the thing I kept: I kept it, and the keeping was the thing I did for the first time — the keeping of the paradise that is lost (Proust). The paradise, in the empty house, was recognised: it was recognised smell by smell, drawer by drawer, and the recognising was the thing I did for the first time — the recognising of the things that are kept.
