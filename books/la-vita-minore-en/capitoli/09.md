# Chapter 9 — Hay

**POV:** Teo · **Place:** Turin (my house, the little room) · **Story time:** April–May 2024
**Words:** ~1.150 / translation of `la-vita-minore/capitoli/09.md` ✅

---

## Draft

It was the spring in which I discovered hay.

Hay isn't hay: it's the thing Levin does, in the book Nora read aloud — the one where a man mowed with the peasants and for a whole day thought of nothing: the rhythm, the sweat, the back, and a happiness that doesn't know itself. Nora read an evening per chapter, the medicine at a fixed dose, and I listened from the sofa, and one of those chapters I understood that the work with the hands is the only thing that doesn't make fear. I had never done it: thirty-three years of headsets and other people's voices, and no thing done with the hands, except the coffee. Coffee is made with the hands, but it doesn't count: it goes cold and you throw it away — you don't ask coffee to stay.

She read the part of Levin, the one of the mowing: a man in a line with the peasants, who mowed and forgot himself — the horror, the death, the question. Hay answers nothing: it's cut, and that's it. It's the only thing that doesn't make questions, hay, and it's for this that hay doesn't make fear.

It was the spring in which I understood that Levin didn't mow for the hay: he mowed to not think. And I had nothing left to not think, except the things we were preparing — and the things that are prepared are made with the hands.

I started from the walls of the little room.

The little room wasn't there: there was a room that kept the things that aren't kept — Nora's moving boxes, my mother's iron, the sewing machine with the unfinished shirt under the needle. We emptied it, we painted it: an azure that Nora had chosen and that had no name, and it's fine, because true things have no name; names come after, and are lost last.

I assembled the cot with the instructions of a badly translated sheet, and for the first time in years I didn't shout even inside. Badly translated instructions are the only thing that doesn't make fear: they say you can get it wrong, and you can. True things say nothing. We bought the small things: the pram, the bottle, the white curtains. The pram opened by itself, as is their habit, new things: they open without anyone asking.

It was the spring in which I discovered that the work with the hands is the only thing that doesn't make fear: the wall, the cot, the back, the sweat. Fear, as is its habit, doesn't go there — it doesn't like sweat. Levin knew it, and the peasants knew it, and I discovered it thirty-three years late, in a little room that before wasn't there. I mowed my hay: the walls, the curtains, the pram that opened by itself. And happiness, in those weeks, didn't know itself. It's the only happiness that counts: the one that doesn't know itself. True happiness is seen only after, the way lost paradises are seen.

We made the lists.

The lists of the small things the big one needs: the pram, the bottle, the answers to the relatives' questions. And the name. The name we didn't know: we had two names on the lists, and we didn't know which — one was my father's and one was my mother's, and neither did we say out loud, as is its habit, family: heavy things aren't said, they're kept on lists.

The lists were the thing I did with the hands, and the hands, in the lists, were the hands of the things that are kept: the lists were kept, and the keeping was the thing I did for the first time — the keeping of the things that arrive. And the things that arrive, at thirty-three, were the things that don't make fear: the lists, the walls, the hay. And the not making fear, at thirty-three, was the thing I learned: the things that arrive, you keep, and the keeping is the thing that doesn't make fear — it's the thing Nora said, in the empty house, with the iron: *it's still warm*.

The false triumph, in May, was the thing I had: the walls painted, the cot assembled, the lists made. And the false triumph, at thirty-three, was the thing I believed: I believed the things that are prepared were enough. They're not enough. The things that are prepared are the things you do with the hands, and the hands, in May, were the hands of the things that are kept — and the keeping was the thing I did, and the doing was the thing that didn't make fear. But the fear, as is its habit, doesn't go away: it stays, and the staying is the thing I learned in May — the staying of the things that are kept, said to the man who mows: the hay doesn't answer, and the hay is the thing you mow, and the mowing is the thing that doesn't make fear. It's the only thing that doesn't make fear: the mowing. And the mowing, at thirty-three, was the thing I did: I mowed my hay.
