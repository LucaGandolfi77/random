# *The Smaller Life*

**Author:** [Name] · **Genre:** contemporary literary fiction · **Translated from Italian**

*To those who stay.*


# Prologue — After


I write this in April, and I write it because Nora told me to. Not with those words: Nora never says things with those words. She said: — Tell them. — And the *them* were the things I don't tell anyone, and I am telling them here, one at a time, the way you take things from a cart: one at a time, and you count them.

I am thirty-four years old, and I answer calls for a living. I answer them for a light and gas company, in Turin, on the seventh floor, with a headset on. The calls come from all over Piedmont, and they are the calls of people who shout, and I answer with the voice they use: softly, as is their habit, the answers of the lists — they say nothing, and they don't know it. The people on the phone believe they are speaking with the company. I believed I was Teo. We were both wrong.

In March my mother called me on a Sunday evening and said everything was fine. Everything wasn't fine. My mother had known for three months, and she hadn't said it, and the not saying was her way of sewing: the things, in our house, weren't said, they were sewn. And while my mother sewed, on the fourth floor of the hospital, there was Nora: the night nurse who reads Proust to the dying. Nora reads to them the way you give water to someone who is thirsty: without asking.

This is the story of the year in which everything arrived: the love that takes everything and the death that takes the rest. It is the story of my mother, who sewed; of Nora, who reads; of the small things — the right coffee, the laundry, the series one episode a day — which I believed were a consolation, and which were the life itself.

I write it in April, because in April you understand the things of March, and the things of February, and the things of always. And I write it here, in the room in Varigotti, with the sea that you can hear from the window and can't see: you hear it, and it's enough.

The books say the only true paradise is the paradise we have lost (Proust, *Time Regained*). My paradise had the smell of my mother's ironing, and the voice of the night nurse, and the sea that you can hear. I hadn't lost it. I hadn't seen it: the paradise we have lost, you recognise only afterwards, and the after is made of smells.

I begin at the beginning, which was a call, and a call is the thing you answer.

# Chapter 1 — The Factory of Voices


> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

The ten past seven tram was punctual like a sentence.

I went up to the seventh floor of Elnord with the badge that beeped and the headset around my neck, and at my desk there was always the same light: the light of the fluorescents, which says neither day nor night, and it's fine, because in factories time isn't needed: shifts are. On the screen, top right, my numbers: average handling time, calls resolved at first contact, unauthorised breaks. My numbers knew me better than I knew them. Every morning I looked at them with the feeling of looking at a test result.

The seventh floor was an open space of forty desks, and the forty desks were forty people with a headset around their neck who all spoke at the same time, softly, as is their habit, the factories of voices: you speak softly because shouting tires, and tiredness is the only thing you can't say to a customer. There was the usual noise: the thick voices, the keyboards, the hum of the computers, and every so often a laugh — one, stifled at once, as is their habit, laughs in factories: you can't, they last too long.

Next to me there was Ivo. Fifty-six, thirty years of headset, a smell of coffee and rolls of files. Ivo had a theory of coffee, which he repeated every morning with the seriousness of someone who has worked his whole life in one desk and has had the time to think about it:

— The ones from the machine — he said — are like us: long, lukewarm, and nobody really chose them. They happen to you. —

He also had a theory of the tram, a theory of his colleagues' clothes, and a theory of the sky, which he said was a badly made ceiling. He didn't have a theory of happiness. He said there was no material for it. On his desk there were the things people keep: a plastic cactus, a photo of a woman I didn't know, the coffee always half full.

— My woman — he said, once, without my asking.

And he added nothing else, and I didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked.

That morning there was the woman who shouted because the electricity had come back at double the price, and a man who spoke softly, with the voice of someone who has already called four times. The man was called Marchetti, he knew it because he gave it himself on the phone, and he had the voice of a man who never shouts, not even when he should.

— Good morning — he said. — I'm calling about a thing that happened to me four times. —

— Tell me, sir. —

— The bill. The bill says I use double the electricity, and I use half. I've used half since I've been alone. —

It was the truest thing anyone had said to me on the phone in three years, and I didn't know what to answer. My answers were on the screen, in a list: *meter reading check, meter check, technical assessment*. I read them all, one by one, with the voice you use: softly, as is their habit, the answers of lists — they say nothing, and they don't know it.

— You're right, sir — I said.

It was the first true thing I said on the phone in three years, and it wasn't on the list. I know it doesn't help: true things on the phone don't help, they're said and they stay. There was always something underneath, in the calls: the bill was the pretext, and underneath there was the fear of not making it. I felt it. It's the only thing I learned to do really well in my life: feel other people's fear on the phone. Nobody taught me: you feel it, the way you feel damp in walls. In Mr Marchetti there was the fear of the man who is alone and uses half the electricity and doesn't know why the bill says double. The bill said double because companies count everything, except people. I knew it. I didn't say it: on the phone true things don't help.

I said: *sir, I understand, it will be handled as soon as possible*. I was standing in for the company, with the voice. This is what my job was: being the voice of something that has no human voice. The people believed they were speaking with Elnord. I believed I was Teo. We were both wrong.

At noon I ate at my desk with a series on the phone, one episode a day, so it lasted the whole year. I didn't feel hunger either: I ate it for the same reasons I watched the series — to fill the hours, not to live them. The company had invented the small things for us and gave them to us in instalments: a series that lasts, an app that scrolls, a coffee that takes you. You can live on this, it seemed to me. You can live on this for years, and it seemed to me it was the deal: you ask nothing, and they leave you alone.

In the evening, instead, the small things happened. One of those evenings — it was Thursday, and on Thursdays the laundromat was empty — I did the washing at the laundromat on via Traiano, with the smell of detergent that smelled of order. I took the things from the cart to the machine, one by one, and I counted them: eleven. Eleven things for a whole life, and it wasn't little: it was everything I had. The bar downstairs after, with the right coffee drunk slowly, in front of the shop window where nothing happened — and nothing happened inside either, as is their habit, shop windows: nothing happens inside or outside, and you stay there anyway. The app, on the way home, on the sofa: I brushed past one face after another with my thumb, and one of those — one — answered me: *hi*.

I looked at the writing for a minute, the way you look at rare things. I didn't answer. I don't know why. Small things, when they come alive, make fear, and it's their secret: they're enough when they're dead, and when they're alive they take you.

I walked under the porticoes, which in Turin rain inside, and I thought about men read in books: in Petersburg, once, people walked alone at night, with nobody waiting for them, and they were called dreamers. We don't even have that anymore: a name for solitude. Ours is called with apps, with series, with the right coffee. Nobody calls it dreams anymore. Dreams cost, or make fear, and they are the only two currencies left.

On the tram back, one of those evenings, a thing happened to me that I don't tell anyone. My heart left as if it had to give me bad news: three extra beats a second, the sky that came close, my hands that froze. It lasted four minutes, or a life; at the stop it ended, and only the tiredness stayed. I didn't say it to anyone. It's hard to tell fear to people who don't expect it from you: people believe you're someone who manages, and if you open your mouth, their surprise costs you more than the fear. So I kept it. I rested it at the bottom of the drawer, under the laundromat receipts, and the Sunday after I was someone who manages again.

At home I had a shelf of four levels, and on one of them there was a book I hadn't opened in years: *The Brothers Karamazov*, with my father's pencil underlinings. Every so often I passed by it and didn't touch it, the way you do with the things of the dead: you can look, you can't use. It stayed there, with the cover worn at the corners and the breath of old paper, doing its part: being the father who wasn't there, and saying nothing, as was his habit.

It was on Sunday evening, in that state of things — the headset, the series, the app, the fear hidden in the drawer — that my mother's call came.

The phone rang twice and on the display it said *Mum*. I took the phone the way you take the phone on Sunday evening: without suspicion, with the hand that was looking for the remote.

— Hi love, all fine — she said.

Her voice was too cheerful, like a room too brightly lit: you see everything, except what they don't want you to see. She seemed like someone pretending to be serene.

Pretending it, people tell you everything.

# Chapter 2 — The Small Things


> "All happy families are alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina*

My mother's house was on the first floor of a small building on corso Traiano, above a curtain shop. You knew from the stairwell that a seamstress lived there: it smelled of fabric and hot iron, and there was always a thread, somewhere, that nobody gathered. The windows gave onto the back, where there was a courtyard with an acanthus that had never flowered, and inside the house there was the usual light: yellow, low, the light of a lamp that makes you look but doesn't make you see.

I went to her that same evening, as is their habit, the Sunday calls: they're there to call you, and I went.

My mother was in the sewing room, which was also the dinner room: a wooden table covered with a coarse cloth, the sewing machine in the corner, the iron breathing heat on a chair. She was the only person I knew with two trades in the same room: she sewed and she cooked, and she did the two things with the same hands, and the hands were always visible — short nails, the needle's hump, the wrist that doesn't shake. On the sewing machine there was a job covered with a gauze, and I didn't ask what it was: covered things aren't asked, they uncover themselves, when they're ready.

She made dinner the way Sunday dinners are made: without hurry and with hurry, two things together, like someone who knows there's a thing to be said and is preparing it too, in the dish. We talked about small things: the curtain shop that had closed, the Ladies of the courtyard, the fact that it hadn't rained yet in Turin.

— I made those curtains, you know — she said. — Thirty years ago. The shop kept them ten years, and then took them down, and threw them away. —

She said *throws away* with the same tone she said *sews*: a detail, not a sentence.

— And of the Ladies of the courtyard, one has gone to a care home. The other brings her flowers every Sunday. —

— And do you go and see her? — I asked.

— Once a month. — She set the dish in front of me, with her hands. — Things are kept, Teo. They're kept once a month, they're kept every Sunday. They're kept. —

I don't know what she meant exactly. I know she was right, as is her habit, my mother: she was always right, and she didn't explain the reason.

And in the end, while drying the dishes — she had the hands of someone who has redone hems for forty years, short nails, a thin scar on her right thumb — she said, without turning:

— I have a thing to tell you, but don't be frightened. —

Things said with *don't be frightened* are always the worst. I took the dish from her hand and waited.

— I have a knot on my ovary — she said. — They've known for three months. No panic: they're taking it out. —

A knot. She had said *knot*, with the voice of the sewing room: as if you could pull the thread, as if you could undo the knot and redo the seam, as if the body were a garment you take back to the atelier. And maybe for her it was: she was a seamstress, and bodies she had always seen from outside, the way you see garments — from the reverse, which is the side where you understand how the front is made.

— They've known for three months — I repeated.

They had known for three months and she had kept calling me on Sunday evenings saying *all fine*, with the too-cheerful voice, the room too brightly lit. I didn't get angry. I only understood, with a blow to the chest that didn't pass for a week, that the fear I felt on the phone wasn't hers. It was mine. Hers she had already put away: folded, put in the needle drawer, the way you put the things you need and don't want to look at.

Every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way — I read it somewhere, years later, and I recognised it at once. Ours was unhappy in the silence: we didn't shout, we didn't say things to each other, we put them away. My mother sewed silence the way hems are sewn: with small, regular stitches, invisible from outside. And I grew inside that seam, and I called it peace, because I didn't know the name silence has when it isn't peace.

The last time a thing like this had happened I was nineteen.

Dad was at Mirafiori, on the assembly line, and every morning he left at ten to five with the thermal bag I still keep: a bag of those that smelled of coffee and work. I remember his mornings: the door that closed softly, as is their habit, fathers — they close softly, so as not to wake you, and you wake anyway and don't know it. I remember the bag on the kitchen table, with the coffee that smelled of work. And I remember the eleventh of February, when a foreman called me from a number I didn't know, at eleven on a February morning.

— Your father has been taken ill. Come. —

*Come*, in the plural, as if we were one thing. And we were: we were two, mum and I, and from that day we have been one.

We arrived at the hospital at twenty past eleven, and my father wasn't there anymore: he had gone out on the assembly line.

— Heart attack — they said. — All of a sudden. — With the *all of a sudden* that in a factory is more frequent than elsewhere: hearts that work to a rhythm break to a rhythm. Nobody says it. You feel it, the way you feel damp in walls: there are factories where you know the rhythm by the smell.

At the funeral the factory came: the workers in a line, with hands that didn't know what to do, and the foreman who held my hand for a second, as is their habit, heavy things — they aren't said, they're kept. And there were the things that are kept: the thermal bag, which my mother put in the sewing room's wardrobe and we never opened again. The things of the dead are kept closed: they open when the other dies, as is their habit, things — they give you back the smell, not the person.

After the funeral, going home, I found on his bedside table *The Brothers Karamazov*, open at half. From that time it was there, on the shelf, with the worn cover. In the margins there were his pencil underlinings, light, the way you underline the things you don't dare say out loud. On the page where an old monk tells a father, above all, not to lie to himself, there was a long, curved mark, that resembled a seam.

I didn't say it to anyone. I didn't say it even to me. But I always knew it: my father, in a life of shifts and rhythm and thermal bags, had found the time to underline one thing only, and had chosen that one. Above all, don't lie to yourself. He had underlined the *above all*. And then he had died on a production line, telling everyone he was fine.

The days after I did the things I knew how to do. I went to work, I said *sir, I understand*, I ate the episode of the day, I drank the right coffee. But the small things, that time, didn't want to be enough: the coffee tasted of nothing, the four-stop walk seemed a corridor, and in the app I brushed past one face after another with the feeling of stopping time with my thumb — stopping it, not living it. It isn't true that the small things are enough. They're enough as long as there's the big one, somewhere, even if you don't see it. When the big one isn't there, the small ones are seen for what they are: dots. A seam made of dots, so as not to show the reverse.

# Chapter 3 — The Night Ward


I went to the hospital the day after, with the driver of a Monday — the driver of a Monday is the driver you don't choose: you take it. The parking of the Molinette was full, and the full, in a hospital, isn't called full: it's called Tuesday. I found the fourth floor with the lift, and the lift, in a hospital, is the thing you don't choose: you take it, and it takes you.

The fourth floor was a corridor, and the corridor was the corridor of always: the neon, the doors, the smell that isn't a smell — the smell of the things that are counted: the disinfectant, the blood, the machines. On the fourth floor there were the rooms, and the rooms had numbers, and the numbers were the thing that stays: the room 412 was my mother's, and the 412, for six weeks, was my address.

My mother was in the room with the window that gives on the other hospital, and the window, in a hospital, is the window you don't choose: you take it, and it takes you. My mother was sitting on the bed, with the nightgown of the hospital and the face of someone who has already put away the fear — folded, put in the drawer of the needles. She wasn't frightened. She was the one who frightened me: she was fine, in the way of the people who have already decided, and the decision was the thing I saw: it was in the hands, and the hands were on the blanket, still.

The operation was on the Thursday, and the Thursday, in a hospital, is the Thursday of the operations: you take it. I waited in the corridor with the families of the others, and the families of the others were the families of the things that are kept: the mothers, the sons, the husbands — and nobody spoke, because in the corridors of the operations nobody speaks: they keep. The operation lasted three hours, and the three hours, in the corridor, were the three hours I counted: three hundred and sixty, three hundred and fifty-nine, three hundred and fifty-eight — backwards, the way you count the things you don't say.

My mother came out at five, and my mother was fine: the knot was out, and the knot was the thing I didn't see. I saw it only in the hands of the surgeon, who said *it went well* with the voice of someone who says it every Thursday, and the voice, in a hospital, is the voice that doesn't lie: it's the voice of the things that are counted.

Nora was on the fourth floor, and Nora was the night nurse, and the night nurse, in a hospital, is the nurse of the things that are not counted: the fear of the night, the sleep of the morning, the dying. Nora wasn't my mother's nurse: my mother went out, and Nora stayed. I met her in the corridor, the third week, and the corridor, at three in the morning, is the corridor of the things that are kept.

I had gone to the hospital at night, the first time, without knowing why: you don't go to hospitals at night, and I had gone. The corridor was empty, and in the corridor there was a voice — a voice that read, softly, the way you read to someone who sleeps — and the voice was Nora's, and Nora was in the room 409, and the room 409 was the room of the things that are not counted.

She was reading Proust. Not to me: to the man of the 409, the man who was dying, and the man didn't hear it, or heard it, and the hearing wasn't the thing: the reading was. Nora read the part of the madeleine — the part where the taste of the cake brings back a life — and the reading, at three in the morning, in the corridor, is the thing I heard standing, without entering: you don't enter the rooms of the things that are not counted. You hear.

Nora came out at four, and Nora had the eyes of the night nurses: the eyes that don't close, and the closing, for the night nurses, is the thing that isn't done. She saw me in the corridor, and she didn't ask who I was. Heavy things aren't asked: they're kept.

— Family of 412? — she said, and it was the question of the nurse, and the question was the question of always.

— Son — I said.

— Your mother sleeps — she said. — They all sleep, at four. It's the only hour in which they sleep. —

And she went to the nurses' room, and I stayed in the corridor, with the voice of the 409 that wasn't there anymore: Nora had closed the book, and the book, in the room of the things that are not counted, was the thing that stayed. I stayed in the corridor, and I stayed a long time, and the staying, at three in the morning, in a corridor, is the staying of the things that are kept.

The days after I went to the hospital every day, in the evening, after the work: the sixth floor of Elnord, the tram, the hospital. And Nora was there, in the corridor, at three, and the three, at night, was the hour I learned: the hour of the things that are not counted. We met at the coffee machine, the third time, and the machine of the coffee, in a hospital, is the machine that doesn't choose: it takes you. The coffee was short and lukewarm, and Nora said the thing the night nurses say:

— The coffee here is like the night: it doesn't wake you, and it doesn't let you sleep. —

It was the first thing she said to me, and the thing, at three in the morning, was the thing I kept: I kept it in the pocket of the coat, and the coat was the coat of the visits. Nora had the hands of the night nurses — the hands that don't close — and the hands, at three, were the hands that read: the hands that read Proust to the dying. And I asked her the thing I didn't ask:

— Why Proust? —

Nora looked at me, and the look was the look of the night nurses: the eyes that don't close.

— Because it's long — she said. — And the dying have the time. We don't. They do. —

And she went to the 409, and I stayed in the corridor, and the corridor, at four in the morning, was the corridor of the things that are kept: the voice, the book, the coffee that nobody chose.

The last week of the visits my mother went out, and the going out, in a hospital, is the thing that isn't counted: it's the thing you do. My mother went out, and Nora stayed on the fourth floor, and I stayed with the coffee machine, and the coffee machine, in a hospital, is the machine of the things that are kept: it takes you.

Nora's number I had: she gave it to me at the last visit, without my asking — the way you give the things that are kept. She gave it to me, and she said:

— For the things that aren't counted. —

And the things that aren't counted, at thirty-three, were the things I was beginning to have: the fear, the corridor, the voice that reads. And I called her, the week after, and the call was the call of the things that aren't counted: and she answered.

# Chapter 4 — The Measure of the Days


> "Ivan Ilyich's life was the most simple, the most ordinary, and therefore the most terrible."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*

The operation was on a Tuesday and lasted four hours and a half.

The waiting for surgeons is a thing nobody tells, and instead it's the truest part of the hospital: the room with the plastic chairs, the silent television that gave the weather of a city I didn't know, the relatives who looked at their phones the way you look at waters. There was a man who ate crisps from the vending machine without looking at them, as is their habit, people in waiting rooms: they eat with their hands, not from hunger. There was a woman who embroidered — embroidered, in a waiting room, with hands that knew the reverse, and I looked at her a long time: she was the only thing in that room that didn't shake. I counted the tiles: thirty-three, up to the door, then thirty-three again. The fear, in the drawer, made its noise only when I stopped counting, so I counted all morning. The number is a story without characters, and in those rooms it's fine like this: the characters arrive after, with the green slippers.

At ten past two the surgeon came out with the green slippers and looked with his eyes for someone. His hands held a report and didn't shake, and the hands that don't shake, in those rooms, are the only thing that holds.

— Lucia Resta's family? —

— Yes. —

— We are satisfied. We took out everything that could be taken. Now we see what the pathology says, but for now we can sleep. —

*For now*. It's the truest thing you can say in a hospital, and the most terrible: it's the only word that holds the hopes and the reports together. I learned to hate it and to love it at the same time, the way you hate and love a season.

My mother got back on her feet the way seamstresses get back on their feet: with the hands first and the rest after. The third morning she was already doing her gestures in the air, above the blanket, as if she were ironing something we didn't see, and when I asked how she was she said: — They took out the knot. Now I only have to redo the seams. — She also said *tired*, but she said it the way you say a detail, not a sentence.

I did a thing I had never done: I went back to the fourth floor also on the days there was nothing to go back for. The Tuesday after the operation I went back, and the Wednesday, and the Thursday; the day nurses began to know me by name, and one of them told me I was "the son of the eight", because I always came at eight and always stayed longer. I went down to the coffee machine and came back up with two cups, one in each hand. One for my mother, one for whoever was there. At three at night, for whoever was there, there was almost always Nora.

One of those mornings, at five, when the fourth floor slept and the two of us sat in the plastic chairs of the corridor, I asked Nora why she read aloud to the patients. Nobody had ever asked her, and I didn't expect her to tell me.

— My grandmother had Alzheimer's — she said, with the voice of someone who has already finished crying a thing and only keeps it to tell. — She didn't recognise anyone anymore. Except my grandfather's voice reading. The voice stayed, when everything else had gone. I learned from her that names are lost last, and voices are never lost. —

She said *my grandfather* with the same care with which she said *Lucia*: names, for her, were medicine. I didn't ask anything else. Heavy things aren't asked.

It was also in those nights that Nora looked me in the face for the second time, and the second time was worse than the first, because this time she asked:

— And how are you really? —

— Fine — I said.

— You too — she said. — You too, one who pretends. —

She added nothing else. She had the trade of seeing people, and she had seen me: someone who pretends serenity the way you pretend a hem, with small and invisible stitches. I wasn't angry. I was only grateful, with the gratitude of someone who is seen for the first time after years — which is a gratitude that hurts, as it's right that it hurts: the things that don't hurt, in a hospital, are the ones that aren't needed.

On Sunday Nora had her day off and came to the market of Porta Palazzo with me. She had said *she comes*, in the present, as if it were already done, and with her the things in the present were.

The market of Porta Palazzo was full, and the full, in a market, is the thing that doesn't count: it's the thing that is. Nora walked among the stalls the way the night nurses walk: without looking at the things, and seeing them all. She bought the vegetables for my mother — the courgettes, the tomatoes — and the buying, in a market, was the thing she did with the hands: the hands of the night nurses, the hands that don't close. At the coffee of the market there was the coffee of always — short, lukewarm — and Nora said the thing of the books:

— In the books I read, they say the small things are the consolation. — She drank the coffee. — They're not the consolation. They're the life, and the consolation is the thing we invent not to see them. —

It was the thing I had thought in the office, at the episodes of the day, and the thing, said by Nora, was the thing I had thought without knowing I thought it: the small things were the life, and the consolation was the invention. I drank the coffee, and the coffee, in the market, with Nora, was the coffee of the things that are kept.

I was a man like any other, living as I should (Tolstoy): the office, the episodes, the app. And the living as I should, at thirty-three, was the living of the appearances — and the appearances, in the market, with Nora, were the thing that didn't hold. Nora held. The holding, at thirty-three, was the thing I didn't know how to do, and Nora did it without knowing she did it: the way you do the things that are kept.

The Sunday after I took her to dinner, and the dinner was the dinner of the things that are counted: the restaurant, the wine, the bill. And the bill, at thirty-three, was the thing I paid without counting it: the first thing I paid without counting, in years. Nora looked at the bill, and the look was the look of the night nurses:

— You didn't count it. —

— No — I said.

— That's how you keep — she said. —

# Chapter 5 — Lucia


Nora came to know my mother on an afternoon in November, at four, outside the visiting hours, because at that hour the fourth floor was Nora's and the rules were hers.

My mother watched her come in and did the thing she did with people: she looked her in the face, from the hands to the hair, the way you look at fabrics. Fabrics are looked at from the reverse, but people are looked at from the front, and my mother knew both things.

— You're the one of the coffee — she said.

— I'm the one of almost everything, in here — Nora said. — But the coffee, yes. —

My mother laughed softly, with the laugh she had learned in the ateliers, in a low voice, as is the habit of those who work in rooms where little is said, and made Nora a place next to the bed.

She had brought up two cups, one in each hand, as was her habit. My mother took hers with two hands, and the hands of those who keep things are always visible: short nails, the hump of the fabric, the wrist that doesn't shake. They talked about the things that are talked about on fourth floors at four, which are the small things, and they were the true ones. Then my mother called me next to the bed and gave me the thimble.

She kept it in the bedside drawer, with the things that were needed. # Chapter 6 — Everything Else Is Noise


> "the only true paradise is the paradise we have lost."

> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

Telling the death to everyone else is a thing you don't learn. You do it, and it stays.

I said it to the relatives on the phone, one by one, with the voice I had learned from my mother — not the cheerful one, the soft one. And the smell was the thing I kept: I kept it, and the keeping was the thing I did for the first time — the keeping of the paradise that is lost (Proust). The paradise, in the empty house, was recognised: it was recognised smell by smell, drawer by drawer, and the recognising was the thing I did for the first time — the recognising of the things that are kept.

# Chapter 7 — The Nights of the Living


Cohabitation never begins: it's noticed.

# Chapter 9 — Hay

# Chapter 11 — The Two Cups


The night shift begins like this: it isn't said, it's done.

The book opened where he had stopped, fourteen years before: at half. Books open where the people who read them stopped, and it's the cruelest and the kindest thing they do — they give you back the page, not the person.

In the margins there were the pencil underlinings, light, as is their habit, the things not said: you underline the things you don't dare say out loud. On the page where an old monk tells a father, above all, not to lie to himself, there was the long, curved mark, that resembled a seam.

I read that page aloud, to Nora, at three at night, in a kitchen.

She had never heard it from anyone, and neither had I: I had only read it inside, which is like not reading it. I read the way it is her habit, the voice that reads: slowly, the way you read to children or to the old or to the people who can no longer hold a book. And Nora listened as is their habit, medicines: with the ears, the eyes closed, the breath slow. It was my first shift. The patient was the person I love, and it's the only thing that can be done for those who assist: assist her. The rest is bureaucracy.

I read and I felt my father's pencil in the lines: he underlined with the care used by someone who reads one thing only in a life, and read it well. He had read it, in a life of shifts, between one piece and another, and had underlined it: the true thing of a life, written in pencil by a man who never wrote.

When the page ended, Nora said the name.

Out loud, for the first time since May. I don't write the name: it's the only word of this book I don't give. But it was the first thing I heard in her voice since the dark had entered our house. Names are lost last: it's what Nora knew, from her grandmother. And they are never lost, if someone says them.

I took the sewing machine from the little room that night and put it in the kitchen, next to the box. The things that are kept stay together: the unfinished shirt, the thimble, the lists. I didn't know yet what I would do with it. Things aren't asked: they arrive by themselves, when they're ready.

It was at the end of June, in one of those nights, that Nora said:

— Let's go to the sea. —

And the sea, in the sentence, was the thing that didn't count: it was the thing that is kept. The things that are kept, in the sentence, were the things Nora said: the sea, and the saying was the saying of the things that are kept — said at three at night, in a kitchen, with the book open at half. The saying was the thing I kept: I keep it still, and the keeping is the thing that doesn't count — the keeping of the saying, at thirty-three, was the thing that made the sea. And the sea, in June, was the thing that was kept: it was kept the way the things are kept that don't go — the iron, the book, the sewing machine. And the sea, in June, was the thing Nora and I did: we kept it, and the keeping was the thing that didn't make light — the keeping is the thing that is done in the dark, and the dark, in June, was the dark of the things that are kept.

The two cups, at three at night, were the thing I learned: the trade of those who assist, and the trade isn't taught — it's learned from the people who assist. And the learning, at thirty-three, was the thing I did: I learned it, and the learning was the thing that didn't count. It's the thing that is kept: the two cups, one in each hand, and the keeping was the thing I did for the first time — the keeping of the trade of someone else. And the trade of someone else, at thirty-three, was the thing I did: I did it, and the doing was the thing that didn't make light.

It was the thing that made the sea.

# Chapter 12 — Where the Sea Begins


> "How could I not have seen that lofty sky before?"
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *War and Peace*

The sea was four hours by car, and Dario lent us the car, as is its habit, life that works: it works for others too.

He lent it on Thursday, with the full tank — the full tank is the only thing Dario knows how to do for others without saying it, and he said it anyway, as is their habit, the things that work: they're said, and they don't know it.

— Go slowly — he said. — Not for the tank. —

And he added nothing else. True things aren't explained: they're said, and the rest is understood by those who receive them.

We left on Saturday morning early, as is their habit, journeys: you leave at night or early in the morning, and you don't explain. Nora brought the blue toothbrush, a t-shirt and the small book with the worn cover — *Swann's Way*, which you bring to the sea as is their habit, books: you bring them, and they are never lost. I brought the thimble, and my father's book, and the unfinished shirt. The things that are kept are brought to the sea: it's their habit.

The motorway was full and it wasn't, as is their habit, Saturday motorways: they fill with cars and the cars don't speak. I drove slowly — you drive slowly, when you bring all the things that are kept. Nora looked at the window, and the window said the world: the fields, the roads, the things we didn't know. We didn't speak for an hour. But it was the usual silence — the seam — and seams are kept, if someone has sewn them well.

Varigotti I saw before arriving, from beyond the last curve, as is their habit, seas: they're seen before arriving. And when you arrive you're no longer surprised, and it's fine, because the sea isn't a surprise: it's a thing that has always been awaited.

The sky above the sea was high, and said nothing, as is their habit, skies: they say everything and say nothing, and you understand only that you are small. I had read it in a book — a wounded prince who looked at the sky above a lost battle and understood, for the first time, that everything else was small, except that. At Varigotti I understood what he had understood: that everything else was small, except the sky, and that the sky asks nothing.

It was the first time in years I looked at something without counting. The sky isn't counted: it's looked at.

The beach was of the end of June, and there were the people of the beaches: the umbrellas, the children who shouted — they shout because life is still whole, and they don't know it tears — and the two of us, who walked on the edge of the water.

Nora took off her sandals, and the sandals, in the sand, are the things that aren't kept: they're left. And the leaving was the thing she did: she left them, and the leaving was the thing that didn't count — the leaving of the things that don't count, at thirty-three, is the thing you do at the sea: you leave them, and the sea keeps them. The sea keeps everything, as is its trade, and the trade of the sea is the keeping: it keeps, and the keeping isn't counted.

David's ashes were in the sea, and the ashes, in the sea, are the things that are kept: they're kept by the sea, and the keeping of the sea is the keeping that doesn't ask. We walked to the point where David was, and the point was the point of the things that are kept: you don't see them, you know them. Nora said the name, out loud, for the second time since May — the name isn't written in this book: it's the only word I don't give. And the saying, at the sea, was the saying of the things that are kept: it was said out loud, and the saying was the thing that didn't count — the saying of the things that are kept, at thirty-three, is the thing you do with the voice: and the voice, said at the sea, was the voice of the things that are kept.

We let go. We didn't forget: you don't forget the things that are kept — you let them go, and the letting go is the thing that is done with the sea, and the sea keeps them. It's their secret: the sea keeps, and the keeping is the thing that doesn't ask.

The afternoon was the afternoon of the things that are kept: the sandwiches, the book open at half, the shirt. And the shirt, unfinished, was on the sand, with the needle in it, and the needle was the thing my mother had: the needle of the things that are kept. And I took the needle, and I sewed: I sewed the shirt, on the sand, with the thimble on the thumb — the thimble my mother had given me, the thimble of the things that are kept. And the sewing, at thirty-three, was the thing I did for the first time: the sewing of the things that are kept, done with the hands. And the sewing didn't count: it was the thing that is kept — the sewing, at thirty-three, was the thing that made the shirt, and the shirt was the thing that was kept, and the keeping was the thing that didn't ask.

Nora watched me sew, and the watching was the thing she did: the watching of the things that are kept, and the things that are kept, watched, are warm. It's their secret: they're kept, and the keeping warms them — it's the thing Nora said, in the empty house, with the iron: *it's still warm*.

The sky above the sea was high, and didn't say anything, and the sky was the thing I looked at without counting: the sky isn't counted, it's looked at. And the looking, at thirty-three, was the thing I learned: the things that don't count, you look at, and the looking is the thing that is kept. It's the secret of the things that are kept: they're kept, and the keeping is the thing that doesn't count.

The morning after we went back, and the going back was the thing that is done with the sea: you go back, and the sea keeps. The things that are kept, in the car, were the things of always: the toothbrush, the book, the shirt — the shirt, finished, was on the back seat, and the finishing was the thing I did: the sewing of the things that are kept, done on the sand, with the thimble. And the finishing, at thirty-three, was the thing that didn't count: it was the thing that is kept.

# Epilogue — After


A year has passed.

I am at home, in the kitchen, at eleven on a spring morning, and I write. It isn't a trade I have: I am learning it, as is their habit, true things — they're learned late, and you never stop. I write slowly, without counting the words — the words aren't counted, they're kept. It's the only difference I have learned in a year: counting the tiles and keeping the words.

The sewing machine is next to me, on the table, and the shirt is finished.

I finished it in spring, with the thimble and the needle drawer: small, regular stitches, invisible from outside — as is their habit, my mother's seams. I didn't know how to sew. I learned: it's the only thing that can be done for unfinished things — finish them, as is their habit, things: they aren't thrown away, they're redone. My mother always said: *things must be redone, not thrown away*. I finished it and I am wearing it now, while I write. It's grey, as is their habit, true things: grey, and it's fine.

Nora went back to her shift in November, with the voice that reads and the names as medicine. It was the bravest thing I have ever seen: those who assist, after having lost, go back to assisting — and it isn't oblivion, it's courage. It's her trade, and she took it back as is their habit, true trades: they aren't left, they're taken back.

We repainted the wall of the little room white, as soon as she knew. The azure is still there, underneath: as is their habit, things — they aren't erased, they're covered. And the covered things are all kept, underneath. The white is the only thing that can be done for a room that waits: making it wait well.

Nora is pregnant again. She learned it in March, with firm hands on the table and the eyes in the face — as is their habit, big things: they're said in the face.

— We have a small thing coming — she said, with January's voice, and the voice was the same, and it wasn't: it had inside a new fear, which is the fear of those who have already lost.

— And you? — I asked. — Are you afraid? —

— A lot — she said. — And I keep it. —

The fear of those who have already lost isn't the double of fear: it's a thing you keep with your hands, and it's the only thing that can be done: keeping it.

We kept it.

The lists we never closed: the two names are still there, and there is a new one, which we didn't know — and which we would choose when it comes. It will come in its own way, without knocking, and we will say it in the face, with firm hands on the table. Lists aren't closed: you close them, and the future closes — I wrote it, and it's true.

The small things are the same: the right coffee, the rising fog, the four stops that lead nowhere. But I look at them as if they were new, as is their habit, the things that are found again: they're not found where they were — they're found in the eyes of those who look. The lost paradise, the true one, I have found it again: it wasn't lost.

We were the ones who didn't look.

This book is the story of that year. I wrote it so as not to lose the things twice, as I had promised at the beginning — to nobody, and to me. I lost them once only: my mother, the daughter. And I kept them all, as is their habit, the things that are kept: in the thimble, in the drawer, in the voice, in the name I don't write.

This book is for those who stay.

## A note on the allusions

The protagonist's reflections contain subtle allusions to Dostoevsky, Tolstoy and Proust, marked in parentheses: they are not quotations — they are ways of thinking borrowed. The epigraphs at the start of the chapters are real quotations: Fyodor Dostoevsky (*The Brothers Karamazov*), Leo Tolstoy (*Anna Karenina*, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, *War and Peace*), Marcel Proust (*Time Regained*, *Swann's Way*, *Pleasures and Days*). The name of the daughter is not written in this book: it is the only word the narrator does not give.
