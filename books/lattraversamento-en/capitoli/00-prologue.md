# Prologue — The Folded Sheet

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (the Questura) · **Story time:** winter 2026–27
**Words:** ~900 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/00-prologo.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "Man is a being that gets used to everything."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Memories from the House of the Dead*

The Questura office in Milan opens at half past eight, and by nine the queue already reaches the gate. You stand in line with the folded sheet in your hand, the way you hold a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of your own life, and that the numbers are never good. The sheet is folded in four, and it has been folded so long that it opens by itself: it is the wisest thing they have given me in four years. A piece of paper with my face printed on it, my name pronounced wrong, and the writing: *provisional residence permit*. Provisional. After four years, provisional.

In front of me was a man who had come from Bangladesh, and he spoke of the renewal with the voice of someone who has long stopped explaining. Behind, a woman who had come from Ecuador, with the child sleeping in her arms, and the child, in the queue, slept the way things sleep: for real. I spoke to nobody. I held the sheet and I looked at the numbers on the display: B047, B048, B049. Mine was B052, and numbers, in a queue, all go up like rents.

My fingerprints were taken at Lampedusa, the first day, and fingerprints don't get erased: it's their law. Fingerprints don't say the days: they say the place. The stamp, in the books of a girl I read, said thirty days; the fingerprints say: here. And here, after four years, is the place that doesn't get erased. In Joal I had a sea that erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back: it's the law of the sea. Fingerprints are the opposite of the sea: they don't come back, and they don't get erased. They keep.

I came here because I had to. Not because I wanted to. I used to say it to everyone, the first months, with the voice of someone who doesn't complain: the father who sells the pirogue, the fish that isn't there, the sister who goes to school. I never said it like that: I said it with the calculation, at home, on the phone: *I will send three hundred euros a month*. And the calculation said a thing I hadn't said to anyone, not even to myself, in four years: that there, in Joal, there wasn't anything left to have. The sea had emptied, and the building sites of Dakar gave fifty euros a day, and fifty euros a day are the fish that isn't there: you earn them and they aren't enough.

Because the calculation counted everything, except the main thing: that here, in Milan, I pay double for everything. The bed in the four-bed room at three hundred euros — a bed, not a room —, the ten per cent on every sending home, the language that doesn't bend, the farang price at the shop downstairs. In Joal my father's house cost nothing, and the fish was my father's, and the sea was everyone's. Here nothing is everyone's, and everything costs double, and I have paid double for four years, and I can't go back. You can pay double for years, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom: the girl in the book ran away so she would stop overpaying. I came to pay. I can't go back: it's the thing you don't say at home, and you don't say in Milan.

The number was called: B052, counter nine. The man behind the glass took the sheet, looked at the fingerprints, and asked me the purpose of my stay. The purpose of my stay. I stood there, holding the question, and I had already answered it four years before, in a camp at Lampedusa, to a man with a pencil: work. It was the right word and it wasn't the truth: after four years I wasn't someone who arrives anymore, and I wasn't someone who stays, and I wasn't anything that has a name on a form.

I went out, and outside there was the fog, the fog of Milan, the fog I didn't know before and that now I know better than the sun. In Joal the sun set at seven, and the sea erased itself, and the sea came back. The fog, in Milan, doesn't come back: it stays.

This is the story of four years, and of how a man from Joal crosses the sea and pays double for everything — the bed, the language, the sendings — and can't go back; and of how, in the end, coming back, he asks himself the question nobody answers.

I begin at the beginning, which was Joal, and it cost nothing: and it cost everything else.
