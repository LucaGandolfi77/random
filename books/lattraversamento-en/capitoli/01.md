# Chapter 1 — The Pirogue

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Joal (Senegal) · **Story time:** 2021–2023, two years
**Words:** ~1.100 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/01.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "All happy families are alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina*

In Joal the sea erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back. It's the law of the sea, and I learned the law from my father, who had learned it from his father: the sea goes and the sea comes back, and those who live with the sea never really leave. I lived in a house that cost nothing — my father's house, of bricks and tin, with the street that gave onto the sea — and the sea was everyone's, and the fish was my father's, and the house was ours. You can be poor in everything, and not poor in home: it was my wealth, and I didn't know it.

The families of Joal, in the evening, were all alike: the mother who cooks, the father who comes back with the sea, the children who run until night. Happy families are all alike (Tolstoy), and ours resembled those: it was happy in the way of families that have the sea, and the sea was the only thing that wasn't counted. The Sunday thieboudienne, in the evening, was the truest thing: my mother made it with my father's fish, and my father's fish, on Sunday, was the gift you don't buy: you receive it. Pleasure, in Joal, belonged to living desire, and living desire, in Joal, isn't bought: you have it.

My father was called Ousmane, and he had thirty years of sea. Thirty years on the same pirogue, painted blue, that was called Fatou — my sister's name, given to the boat the day she was born. My father spoke of the sea with the voice of someone who has worked his whole life in one thing, and the one thing was the sea: the sea isn't a trade. The sea is a place, and the trade is the fish, and for thirty years there was fish. My father, in the morning, went out with the sea at five, and five in the morning, in Joal, is the five of those who have the sea: you go out and you ask nothing. My father's hands, in the evening, came back full, or empty: and empty hands, in the evening, are the hands that aren't said: they're kept.

Then the sea emptied. The big boats arrived from over there, on the horizon, and the big boats took everyone's fish, and everyone's fish is nobody's fish. Nature, they said at school, promises: and nature doesn't deliver what it promises then (Leopardi, *A Silvia*), and the sea that promised fish didn't give it anymore. The fishermen of Joal began to come back with empty pirogues, and empty pirogues, in the evening, are the heaviest pirogues in the world: emptiness weighs. My father came back, and said nothing. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're weighed in the evening, and the weight, in the evening, you don't feel: you notice later. It was the thing I understood late, and I understood it four years late: the weight of empty pirogues you don't feel in the evening. You feel it years later, in another country, in a queue.

I worked, those years, on the building sites of Dakar. The building sites of Dakar are a trade: you carry the cement, you mix, you tie the iron. Fifty euros a day, ten hours, and the undeclared work: the hours aren't counted, they're finished. I was, though, someone who had studied — I had done secondary school in Joal, and I knew French well, and French, on building sites, isn't needed: you know it perfectly. The things you study, when they aren't needed, you know perfectly: it's their secret. French wasn't needed. Wolof was spoken, and wolof is home. Italian I didn't even know existed.

In two years I put aside fifteen hundred euros. Fifteen hundred euros put aside in two years, living the reduced version: the shared room in Dakar, the market shopping, presents made with reason. Fifteen hundred euros: it was everything I had, and it wasn't little: it was half of nothing. The crossing cost three thousand euros. Three thousand a head, taken from the guy who fixed things — the fixer, and the fixer doesn't have a name: he has a price. Three thousand euros for a place on a boat you don't choose, and you don't choose the boat because the boats you choose don't leave at night.

Moussa stayed. Moussa had been my friend since we were six, and he stayed: he had taken his father's pirogue, the way you take things that stay, and his life was ours: the sea, the fish, the house. His life aimed: at the wedding, at the children, at the fish. Moussa had a purpose, and a purpose, in Joal, makes a life.

Omar had left two years before me, for Paris. Omar had the escape done well: he had the papers, a job, an apartment with a door that closes. Escaping, he said on the phone, is done well when you have a door that closes and a door that opens: two doors. I had neither. I had three thousand euros and a boat at night.

I made the decision because I had to. Not because I wanted to: I didn't say it to myself with these words. I said it with the phone, to my mother: *Fatou's school*, and Fatou's school was my purpose. My sister was eleven, and went to school, and school was paid, and the fish wasn't there, and the building sites weren't enough. The reversal, at twenty-six, is that those who leave leave for a purpose: not for freedom. Freedom, in the books of the girl I read, was the opposite: you leave so you stop paying. I left to pay. It's different, and it's nothing different.

My father sold the pirogue. The blue pirogue, the one called Fatou, thirty years of sea, sold for fifteen hundred euros: the other half of the crossing. The sea paid with the sea, and the sea, in Joal, is the biggest thing you have: you sell it and you don't take it back. My father sold it without my asking. Heavy things aren't asked.

— You'll come back — my father said, in the evening, with his voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly. — Inshallah. —

Inshallah. God willing. The answer to everything. I didn't yet know it was the answer to everything, and that the answer to everything answers nothing.

The morning after I left. Heavy things aren't said. They're kept, and you cross with them.
