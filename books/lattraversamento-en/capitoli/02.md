# Chapter 2 — The Desert

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** from Dakar to Tripoli · **Story time:** June 2023, three weeks
**Words:** ~1.000 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/02.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "O nature, o nature, / why do you not deliver / what you promise then? why do you deceive / your children so?"
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *A Silvia*

I left on a Friday in June, from Dakar, and in Dakar there was my mother, at the station, with the shopping package and the face that doesn't cry. My father wasn't there: he was in Joal, with the sea, and the sea, in the morning, doesn't let go: you look at the things that let go, and my father didn't look at them. Heavy things aren't said. My mother gave me the package, and in the package there was thieboudienne, and my mother's thieboudienne tasted of those things you don't describe: you eat them. Fatou held my hand until the bus, and the hand, at the station, is the hand you don't buy: you keep it. I ate it on the bus, for three days, and for three days the bus was the world.

The bus went north, and the route was the one you don't choose: Mali, then Niger, then Libya. The route, in books, isn't chosen: it's taken. The bus was full of people like me: people with a package, people with a purpose, people who don't complain. The reason, in the morning, you feel in your legs: you don't feel it. You notice later, and later is made of borders.

The desert arrived on the fifth day, and the desert is the thing you don't know until you live it: the sun, the sand, the wind that takes the sand and puts it everywhere. Nature, in the desert, doesn't deliver what it promises then (Leopardi, *A Silvia*): the sun promises the day and gives the heat, and the heat, in the desert, is a wall. In Joal the heat was a wall that hugged you: it was the heat of the sea. There it was a wall that jostled you: the heat of the sand. The difference, in the desert, I felt. At night, instead, the desert turned: the heat went and the cold came, and the cold, in a country of sun, is the cold you don't expect: you put up with it. I slept with the sack, and the sack, at night, was the truest thing: you keep it.

In the truck there was water, and the water was paid for. Not double: triple. The bottle of water, in the truck, cost triple what it cost in Dakar, and triple, in the desert, isn't discussed: it's paid. The price, in the desert, is the price of those who pass: those who pass don't choose. I paid it, and I paid it without saying anything: the things you pay for in the desert, usually, aren't said. They're kept.

The borders cost. The border of Mali, the border of Niger, the border of Libya: three borders, and the three borders were paid for, and the price wasn't written, and you don't negotiate the price with anger. I made the anger, the first time, at the border of Niger: I said *how much?*, and I said *why?*, and the man at the border didn't laugh, and the not laughing, at the border, is the heaviest thing: you feel that the other knows, and the other doesn't say. In Joal heavy things aren't said: they're kept. At borders heavy things are paid, and the price, at the border, is the price of those who don't have a door that closes.

At the border of Niger, besides, there was the thing I don't tell anyone. A boy, in the truck, didn't leave again. I didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the truck, the way you keep the things that aren't needed. The truck left again, and the truck, in the morning, was the heaviest truck in the world.

Tripoli arrived on the fifteenth day, and Tripoli is the city you don't choose: with the walls, the streets, the dormitory. The dormitory was a room with twenty beds, and the twenty beds were twenty men with a package and a purpose, and nobody spoke: you speak softly, as is their habit, the men who wait — you speak softly because shouting tires, and tiredness, in the dormitory, is the only thing that isn't bought. There was the usual noise: the voices, the steps, the hum of the fan, and every so often a prayer — one, softly, as is their habit, prayers: they're said softly because God hears, and God, in the dormitory, hears everything.

I waited for the boat. The boat isn't waited for: it's awaited. The weeks in the dormitory were the longest weeks in the world, and the long weeks, in Tripoli, are the weeks that don't end: you cross them. The fixer came every evening, and every evening he said *tomorrow*, and *tomorrow*, in Tripoli, is the word you don't believe: you say.

I made the fear, the second week, at night, in the dormitory. The heart left as if it had to give me bad news: three extra beats a second, the sky that came close, the hands that froze. It lasted four minutes, or a life; at the end it ended, and only the tiredness stayed. I didn't say it to anyone. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the drawer, the way you keep the things that aren't needed.

In the evening, coming back from the dormitory, the fixer said the thing he said every evening: — Tomorrow. —

And I said the word I had learned from my father, with his voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly:

— Inshallah. —

It was the answer to everything, and I said it the way my father said it: without knowing it was the answer to everything. The answer to everything, in Tripoli, answers nothing. The boat came the evening after, and the boat was the last thing I didn't know. The sea, at night, you see: you don't look at it.
