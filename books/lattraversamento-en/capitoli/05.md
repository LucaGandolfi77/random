# Chapter 5 — The Ten Per Cent

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (via Padova, the sending office) · **Story time:** winter 2024
**Words:** ~1.100 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/05.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

I made the first sending in January, from the office on via Padova, with the three hundred euros in my hand. The office on via Padova is an office of those you don't choose: you take. The queue was the usual one: the men with packages, the women with children, the people who send. People who send, in the morning, don't look at each other: they recognise each other. We recognised each other, all of us, in that queue, and nobody said anything: they recognised each other without knowing each other, as is their habit, the queues of those who send.

The woman behind the glass counted the three hundred euros, and said the thing she says every time: — Ten euros commission. —

Ten euros. Ten euros on three hundred, to send the three hundred euros home: the ten per cent. The ten per cent, at twenty-six, is the tax of being far away, and the tax of being far away isn't contested: it's paid. In Joal nothing cost ten per cent: the fish was my father's, and the sea was everyone's, and nobody took the ten per cent. Here they take the ten per cent every month, and the month, in Milan, is the measure of life. I signed the receipt, and the signature, on the receipt, is the signature of those who send: the signature of those who don't choose.

I kept the receipt. It's still here, in a pocket of a notebook, and I keep it the way you keep proofs: you don't use them, you show them. The receipt said *ten euros*, and the ten euros, read twice, are the price of the sending: the price, in Milan, is the price of people, and people who send, usually, don't choose. They pay.

I did the calculation every evening, on the notebook, the way you do accounts: excessive consciousness is a sickness (Dostoevsky), and I had been sick for four years. The undeclared work gave fifty euros a day, and fifty euros a day, for twenty-five days, were one thousand two hundred and twenty-five euros a month: the undeclared work isn't contested. One thousand two hundred and twenty-five euros, and the undeclared work divided like this: the four-bed room three hundred, the food one hundred and fifty, the phone twenty, the sending three hundred and ten. Four hundred and forty-five euros were left. Four hundred and forty-five euros for a whole life, and it wasn't little: it was everything I didn't send home. The rest was sent, and the rest, at home, you don't see: you feel it.

The Sunday calls I made at six, with the time difference, at six in Milan which was five in Joal, and the time difference, on Sunday, is the thing you feel: you're together and you're not together, and the two things you can't keep. My mother answered always, and my mother didn't ask *are you well?*. It's the thing I understood late, and I understood it four years late: my mother didn't ask, because she knew. The mothers of Joal don't ask *are you well?*: they know the answer, and the answer, in Joal, isn't said: it's sent in instalments. In Milan, in the books of the girl I read, the mother asked *but at least you're well?*, and the question was the true one. Questions, in Milan, are the things you make: mothers ask. In Joal mothers don't ask: they know. It's the reversal, and the reversal, at twenty-six, is the thing you feel in your legs.

— Naka la? — my mother said, with her voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly.

— Jam rekk — I said. I'm well, and enough of that.

It was the first lie, and the first, usually, is the time you learn to say it. I didn't say it well. Lies, when they begin, you hear: you hear them the way you hear damp in walls. My mother heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked, and the lies of children are the heavy things that aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

— Fatou passed — my mother said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who says the thing she wants to say. — School. She came first. —

First. Fatou's school, and first, at home, is the first that's sent: it's my purpose, said by my mother, with the voice of someone who says heavy things lightly. My purpose, at twenty-six, was my sister's school, and the purpose, at twenty-six, makes a life: I knew it, and only I knew it. The girl in the book, in her books, had no purpose at all, and no purpose, in Milan, was freedom. I had one, and the purpose was the reversal: you leave to pay, and you pay for a purpose. It's different, and it's nothing different.

— And you? — my mother said, every Sunday, with the voice of someone who doesn't say the thing she wants to say.

— I'm well — I said.

It was the second lie, and the second was the worst: it was half true. Half-true lies, usually, are the lies you tell yourself. I knew French, and I knew wolof, and I knew the building site, and I didn't know how to say *I don't understand* without humiliating myself. I didn't say it. Heavy things aren't said on the phone: they're kept, and they're sent in instalments.

The Sunday calls lasted ten minutes, and the ten minutes, on Sunday, are the longest ten minutes in the world: they're kept. The Sunday after, for the first time, I didn't call anyone. And my mother, for the first time, called me: my mother knew the difference, and the difference, at home, you feel across thousands of kilometres.

— Amadou — my mother said, on the phone, with the voice of someone who doesn't sleep every evening for her children. — You though, at least be well, eh. —

It was the question she never asked, and she asked it that Sunday, and the question, asked that Sunday, was the heaviest thing of the month: you feel that the other knows. My mother knew it. And I said the third lie, and the third lie was made with the voice of someone who loves:

— I'm well, mamma. Inshallah. —

It was the answer to everything, and I said it too, and the answer to everything, on the phone, answers nothing.
