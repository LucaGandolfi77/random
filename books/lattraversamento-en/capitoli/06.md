# Chapter 6 — The Subjunctive

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (the association, via Padova) · **Story time:** 2024
**Words:** ~1.100 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/06.md` ✅

## Draft

I started the Italian lessons in February, at the association, so as not to be someone who does nothing: the lessons were on the first floor of a building that isn't a building, with the teacher who spoke softly. The teacher was called Caterina, she was fifty, and Italian she knew everything: she knew it the way you know the things that are needed: perfectly. The class was a room with six tables, and the six tables were six men with a purpose, and the six men spoke softly: you speak softly, as is their habit, the men who learn — you speak softly because getting it wrong out loud costs, and the cost, in the classroom, is the only thing that isn't paid.

Italian, Caterina said, doesn't have tones: it has the subjunctive. The subjunctive, in the Thai of the books of the girl I read, was the tone that didn't rise; in Italian, the subjunctive is the mood that doesn't bend: *if I were, if I had, if I could*. I repeated the subjunctive, and the subjunctive didn't bend: it stayed straight, and straight Italian isn't Italian: it's a noise.

— Again — Caterina said, and *again*, at twenty-seven, is the most humbling word in the world: I said it every week, for two years, and the weeks, at the association, began on Wednesdays.

— If I were — Caterina said, and the sentence stopped there: the sentence, in the classroom, doesn't end: it's imagined. — If you were home, what would you do? —

— I would eat — I said, once, with the subjunctive in my hand.

— You would eat what? — Caterina said, and the question was the true one.

— Thieboudienne — I said.

It was the first true thing I said at the association, and I said it with the voice of someone who doesn't complain. Caterina didn't ask what it was. Heavy things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay. Thieboudienne, at the association, was the farthest thing in the world: it was my mother's kitchen, and the kitchen, at twenty-seven, you don't describe: you eat it.

Italian, though, had the things I didn't know: names. My name, said in Italian, was another name: *Amadù*, with the rising accent. Caterina pronounced it the way my mother pronounced it, without my asking: it's the thing I understood late, and I understood it four years late. Names, said right, are the things you keep: you keep them the way you keep doors that close.

Caterina, in the third lesson, said three words that weren't Italian: — Jërëjëf — and the three words were wolof, and wolof, at the association, was the farthest thing in the world: it was my language, said by someone else, and language, said by someone else, you don't hear: you hear it later, and later, at the association, answers nothing. — Jërëjëf — Caterina said, and *jërëjëf*, said by someone else, felt like the truest thing of the lesson. The three words she had learned for us: I knew it, and only I knew it. The things you learn for others, at the association, are the things you don't forget: you keep them.

— How do you learn? — I asked her, in the fourth lesson, with the subjunctive in my hand.

— From my student — Caterina said, laughing softly. — A Senegalese boy, three years ago. He taught me *jërëjëf*, and *naka la*, and *jam rekk*. —

*Naka la*, and *jam rekk*. The three words were the three words of my Sundays: *how are you*, *I'm well, and enough of that*. The Senegalese boy, three years ago, had been at the association, or in a nearby class, and had taught the three words to the teacher, and the three words had arrived at me, three years later, the way true things arrive. All at once, and without being asked.

I did the via Padova market every Sunday, and the fish I bought from the shop downstairs. The shop downstairs was a shop of those you don't choose: you take. The fish cost double, and double was the farang price. The farang price isn't written, and you don't negotiate it with anger. I made the anger, the first time, on a Sunday: I said *double?*, and I said *why?*, and the woman at the shop laughed, and the laugh was kind, and kindness, with anger in your hand, is the thing you can't fight: you lose it. Double, explained like that, wasn't a scam anymore: it was a justice upside down. The price, on via Padova, is the price of people, and people who buy, usually, don't choose: they pay.

I cooked the thieboudienne for the first time in March, in the kitchen of the four-bed room, with frozen fish. The recipe I asked for on the phone, from my mother, and my mother said it on the phone, slowly, the way you say the things that aren't said: — Rice. Fish. Onion. Salt. — And salt, in my mother's thieboudienne, isn't measured: it's felt. I measured it, and the measured, in my mother's thieboudienne, is the measured of someone who doesn't know: you eat it. The kitchen of the four-bed room was a kitchen of four: four men, one kitchen, and the kitchen, in the evening, was the most crowded thing in the country. I cooked the thieboudienne in the evening, and the four ate it, and the four said *jërëjëf*, and *jërëjëf*, said by four, was the truest thing of the month.

Pleasure comes only when desire is already dead (Leopardi), and the thieboudienne came at the end, and it tasted of a spent desire. It was good. It was good the way good things are good: and it wasn't it. The thieboudienne I had thought about for a year was better than the thieboudienne in front of me, and the thing felt like a discovery: that the things you think about, when they arrive, arrive late. Desire dies waiting, and pleasure arrives at the dead. In Joal the thieboudienne was my mother's, and pleasure belonged to living desire, and living desire, in Joal, isn't cooked: you have it. Here is what I had come to look for, and I didn't know: not the thieboudienne at double price. The pleasure of living desire. And the pleasure of living desire, there as here, isn't bought: you have it, or you don't.

The Sunday after, for the first time, I called my mother for the recipe, and not for the sending. The recipe I said on the phone, and my mother gave it slowly, the way you say the things that aren't said: — Rice. Fish. Onion. Salt. — And I wrote, and the recipe notebook filled, and the notebook, at twenty-seven, was the truest thing of the month: it was the kitchen speaking, and the kitchen, on the phone, says nothing: it says *cook*.

— And the salt? — I asked my mother, and the salt was the last thing I didn't know.

— The salt is felt — my mother said. — Inshallah. —

It was the answer to everything, and I said it too, and the answer to everything, in the recipe notebook, answers nothing. The notebook, in the evening, was full: and the full notebook, in the evening, isn't eaten. It's kept.
