# Chapter 7 — The Room

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (the warehouse, via Padova) · **Story time:** 2025
**Words:** ~1.150 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/07.md` ✅

## Draft

The folded sheets were three. Three sheets, six months each, renewed in the queue at the Questura every six months: December, June, December. The queue is the oldest thing in the world, and sheets, in a queue, you take the way you take things: without choosing. The first sheet said *provisional*, and the second said *provisional*, and the third said *provisional*, and the three sheets, read twice, said the same thing: you are here, but you are not from here. You can be provisional in three sheets for two years, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom.

In Milan, in the books of the girl I read, the contract renewed every six months, and the renewal, in Milan, was the most anguishing thing in the world: six months of life, renewed in instalments, always halfway. The folded sheets lasted six months, and they felt even more anguishing: not six months of life. One sheet. The reversal, at twenty-seven, is that the girl's stamp said thirty days, and my sheets said six months: you're provisional in two places, and provisionality doesn't add up. It multiplies.

In March 2025 the regularisation came. The regularisation is the thing you don't ask for: you take it, when it comes, and when it comes, it comes the way true things come. All at once, and without being asked. The building site signed, and signing, on the building site, is the foreman's trade: he signs, and the foreman signs those who have hands. I had hands, and hands, at twenty-seven, are the things you see: you see them in cement.

The card arrived in May, from the office on via Padova, with my face printed on it and my name pronounced right: *residence permit for work*. The card was the first document in four years with my name said right, and the name, said right, is the thing you keep: you keep it the way you keep doors that close. I looked at it a long time, the way you look at rare things, and the card, at twenty-seven, felt like the truest thing of the month: it wasn't true. It was a card. Cards aren't true: they're the things that mark you, and pass. But the name, on the card, kept: *Amadou Fall*, said right, and the right name, at twenty-seven, was the thing you don't buy: you have it.

I found the regular work the month after, with Bakary's voice, who had the warehouse. Bakary had found a warehouse, and the warehouse was the one of those who don't choose: you take. One thousand three hundred euros a month, eight hours, and the regular work: the hours are counted, and counted hours, in Milan, are a trade. The warehouse was a trade: you scan, you carry, you answer. The scans, at the warehouse, are the scans of always: the people who pass under the scanner, and people, at the warehouse, don't pass: packages pass. Packages don't complain: they're scanned. The undeclared work isn't contested, and the regular work isn't contested: it's taken. The regular work, though, was the regular work with the contract: the contract said my name, and the name, on the contract, was said right. Things said right, at twenty-seven, are the things you keep.

I found my own room in September, on the app, with the photo and the price. The room was on via Padova, fourth floor, with the window that gives onto the inner courtyard. Four hundred and fifty euros a month. The room wasn't a bed: it was a room, and the room, at twenty-seven, is the richest thing you have: you have it and you don't share it. In Joal my father's house cost nothing, and the fish was my father's, and the sea was everyone's. Here a room cost four hundred and fifty euros, and the room was mine, and the room, in the evening, closes with the door: the door that closes, at twenty-seven, is the thing you didn't have. It felt like freedom. It wasn't freedom: it was a room. Rooms, at twenty-seven, aren't freedom: they're the things you pay for.

Omar called one evening in October, from Paris, and Omar had the escape done well: the papers, the job, the apartment with the door that closes and the door that opens. Escaping, Omar said, is done well when you have two doors: one that closes and one that opens. I had one: the one that closes. The door that opens, at twenty-seven, is the thing you didn't have: the return. Omar could go back. I couldn't: it's the thing you don't say at home, and you don't say in Milan.

— And you? — Omar said. — How is it, in Milan? —

— I'm well — I said. — I have a room. I have the job. I have the permit. —

— And do you go home? — Omar said, and the question was the true one: the question nobody asked me.

— I can't — I said. — The sheet. —

— The sheet — Omar said. — I had the sheet, once. Then I threw it away. —

Omar had the escape done well, and the escape done well, at twenty-seven, is the escape with two doors: and two doors, at twenty-seven, are the things you buy. You buy them and you don't keep them: Omar's two doors were his world. Mine was one door, and the door that closes, at twenty-seven, isn't a world: it's a room. It's the difference, and the difference, on the phone, you don't feel: you feel it later, and later, at night, answers nothing.

The new habit is an anaesthesia, it puts your days to sleep one by one (Proust), and the days put to sleep, at twenty-seven, look like a life. I thought I had arrived. I was only busy. Busy things, at twenty-seven, you don't count: you do. And the doing, at twenty-seven, felt like a healing.

It was in October, in that state of things — the card, the room, the regular work — that I had the idea. The idea was the one I shouldn't have had: and what if I stayed? You can think you stay, and the thinking, at twenty-seven, felt like freedom. It wasn't freedom: it was the reversal. Staying, in the books of the girl I read, was the dream; going, at twenty-seven, was my trade. And the trade, at twenty-seven, has a purpose: Fatou's school. And those who have a purpose, in the end, don't ask themselves if they stay. They ask themselves if the purpose is enough.

It was in October, though, also my mother's call. My mother said the thing she doesn't say every Sunday: — Your father isn't well. —

It was the heaviest thing of the evening, and the heavy thing, on the phone, isn't asked: it's listened to, and it stays.
