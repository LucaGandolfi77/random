# Chapter 10 — The Phone Held Up

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (via Padova, the warehouse) · **Story time:** November–December 2026
**Words:** ~1.150 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/10.md` ✅

## Draft

> "In place of death there was light."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*

On Friday, at noon in Milan which was one in the afternoon in Joal, the phone rang. It was Moussa, and Moussa had the voice of someone who doesn't sleep every evening for his friends, and the voice, on the phone, you don't see: you feel it.

— Amadou — he said. — I'm holding the phone for you. —

And he held the phone up. The funeral had begun, and the funeral, in Joal, is done with the village: it's done with hands, with the sea, with voice. Moussa held the phone up, and the phone held up, at twenty-nine, is the truest thing in the world: you hear everything, and you see nothing. I heard the chants, and the chants, on the phone, are the chants you don't follow: you listen. I heard the sea, and the sea, on the phone, is the sea that erases itself: you feel it. I heard the people, and the people, on the phone, are the people you don't know: you feel them.

The phone held up lasted ten minutes, and the ten minutes, at twenty-nine, are the longest ten minutes in the world: they're kept. I said nothing. Heavy things aren't said: they're listened to, and they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the drawer, the way you keep the things that aren't needed. And at the bottom of the drawer, that Friday, there was everything: the pirogue sold, the medicine not taken, the ticket not bought, the funeral not attended.

— In place of death there was light — the books say (Tolstoy), and the sentence, on the phone held up, felt like the truest thing: in the tenth minute, among the chants, there was a laugh — one, softly, as is their habit, laughs at funerals: you can't, they last too long. It was Moussa's child, and Moussa's child, at funerals, laughs the way things laugh: for real. In place of death there was light, and the light, on the phone held up, was the laugh of a child I didn't know.

Moussa's hands, that Friday, I felt for the first time: they were the hands of someone who gives, and the hands of someone who gives, on the phone held up, are the hands that don't stop. Moussa held the phone up for ten minutes, and Moussa's ten minutes were the ten minutes of a man who doesn't ask anything: he gives. The hands of those who give, at twenty-nine, are the hands you don't buy: you feel them. Bakary, at the warehouse, in the evening, said the thing the books say: — The hands of those who give, in books, are the hands that don't stop. And the hands that don't stop, in the end, are the hands that keep. — And he was right, and I didn't know it: Moussa's hands, on the phone held up, were the hands that keep: they keep and they aren't bought.

On Tuesday I did the math, on the notebook, with the pencil. Excessive consciousness is a sickness (Dostoevsky), and I had been sick for four years: I did the math for the last time, and the last time, at twenty-nine, is the time the numbers say everything. Three hundred euros a month, for thirty-six months: ten thousand eight hundred euros. Ten thousand eight hundred euros sent home in three years, and the ten thousand eight hundred euros, at twenty-nine, were Fatou's school: they were my purpose, said in numbers. The medicine was fifty euros a month, and the fifty euros, for two years, were one thousand two hundred euros: one thousand two hundred euros, at twenty-nine, were the medicine my father didn't take, and the medicine my father didn't take, on the notebook, was the line that wasn't there. The ticket was seven hundred euros, and the seven hundred euros, added, were the funeral: and the funeral, on the notebook, was the line that wasn't there.

The math, at twenty-nine, said one thing only: that I had paid everything — the rent, the language, the sendings — except the two things that counted: the medicine, the funeral. The price, at twenty-nine, is the price of people, and people who send, usually, pay everything except what counts. You pay the ten per cent, and the ten per cent is paid: the medicine and the funeral aren't paid, and the two lines that weren't there, on the notebook, were the two things of my life.

The thread of the lies stopped. For four years I had said *I'm well* to my mother, every Sunday, and the lies, on the notebook, stop: they stop like my father's heart, and my father's heart, at twenty-nine, stopped when it stopped. The truth, on the notebook, was one: I wasn't well. And the truth, said for the first time, on the notebook, at twenty-nine, felt like the heaviest thing in the world. The weight, though, was mine: it wasn't the four-bed room, it wasn't the undeclared work, it wasn't the ten per cent. It was mine, and my weight, in the evening, you don't take off: you keep it. I still keep it.

In the evening, coming back to via Padova, I looked at the fog. The fog of Milan, at twenty-nine, doesn't come back: it stays. In Joal the sea erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back: it's the law of the sea. The fog isn't the sea, and December isn't the evening, and the question, at twenty-nine, was the question that isn't said: it's made.

Was it better to stay? I asked the question to myself, in the evening, with the fog at the window, and the question, in the evening, answers nothing. The answer, in books, is there: the hedge, the work, the purpose. I had the purpose, and the purpose makes you live; the question is made by death, and death, at twenty-nine, doesn't make you live: it makes the question.

On Tuesday after, at the Questura, I took the card: the renewal had arrived. The renewal, at twenty-nine, was the usual card, with the printed face and the name pronounced right, and the card, at twenty-nine, was the door that opened. The permit was in my hand. And the permit, at twenty-nine, was the door that opened: the door that opened, at twenty-nine, was the return.
