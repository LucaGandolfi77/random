# Chapter 11 — The Ticket

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (via Padova, the sending office) · **Story time:** December 2026 – January 2027

## Draft

The permit was in my hand, and the permit in your hand allows you to travel: it's the rule, and the rule, at twenty-nine, is the thing you don't negotiate with anger. I didn't make the anger. The anger, at twenty-nine, I had made in November, and November's anger was the anger of someone who doesn't have the door that opens. The card, in December, was the door that opened, and the door that opened, at twenty-nine, was the return.

I bought the ticket on Saturday, from the office on via Padova, with the seven hundred euros in my hand. Seven hundred euros were the margin: the margin I had kept for the ticket, and the ticket was the return, and the return was the purpose. My father didn't take the medicine: the sea is paid for first. The ticket, at twenty-nine, was the margin spent on the return, and the spent margin, on the notebook, said one thing only: that the return, at twenty-nine, cost fourteen months of medicine. I knew it. Heavy things aren't said: they're bought.

The ticket said *Milan–Dakar, return, one month*. Return: the two doors, at twenty-nine, were the ticket. Omar had had the two doors for years, and I had had one for four years; and the ticket, at twenty-nine, was the second door: the door that opened. It felt like the truest thing of the month. It wasn't true: it was a ticket. Tickets, at twenty-nine, aren't true: they're the things you buy, and the things you buy you don't keep. I keep the ticket. It's still here, in a pocket of a notebook, and I keep it the way you keep proofs: you don't use them, you show them.

I called my mother on Sunday, at six in Milan which was five in Joal.

— I'm coming home — I said.

My mother didn't ask why. True things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay. She said: — All right. Come. — And the *come*, on the phone, at six in Milan, was the heaviest thing in the world: it was the house speaking, and the house, on the phone, says nothing: it says *come*, and *come*, in the morning, you don't take off: you keep it.

— Your room is ready — my mother said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who says heavy things lightly. — Moussa keeps it. Your room. —

Your room is ready. My father's house, of bricks and tin, that cost nothing, and that cost everything else: the house was ready, and the house, at twenty-nine, was the thing you don't buy: you have it, or you don't, and you have it with the things you keep, and the things I kept, at twenty-nine, were one: the room Moussa kept. The room of a boy who left four years ago, kept by a friend who stayed: home, in Joal, isn't bought. It's kept.

The waiting lasted a month, and the month was the month of the ticket, and the weeks of the month all aimed: at the ticket, at the airport, at the sea. The weeks that aim, at twenty-nine, are Leopardi's weeks: happiness was in the waiting (Leopardi, *The Village Saturday*), and the waiting, at twenty-nine, was the happiness. In Milan, in the books of the girl I read, happiness was in the waiting for Saturday; in Joal, happiness was in the waiting for the return. The waiting, at twenty-nine, isn't contested: it's lived.

The sending, that month, I didn't do: the seven hundred euros were the margin, and the margin, spent on the ticket, wasn't there. The receipt of the month I didn't keep: there wasn't one. It was the first time in three years there wasn't the receipt, and the not being there, at twenty-nine, is the thing you feel: you feel it the way you feel damp in walls. My mother knew it, and my mother didn't ask: heavy things aren't asked.

Was it better to stay? I had asked the question in November, and I asked it still, in the evening, with the fog at the window. The question, in the evening, answers nothing. And the not answering, at twenty-nine, is the thing you carry: you carry it the way you carry heavy things.

On the morning of the flight, in January, at the airport, there was the fog. The fog of Milan, at twenty-nine, doesn't come back: it stays. The flight left at seven, and the fog stayed below, and the fog, from the window, was the thing I didn't know I would miss. The plane's door opened, and the door that opens, at twenty-nine, is the return.
