# Chapter 12 — The Return

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan → Dakar → Joal · **Story time:** February 2027, one month
**Words:** ~1.150 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/12.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "…and shipwreck is sweet to me in this sea."
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *L'infinito*

The flight lasted six hours, and the six hours were the slowest hours in the world, and the slow hours, at twenty-nine, are the hours you keep. The descent onto Dakar I saw from the window, and the descent, in the morning, was the descent into the sun. The sun of Dakar I didn't know anymore: I knew it the way you know the things you don't have: perfectly. The things you don't have, in the morning, are the things you see: and the sun, in the morning, was seen.

Dakar arrived in the morning, and Dakar is the city you don't choose: you cross it. The bus to Joal left at nine, and it took five hours, and the bus was the usual one: with the seats that don't recline, the air that isn't there, and the people who sleep the way things sleep: for real. I slept too, an hour, with my head on the window, and the window, in the morning, was warm: the heat, in a country of sun, is the heat you don't choose: you have it. The bus went to the sea, and the sea, at the end of the road, was seen: I felt it. The sea, in Senegal, is felt before it's seen: it's the law of the sea, and I learned the law from my father.

Joal arrived at noon, and Joal is the village you don't choose: you have it. The village, though, wasn't the one I had left. The boys weren't there anymore: the village of the old and the children, and the old, at noon, sat at the doors, and the children, at noon, ran, and the boys, at noon, weren't there: they were there, in Milan, in Paris, in Madrid. The village had emptied like the sea, and the empty village, at noon, is the heaviest village in the world: emptiness weighs. The sea, at noon, was seen, and the sea had emptied: the big boats over there, on the horizon, and no pirogue. The pirogues, at noon, were on the beach: turned, empty, without the sea.

The house was the usual one: of bricks and tin, with the street that gave onto the sea. My mother was at the door, with the face that doesn't cry, and the embrace, at noon, is the thing you don't buy: you have it. My mother's embrace lasted a minute, or a life; at the end it ended, and only the tiredness stayed. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept.

— Your room — my mother said, with her voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly. — Moussa keeps it. —

The room was the usual one: the bed, the recipe notebook on the table, the window that gives onto the sea. The room of a boy who left four years ago, kept by a friend who stayed: home, in Joal, isn't bought. It's kept. The recipe notebook was full, and the full notebook, at twenty-nine, was the truest thing in the village: it was the kitchen speaking, and the kitchen, on the phone, says nothing: it says *cook*.

Moussa arrived in the evening, with the child. Moussa was thirty, had the wife, a child, and the child was the one who had laughed on the phone, at the funerals, in the tenth minute: the laugh that in place of death there was light. Moussa spoke of the sea with the voice of someone who has long stopped explaining: — There's no fish — he said. — The big boats took it. I have to cross too. —

— And if you stay? — I asked, and the question was mine: it was the question I asked myself, in the evening, in Milan, and I asked it to Moussa, in the evening, in the village.

— And if I stay? — Moussa said. — There's no fish. My family doesn't eat. —

It was the answer of his life, and the answer, at twenty-nine, isn't contested: it's crossed. I left for Fatou's school, and Moussa leaves for his family: the two departures are the same departure, and the departure, at twenty-nine, isn't contested. The reversal, at twenty-nine, is that those who cross cross for those who stay: and those who stay, at twenty-nine, don't stop. Moussa's child, in the evening, laughed, and the laugh was the laugh that in place of death there was light: and the light, at twenty-nine, isn't bought: you receive it.

My father's grave I saw on Sunday, with the sea. The grave was of bricks, and the grave gave onto the sea, and the sea, on Sunday, erased itself and came back: it's the law of the sea. The grave looked at the sea that isn't there anymore: the empty sea, the big boats over there, on the horizon, and the fish that isn't there. My father had thirty years of sea, and the sea, at twenty-nine, is the place you sell: you sell it and you don't take it back. The grave looked at the sea my father had sold, and the grave, at twenty-nine, is the heaviest thing in the village: you look at it, and you don't touch it. I sat next to it, and sitting next to a grave, on Sunday, is the sitting of things: for real. I said nothing. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the grave, the way you keep the things that aren't needed.

I cooked the thieboudienne on Sunday, in the kitchen of the house, with the fish Moussa had taken in the morning. The fish, in Joal, isn't bought: it's fished, and fishing, at twenty-nine, is my father's trade, and my father isn't there anymore. But the fish, that Sunday, was there: Moussa had taken it, and the fish, at twenty-nine, is the gift you don't buy: you receive it. I cooked the thieboudienne with the recipe notebook open, and the notebook said *rice, fish, onion, salt*, and the salt, which isn't measured: it's felt. The Sunday thieboudienne, at twenty-nine, had the taste of pleasure: pleasure, at twenty-nine, comes to living desire, and living desire, at twenty-nine, isn't cooked: you have it. And I had it. My mother ate it, and my mother said nothing: heavy things aren't said. But the unsaid, at twenty-nine, was the truest thing of the Sunday: my father didn't eat it. The not eating, at twenty-nine, is the thing that isn't said: it's kept.

On Sunday evening, at the sea, I asked the question. I asked it to myself, in the evening, with the sea at the window, and the question, in the evening, answers nothing.

Was it better to stay?

The answer, in books, is there: the answer is the hedge, the work, the purpose. I had the purpose: Fatou's school, the ten thousand eight hundred euros, the room on via Padova. The purpose makes you live. But the question, at twenty-nine, doesn't answer the purpose: the question is made by death, and death, at twenty-nine, makes the question that doesn't close. The village wasn't the one I had left: and I wasn't the one the village had left. Shipwreck, there, was sweet (Leopardi, *L'infinito*), and the sweet, at twenty-nine, isn't said: it's kept.

The morning after, the return flight left at seven. The return flight is the flight you don't choose: you take it. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and you cross with them.
