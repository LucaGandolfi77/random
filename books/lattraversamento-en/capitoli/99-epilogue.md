# Epilogue — The Question

**POV:** Amadou · **Place:** Milan (via Padova) · **Story time:** spring 2027 (May)
**Words:** ~1.000 / translation of `lattraversamento/capitoli/99-epilogo.md` ✅

---

## Draft

I am in Milan, and Milan, in May, has the sun. The sun of Milan I didn't know: I knew it the way you know the things you don't have. The things you don't have, in the morning, are the things you see: and the sun, in the morning, was seen. In Joal the sun set at seven, and the sea erased itself, and the sea came back: it's the law of the sea. I learned the law of the sea from my father, and my father isn't there anymore, and the law stays: it's kept.

The things you keep, at twenty-nine, are the things you don't buy: the recipe notebook, the receipts of the ten per cent, the card of the permit, the Sunday calls. I keep them on the shelf, and the shelf, at twenty-nine, is the thing I didn't have: you have it, or you don't, and you have it with the things you keep, and the things I keep, at twenty-nine, are the truest things.

The Sunday call I make at six, with the time difference, at six in Milan which is five in Joal. My mother answers, and my mother doesn't ask *are you well?*: the mothers of Joal don't ask, because they know. The answer, at twenty-nine, isn't said: it's sent in instalments. Fatou answers, and Fatou's school is my purpose: Fatou, on the phone, talks of school with the voice of someone who says heavy things lightly, and school, at twenty-nine, is the thing that's paid: it's paid and you don't see it. School, on the phone, is felt: you feel it the way you feel damp in walls.

Moussa doesn't answer anymore, on Sunday: Moussa's child answers, who is two, and Moussa's child, on the phone, laughs the way things laugh: for real. In place of death there was light (Tolstoy), and the light, on the phone, is the laugh of a child I don't know: I know him, and I know him by the laugh. Moussa, on Sunday, still says the thing he said: — I have to cross too. — And the reversal, at twenty-nine, is that those who cross cross for those who stay: and those who stay, at twenty-nine, don't stop. I couldn't stop him: the sea is the only road, and the road, in Joal, isn't contested. But the sea, now, I have crossed in two directions, and the two directions don't add up: they're kept.

The work I still do, at the warehouse: the scans, the eight hours, the regular work. The scans, at twenty-nine, are the scans of someone who has nothing to say: they're done. The regular work, at twenty-nine, is the regular work of those who don't choose: it's taken. But the regular work, at twenty-nine, has a why: Fatou's school, the three hundred euros of the ten per cent, the room on via Padova. The why, at twenty-nine, is the thing that holds: it holds the way doors that close hold. In the books of the girl I read, work had a purpose, and the purpose makes you live. I have one, and the purpose, at twenty-nine, makes you live: and living, at twenty-nine, doesn't answer the question.

Was it better to stay home?

The question I asked in Joal, on Sunday evening, with the sea at the window, and I ask it still, every evening, in Milan, with the window that gives onto the inner courtyard. The answer, in books, is there: the answer is the hedge, the work, the purpose. And I have the purpose, and the purpose makes you live. But the question, at twenty-nine, doesn't answer the purpose: the question is made by death, and death, at twenty-nine, makes the question that doesn't close.

The village wasn't the one I had left: and I wasn't the one the village had left. The sea, in Joal, erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back: it's the law of the sea. The law I learned from my father, and my father isn't there anymore, and the law stays. Shipwreck, there, was sweet (Leopardi, *L'infinito*), and the sweet, at twenty-nine, isn't said: it's kept.

Was it better to stay? I don't know. I ask it still, every evening, and the evening doesn't answer. Maybe yes. Maybe staying was the dream of those who couldn't leave, and leaving is my trade, and the trade has a purpose: Fatou's school. And those who have a purpose, in the end — I write it, and I write it for the first time — are well, or almost. The question stays. And the question, at twenty-nine, is the only thing that isn't bought: it's made.

My father said *inshallah*, and *inshallah* was the answer to everything. The answer to everything, at twenty-nine, doesn't answer the question: the answer to everything answers the future, and the future, at twenty-nine, isn't known. It's crossed.

I am writing a book, in the evening, at the desk of the room, with the recipe notebook next to it. I write it for the first time, and the first time, at twenty-nine, is the truest thing: I didn't know it. The book is called *The Crossing*, and the book I write for the question: questions, in the evening, are the things you keep.

And the things you keep, in the evening, aren't bought. They're kept.
