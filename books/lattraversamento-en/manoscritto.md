*To those who cross, and to those who stay.*


# Prologue — The Folded Sheet


> "Man is a being that gets used to everything."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Memories from the House of the Dead*

The Questura office in Milan opens at half past eight, and by nine the queue already reaches the gate. You stand in line with the folded sheet in your hand, the way you hold a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of your own life, and that the numbers are never good. The sheet is folded in four, and it has been folded so long that it opens by itself: it is the wisest thing they have given me in four years. A piece of paper with my face printed on it, my name pronounced wrong, and the writing: *provisional residence permit*. Provisional. After four years, provisional.

In front of me was a man who had come from Bangladesh, and he spoke of the renewal with the voice of someone who has long stopped explaining. Behind, a woman who had come from Ecuador, with the child sleeping in her arms, and the child, in the queue, slept the way things sleep: for real. I spoke to nobody. I held the sheet and I looked at the numbers on the display: B047, B048, B049. Mine was B052, and numbers, in a queue, all go up like rents.

My fingerprints were taken at Lampedusa, the first day, and fingerprints don't get erased: it's their law. Fingerprints don't say the days: they say the place. The stamp, in the books of a girl I read, said thirty days; the fingerprints say: here. And here, after four years, is the place that doesn't get erased. In Joal I had a sea that erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back: it's the law of the sea. Fingerprints are the opposite of the sea: they don't come back, and they don't get erased. They keep.

I came here because I had to. Not because I wanted to. I used to say it to everyone, the first months, with the voice of someone who doesn't complain: the father who sells the pirogue, the fish that isn't there, the sister who goes to school. I never said it like that: I said it with the calculation, at home, on the phone: *I will send three hundred euros a month*. And the calculation said a thing I hadn't said to anyone, not even to myself, in four years: that there, in Joal, there wasn't anything left to have. The sea had emptied, and the building sites of Dakar gave fifty euros a day, and fifty euros a day are the fish that isn't there: you earn them and they aren't enough.

Because the calculation counted everything, except the main thing: that here, in Milan, I pay double for everything. The bed in the four-bed room at three hundred euros — a bed, not a room —, the ten per cent on every sending home, the language that doesn't bend, the farang price at the shop downstairs. In Joal my father's house cost nothing, and the fish was my father's, and the sea was everyone's. Here nothing is everyone's, and everything costs double, and I have paid double for four years, and I can't go back. You can pay double for years, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom: the girl in the book ran away so she would stop overpaying. I came to pay. I can't go back: it's the thing you don't say at home, and you don't say in Milan.

The number was called: B052, counter nine. The man behind the glass took the sheet, looked at the fingerprints, and asked me the purpose of my stay. The purpose of my stay. I stood there, holding the question, and I had already answered it four years before, in a camp at Lampedusa, to a man with a pencil: work. It was the right word and it wasn't the truth: after four years I wasn't someone who arrives anymore, and I wasn't someone who stays, and I wasn't anything that has a name on a form.

I went out, and outside there was the fog, the fog of Milan, the fog I didn't know before and that now I know better than the sun. In Joal the sun set at seven, and the sea erased itself, and the sea came back. The fog, in Milan, doesn't come back: it stays.

This is the story of four years, and of how a man from Joal crosses the sea and pays double for everything — the bed, the language, the sendings — and can't go back; and of how, in the end, coming back, he asks himself the question nobody answers.

I begin at the beginning, which was Joal, and it cost nothing: and it cost everything else.

# Chapter 1 — The Pirogue


> "All happy families are alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina*

In Joal the sea erased itself every evening, and every evening it came back. It's the law of the sea, and I learned the law from my father, who had learned it from his father: the sea goes and the sea comes back, and those who live with the sea never really leave. I lived in a house that cost nothing — my father's house, of bricks and tin, with the street that gave onto the sea — and the sea was everyone's, and the fish was my father's, and the house was ours. You can be poor in everything, and not poor in home: it was my wealth, and I didn't know it.

The families of Joal, in the evening, were all alike: the mother who cooks, the father who comes back with the sea, the children who run until night. Happy families are all alike (Tolstoy), and ours resembled those: it was happy in the way of families that have the sea, and the sea was the only thing that wasn't counted. The Sunday thieboudienne, in the evening, was the truest thing: my mother made it with my father's fish, and my father's fish, on Sunday, was the gift you don't buy: you receive it. Pleasure, in Joal, belonged to living desire, and living desire, in Joal, isn't bought: you have it.

My father was called Ousmane, and he had thirty years of sea. Thirty years on the same pirogue, painted blue, that was called Fatou — my sister's name, given to the boat the day she was born. My father spoke of the sea with the voice of someone who has worked his whole life in one thing, and the one thing was the sea: the sea isn't a trade. The sea is a place, and the trade is the fish, and for thirty years there was fish. My father, in the morning, went out with the sea at five, and five in the morning, in Joal, is the five of those who have the sea: you go out and you ask nothing. My father's hands, in the evening, came back full, or empty: and empty hands, in the evening, are the hands that aren't said: they're kept.

Then the sea emptied. The big boats arrived from over there, on the horizon, and the big boats took everyone's fish, and everyone's fish is nobody's fish. Nature, they said at school, promises: and nature doesn't deliver what it promises then (Leopardi, *A Silvia*), and the sea that promised fish didn't give it anymore. The fishermen of Joal began to come back with empty pirogues, and empty pirogues, in the evening, are the heaviest pirogues in the world: emptiness weighs. My father came back, and said nothing. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're weighed in the evening, and the weight, in the evening, you don't feel: you notice later. It was the thing I understood late, and I understood it four years late: the weight of empty pirogues you don't feel in the evening. You feel it years later, in another country, in a queue.

I worked, those years, on the building sites of Dakar. The building sites of Dakar are a trade: you carry the cement, you mix, you tie the iron. Fifty euros a day, ten hours, and the undeclared work: the hours aren't counted, they're finished. I was, though, someone who had studied — I had done secondary school in Joal, and I knew French well, and French, on building sites, isn't needed: you know it perfectly. The things you study, when they aren't needed, you know perfectly: it's their secret. French wasn't needed. Wolof was spoken, and wolof is home. Italian I didn't even know existed.

In two years I put aside fifteen hundred euros. Fifteen hundred euros put aside in two years, living the reduced version: the shared room in Dakar, the market shopping, presents made with reason. Fifteen hundred euros: it was everything I had, and it wasn't little: it was half of nothing. The crossing cost three thousand euros. Three thousand a head, taken from the guy who fixed things — the fixer, and the fixer doesn't have a name: he has a price. Three thousand euros for a place on a boat you don't choose, and you don't choose the boat because the boats you choose don't leave at night.

Moussa stayed. Moussa had been my friend since we were six, and he stayed: he had taken his father's pirogue, the way you take things that stay, and his life was ours: the sea, the fish, the house. His life aimed: at the wedding, at the children, at the fish. Moussa had a purpose, and a purpose, in Joal, makes a life.

Omar had left two years before me, for Paris. Omar had the escape done well: he had the papers, a job, an apartment with a door that closes. Escaping, he said on the phone, is done well when you have a door that closes and a door that opens: two doors. I had neither. I had three thousand euros and a boat at night.

I made the decision because I had to. Not because I wanted to: I didn't say it to myself with these words. I said it with the phone, to my mother: *Fatou's school*, and Fatou's school was my purpose. My sister was eleven, and went to school, and school was paid, and the fish wasn't there, and the building sites weren't enough. The reversal, at twenty-six, is that those who leave leave for a purpose: not for freedom. Freedom, in the books of the girl I read, was the opposite: you leave so you stop paying. I left to pay. It's different, and it's nothing different.

My father sold the pirogue. The blue pirogue, the one called Fatou, thirty years of sea, sold for fifteen hundred euros: the other half of the crossing. The sea paid with the sea, and the sea, in Joal, is the biggest thing you have: you sell it and you don't take it back. My father sold it without my asking. Heavy things aren't asked.

— You'll come back — my father said, in the evening, with his voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly. — Inshallah. —

Inshallah. God willing. The answer to everything. I didn't yet know it was the answer to everything, and that the answer to everything answers nothing.

The morning after I left. Heavy things aren't said. They're kept, and you cross with them.

# Chapter 2 — The Desert


> "O nature, o nature, / why do you not deliver / what you promise then? why do you deceive / your children so?"
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *A Silvia*

I left on a Friday in June, from Dakar, and in Dakar there was my mother, at the station, with the shopping package and the face that doesn't cry. My father wasn't there: he was in Joal, with the sea, and the sea, in the morning, doesn't let go: you look at the things that let go, and my father didn't look at them. Heavy things aren't said. My mother gave me the package, and in the package there was thieboudienne, and my mother's thieboudienne tasted of those things you don't describe: you eat them. Fatou held my hand until the bus, and the hand, at the station, is the hand you don't buy: you keep it. I ate it on the bus, for three days, and for three days the bus was the world.

The bus went north, and the route was the one you don't choose: Mali, then Niger, then Libya. The route, in books, isn't chosen: it's taken. The bus was full of people like me: people with a package, people with a purpose, people who don't complain. The reason, in the morning, you feel in your legs: you don't feel it. You notice later, and later is made of borders.

The desert arrived on the fifth day, and the desert is the thing you don't know until you live it: the sun, the sand, the wind that takes the sand and puts it everywhere. Nature, in the desert, doesn't deliver what it promises then (Leopardi, *A Silvia*): the sun promises the day and gives the heat, and the heat, in the desert, is a wall. In Joal the heat was a wall that hugged you: it was the heat of the sea. There it was a wall that jostled you: the heat of the sand. The difference, in the desert, I felt. At night, instead, the desert turned: the heat went and the cold came, and the cold, in a country of sun, is the cold you don't expect: you put up with it. I slept with the sack, and the sack, at night, was the truest thing: you keep it.

In the truck there was water, and the water was paid for. Not double: triple. The bottle of water, in the truck, cost triple what it cost in Dakar, and triple, in the desert, isn't discussed: it's paid. The price, in the desert, is the price of those who pass: those who pass don't choose. I paid it, and I paid it without saying anything: the things you pay for in the desert, usually, aren't said. They're kept.

The borders cost. The border of Mali, the border of Niger, the border of Libya: three borders, and the three borders were paid for, and the price wasn't written, and you don't negotiate the price with anger. I made the anger, the first time, at the border of Niger: I said *how much?*, and I said *why?*, and the man at the border didn't laugh, and the not laughing, at the border, is the heaviest thing: you feel that the other knows, and the other doesn't say. In Joal heavy things aren't said: they're kept. At borders heavy things are paid, and the price, at the border, is the price of those who don't have a door that closes.

At the border of Niger, besides, there was the thing I don't tell anyone. A boy, in the truck, didn't leave again. I didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the truck, the way you keep the things that aren't needed. The truck left again, and the truck, in the morning, was the heaviest truck in the world.

Tripoli arrived on the fifteenth day, and Tripoli is the city you don't choose: with the walls, the streets, the dormitory. The dormitory was a room with twenty beds, and the twenty beds were twenty men with a package and a purpose, and nobody spoke: you speak softly, as is their habit, the men who wait — you speak softly because shouting tires, and tiredness, in the dormitory, is the only thing that isn't bought. There was the usual noise: the voices, the steps, the hum of the fan, and every so often a prayer — one, softly, as is their habit, prayers: they're said softly because God hears, and God, in the dormitory, hears everything.

I waited for the boat. The boat isn't waited for: it's awaited. The weeks in the dormitory were the longest weeks in the world, and the long weeks, in Tripoli, are the weeks that don't end: you cross them. The fixer came every evening, and every evening he said *tomorrow*, and *tomorrow*, in Tripoli, is the word you don't believe: you say.

I made the fear, the second week, at night, in the dormitory. The heart left as if it had to give me bad news: three extra beats a second, the sky that came close, the hands that froze. It lasted four minutes, or a life; at the end it ended, and only the tiredness stayed. I didn't say it to anyone. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're kept at the bottom of the drawer, the way you keep the things that aren't needed.

In the evening, coming back from the dormitory, the fixer said the thing he said every evening: — Tomorrow. —

And I said the word I had learned from my father, with his voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly:

— Inshallah. —

It was the answer to everything, and I said it the way my father said it: without knowing it was the answer to everything. The answer to everything, in Tripoli, answers nothing. The boat came the evening after, and the boat was the last thing I didn't know. The sea, at night, you see: you don't look at it.

# Chapter 3 — The Dinghy


> "the only true paradise is always the paradise that we have lost."
>
> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

The boat was a dinghy, and the dinghy was full: a hundred and twenty people, a package each, and the sea. A hundred and twenty people on a dinghy you don't choose: you take. The dinghy left at night, from the beach of Tripoli, and the beach, at night, is the beach you don't see: you feel it. I felt the sand under my feet, and sand, at night, is sand you don't look at: you cross it. A hundred and twenty people, and the dinghy wasn't made for a hundred and twenty: it was made for forty. The dinghy, at night, was the heaviest dinghy in the world: emptiness doesn't weigh. People weigh.

On the dinghy there was Bakary. Bakary was twenty-six, like me, and came from Mali, and Mali, he said, was his country: he had a package, a purpose, and the face of someone who doesn't complain. We sat close, because neighbours, on a dinghy, you choose: the dinghy was full, but neighbours you choose. Bakary had a biscuit, and the biscuit he divided in two, and the half he gave me was half of nothing: it was everything. A biscuit divided in two, at night, in the middle of the sea, is the truest thing in the world: you eat it, and you say *thank you*, and *thank you*, in wolof, is *jërëjëf*, and I said it.

— And you, what are you going there for? — Bakary said, the first night, with his voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly.

— Work — I said. — In Milan. —

— And at home? — Bakary said.

— My sister's school — I said.

It was the truest thing I said in three days, and I said it with the voice of someone who doesn't complain. Bakary didn't ask anything. Heavy things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay. Bakary had a purpose, and a purpose, on a dinghy, makes a life: his was his mother, and mine was Fatou, and the mother and the sister were the only thing that doesn't get scanned: it's kept.

The sea lasted three days, and the three days were the longest days in the world. The sea, in the daytime, you don't look at: you cross it. At night, the sea you see, and the sea at night is the infinite: the unending spaces, the superhuman silences (Leopardi, *L'infinito*). In Joal the sea was my home, and home you don't look at: you live it. There the sea wasn't home: it was the road, and the road, in the middle of the sea, is the infinite. And the infinite, without a hedge, doesn't free: it frightens. I had the infinite, and I didn't have the hedge, and the hedge, in the middle of the sea, was the dinghy: the dinghy was the measure that made the infinite possible. Shipwreck, there, was sweet (Leopardi, *L'infinito*), and the sweet, in the middle of the sea, is the thing you don't say: you keep it.

The first night I didn't sleep. The dinghy rose and fell, and the rising and the falling, at night, is the movement you don't follow: you put up with it. The water wet my feet, and water, at night, is cold: the cold, on a dinghy, is the cold you don't expect: you put up with it, the way you put up with the sea. Bakary slept, and sleeping, at night, in the middle of the sea, is the sleeping of things: for real. I didn't sleep. I looked at the sky, and the sky, in the middle of the sea, is full of stars: stars, at night, are the things you don't count. You look at them. And I looked at them, and I looked at all of them, and none answered: stars, at night, don't answer. The sky, in the middle of the sea, is the highest sky in the world, and the high sky you don't look at if there's nothing underneath: the sky, in the middle of the sea, you don't look at: you cross it.

The second night there was the fear. The wind left as if it had to give us bad news: the dinghy that went down, the water that rose, the people who screamed. The water rose to the knees, and water to the knees, at night, in the middle of the sea, is the thing you don't imagine: you put up with it. It lasted an hour, or a life; at the end it ended, and only the tiredness stayed. Bakary held my arm, and the arm, at night, in the middle of the sea, is the truest thing: you keep it. I said nothing. Heavy things aren't said: they're kept, and they're kept in the middle of the sea, the way you keep the things that aren't needed.

— It's normal — Bakary said, when it ended, with his voice. — The sea, at night, does that. —

It was the first time someone said *it's normal* to me, and *it's normal*, in the middle of the sea, is the truest thing you can say: you feel that the other knows. Bakary knew it. The sea, at night, does that: the hundred and twenty knew it, and the hundred and twenty didn't say it: they kept it.

The arrival was the third day, in the morning, and Lampedusa arrived the way true things arrive: all at once, and without being asked. The island was small, and the island, in the morning, is the most beautiful island in the world: the light, the houses, the sea that erased itself. The coast guard took us, and the dinghy stayed, and the dinghy, in the morning, is the thing you don't keep: you let it go. I let the dinghy go, and the dinghy had the face of someone who stays: emptiness weighs.

The hotspot was a queue, and the queue is the oldest thing in the world: it was there before borders, and it will be after. I stood in line with the package in my hand, and the package was my mother's, and my mother's package, in a queue, is the truest thing: you keep it. The queue lasted an hour, or a life; at the end I arrived at the desk, and at the desk there was a man with a pencil.

— Name — the man said, and the word was right and it wasn't the truth: my name, said by someone else, was another name.

— Amadou — I said.

The man wrote, and the writing, in Italian, was the first thing I saw in Italian: the letters of a name pronounced wrong. Then the man asked me for the fingerprints, and fingerprints are the thing that doesn't get erased: it's their law. Fingerprints don't say the days: they say the place. The stamp, in the books of the girl I read, said thirty days; the fingerprints say: here. And here, at Lampedusa, is the place that doesn't get erased.

— Purpose — the man said, with the pencil in his hand.

— Work — I said.

It was the right word and it wasn't the truth. After Lampedusa I wasn't someone who works anymore: I was someone who arrives, and someone who arrives, in books, doesn't have a name on a form. The man wrote, and the writing said my name, and my name, written in Italian, was the truest thing on the island: it was my name. The name, at Lampedusa, you keep.

In the evening, inside the hotspot, I smelled the smell. The smell was of the sea, and of salt water, and of the clay of Joal's bricks, and the smell came the way true things come: all at once, and without being asked. The past doesn't call: it arrives, by smell (Proust), and the smell took me to Joal, to the house that cost nothing, to my father with the sea, to the pirogue that wasn't there anymore. Two days before I had crossed the sea, and the sea was my home, and home got lost in the middle of the sea. Paradise, the books say, is the paradise that we have lost (Proust). I had crossed paradise, and I was losing everything else: the language, the sea, my people, the meaning.

This is the thing nobody says about the crossing: that you cross paradise to arrive at the rest, and the rest, at night, is the hotspot: the queue, the fingerprints, the wire.

The morning after the questions began. The questions, at the hotspot, aren't answered: they're kept.

# Chapter 4 — The Four-Bed Room


From Lampedusa I was taken to Sicily, and in Sicily there was a reception centre, and the reception centre was a queue with beds. The queue is the oldest thing in the world, and beds, in a queue, you take the way you take things: without choosing. I stayed in Sicily two months, and the two months were the longest months in the world, and the long weeks, in Sicily, are the weeks that don't end: you cross them. Then I was taken to Milan, and Milan arrived in the morning, with the fog.

The fog of Milan I didn't know. In Joal the sun set at seven, and the sea erased itself, and the sea came back; the fog doesn't come back: it stays. I walked through the city with the sheet in my hand, and the sheet was the usual one: the printed face, the name pronounced wrong. Milan is a city you don't see in the morning: you cross it. People, in the morning, walked without looking, and people who walk without looking, in Milan, walk the way things walk: for real. I walked with the sheet, and the sheet, in my hand, was the thing that made me see: not the city. The sheet.

The reception centre gave me a bed, and the bed was in a room called a four-bed room, in via Padova. The four-bed room was a room with four beds, and the four beds were four men with a package and a purpose, and nobody spoke: you speak softly, as is their habit, the men who arrive. Three hundred euros a month, each: a bed, not a room. In Joal my father's house cost nothing, and the fish was my father's, and the sea was everyone's. Here a bed cost three hundred euros, and the bed wasn't mine: it was the month's. You can pay three hundred euros for a bed, and not be poor in everything, and be poor in home: home, in Milan, is the thing you don't have. The four-bed room, in the evening, had the usual noise: the voices, the steps, the hum of the heating, and every so often a prayer — one, softly, as is their habit, prayers: they're said softly because God hears.

I found the work the third day, with Bakary's voice, who had arrived in Milan two weeks before me. Bakary had found a building site, and the building site was the one of those who don't choose: you take. Fifty euros a day, ten hours, and the undeclared work: the hours aren't counted, they're finished. In Dakar the building sites gave fifty euros a day, and the fifty euros, in Milan, gave fifty euros a day: the price of people, in Milan, is the price of those who arrive. The undeclared work isn't contested: it's taken.

A day on the building site was a day of those you don't forget, and you don't forget because there's nothing to forget: there's the cement. I arrived at seven, with the cold, and the cold, in Milan, is the cold you don't expect: you put up with it. I carried the cement, and cement weighs, and for the first time in four years the weight was mine: it wasn't the package, it wasn't the sheet. It was cement, and the weight of cement, when you no longer have the things that weigh, you choose. I mixed, I tied the iron, and the ten hours I did with my hands, and hands, on the building site, are the hands of someone who does everything: they keep. At noon I ate standing, with the two-euro sandwich, and the sandwich, on the building site, is the sandwich of those who arrive: you eat standing, and you don't know what you're eating.

The work with the hands, in the morning, is the work you don't think: you do. And the work you do, in the morning, holds your life. It was the first thing in four years that didn't make fear. I didn't know it, and I knew it in books: on an estate, once, a man who had everything discovered that the only thing that doesn't make fear is the work with the hands (Tolstoy, *War and Peace*). The work with the hands, in the morning, is the work you don't think: you do.

The language was the hardest thing in the city. French I knew well, and French, on the building sites of Milan, isn't needed: you know it perfectly. The things you study, when they aren't needed, you know perfectly: it's their secret. Italian I knew little, like everyone: I knew it the way you know the things you don't have: from the dinghy, from the centre, from the building site. *I don't understand* was the first sentence I said in Italian, and the sentence, at twenty-six, is the most humbling sentence in the world: I said it every morning, for two years, and mornings, in Milan, began at seven. The foreman said things once, and things said once, on the building site, aren't repeated: they're put up with.

I received the first folded sheet in December, at the Questura, in the queue with the sheet in my hand. The queue is the oldest thing in the world, and the number was B037, and the counter was the usual one. The man behind the glass took the sheet, looked at the fingerprints, and asked me the purpose of my stay. — Work — I said. It was the right word and it wasn't the truth, and the truth, at the Questura, isn't said: it's written. But I received the sheet, and the sheet said *provisional*. Provisional, at twenty-six, is the word you don't say: you keep it.

Habit is an anaesthesia, it puts your days to sleep one by one (Proust), and the days put to sleep, in Milan, look like a life. The first six months melted, and the six melted months aren't counted: they're crossed. I got used to the queue, the undeclared work, the four-bed room, the double price at the shop downstairs. I got used to it in two weeks. Man, the books say, gets used to everything (Dostoevsky, *Memories from the House of the Dead*), and it seemed to me a sentence for the condemned. It was a sentence for everyone. Fear, when you get used to it, doesn't end: it lowers, and it lowers under things, like the smell of tiredness in queues. It stayed there. I knew it.

Bakary, in the bed next to mine, in the evening, said the things we all said: — Tomorrow — and *tomorrow* was the word you don't believe: you say. Bakary was twenty-six, like me, and Mali, and the package, and the purpose. The brother of the road: the road, in Milan, is via Padova, and brothers of the road, in the evening, you choose: you choose them the way you choose neighbours on a dinghy.

The days melt, and melted days aren't sent home: they're kept. The Sunday after, for the first time, I did the calculation: how much I sent. I sent nothing. And nothing, at home, isn't sent: it's explained.

# Chapter 5 — The Ten Per Cent


> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

I made the first sending in January, from the office on via Padova, with the three hundred euros in my hand. The office on via Padova is an office of those you don't choose: you take. ## A note on the allusions

The protagonist's reflections contain subtle allusions to Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Proust and Leopardi, marked in parentheses: they are not quotations — they are ways of thinking borrowed, the way languages are borrowed. The epigraphs at the start of the chapters are real quotations: Fyodor Dostoevsky (*Memories from the House of the Dead*, *The Brothers Karamazov*), Leo Tolstoy (*Anna Karenina*, *War and Peace*, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*), Marcel Proust (*Time Regained*), Giacomo Leopardi (*A Silvia*, *L'infinito*, *The Village Saturday*). *Inshallah* (Arabic: God willing) is the answer to everything. Wolof words are kept as they are: *jërëjëf* (thank you), *naka la* (how are you), *jam rekk* (I'm well, and enough of that).
