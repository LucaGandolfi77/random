# Prologue — The Queue

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (the immigration office) · **Story time:** May 2026
**Words:** ~900 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/00-prologo.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "the only true paradise is always the paradise that we have lost."
>
> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

The immigration office in Chiang Mai opens at eight in the morning, and by ten past eight the queue already reaches the gate. You stand in line with your passport in your hand, the way you hold a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of your own life, and that the numbers are never good. In front of me was a Frenchman who had married here, and behind me a couple of English pensioners who were discussing the cost of fruit. The floor was of clean tiles, and there was the smell of disinfectant, which smelled of order; and under the smell of disinfectant there was the smell of everyone who has been waiting for hours, which is the smell of tiredness left lying around. I knew that smell in Milan: it was the smell of Monday mornings on the underground, and I hadn't smelled it for seven months.

My number was forty-two. On the display, at the top, the numbers that were called: A037, B014, C009. I waited for mine with the feeling of waiting for a verdict. The passport was full of stamps: sixty days, then thirty, then sixty again, then the extension, then the ninety-day report. A stamp that stamps your life in instalments, and the instalments aren't yours to choose. In Milan I had a contract that renewed every six months, and I felt like a temporary worker. Here the stamps lasted thirty days, and I felt even more temporary: I was never inside anything, neither there nor here. You can be temporary in two places for seven months, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom. In Milan the cage, at least, had taut bars: you knocked against them, and knocking is something you can feel. Here there were no bars: there was the stamp, and you don't knock against the stamp. The stamp marks you, and passes.

I came here because it's cheap to live. I used to say it to everyone in Milan, with the confidence of someone who has the numbers: the sixty-baht pad thai — one euro sixty —, the rent for a condominium with a pool at nine thousand baht — two hundred and fifty euros —, life at a third of the cost. Two years before I had been here on holiday, two weeks, and I had seen the markets, the mangoes, the people who smile, and I had done the math on my phone, on the boat, in the sun: thirteen thousand five hundred euros put aside in five years, almost fifteen months of life at flyer prices. I had thought that paradise rents out, and that there the rent costs less than the rent in Milan. It felt like a calculation. It was a miscalculation.

Because the calculation counted everything, except the main thing: that there, without a job and without a purpose, the days don't add up. They melt. In Milan my days cost me everything — the rent, the gym, the food, the bills — and they were mine. Here my days cost me almost nothing, and they belonged to nobody. Nobody waited for them, nobody bought them, nobody paid for them. Money counted out on the table of paradise, and nobody touches it: it is the loneliest thing in the world.

The number was called: A042, counter seven. The woman behind the glass took the passport, looked at the stamps one by one, the way you read test results, and asked me the purpose of my stay. The purpose of my stay. I stood there, holding the question, and I had nothing to answer that wasn't true.

— Tourist — I said.

It was the right word and it wasn't the truth. After seven months I wasn't a tourist, and I wasn't a resident, and I wasn't anything that has a name on a form. The woman stamped, handed me the passport back, and the stamp said thirty days. Thirty more days, and I didn't know what to do with them.

I went out, and outside there was the sun, high and white, the sun that knew how to be there for everyone. Two years before, on holiday, that sun had seemed to me the sun of freedom. That morning it seemed to me the sun of a car park.

Paradise, the books say, is the paradise that we have lost (Proust). I had arrived in paradise, and I was losing everything else: the language, the money, my people, the meaning. I didn't even eat the sixty-baht pad thai anymore: I looked at it on menus, and the calculation from two years before seemed to me the calculation of someone who had never been there.

This is the story of seven months, and of how a girl from Milan resigned from her own life so she would stop overpaying, and discovered that paradise has a queue, and that the queue is made of people like her.

I begin at the beginning, which was Milan, and it cost nine hundred euros a month.
