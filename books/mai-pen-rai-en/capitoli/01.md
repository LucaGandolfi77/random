# Chapter 1 — The Billed Hours

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Milan (via Tortona, via Borsieri) · **Story time:** September 2025, one week of days
**Words:** ~1.300 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/01.md` ✅

## Draft

> "Ivan Ilyich's life was the most simple, the most ordinary, and therefore the most terrible."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*

The 8:11 M5 was full as always, and full, in Milan, isn't called full: it's called normal. I got on at Isola with the pass that beeped and the bag on my shoulder, and from the bag stuck out the strap of the computer that touched people: in Milan you touch people every morning, and people touch you, and nobody says anything, as is their habit, the metros: you stand close without knowing each other, and it's fine, because knowing each other in Milan costs. At the Garibaldi stop got on the clothes I didn't know, and clothes, in Milan, you know them all: you see them on stories, on flyers, in shop windows. I wore the clothes of always, and the clothes of always, at twenty-nine, are the clothes you don't look at: you wear them.

The agency was called Mercurio, and it was in via Tortona, upstairs from a lamp show. The founder had chosen the name — he had studied the Greeks — and he had chosen it well without knowing: Mercury was the god of merchants and of thieves, and it was the same god. In our trade the two businesses happened every day, and nobody noticed.

The floor was an open space of thirty-two desks, and the thirty-two desks were thirty-two people with the computer on, all talking at the same time, softly, as is their habit, the agencies: you speak softly because shouting tires you, and tiredness, in agencies, is the only thing you don't bill. There was the usual noise: the voices, the keyboards, the hum of the computers, and every so often a laugh — one, stifled at once, as is their habit, the laughs in agencies: you can't, they last too long.

My trade was billing hours. On the screen, top right, there was the dashboard with my numbers: hours billed, hours to bill, variance. Fifteen hundred a year, which is the measure of a modern life: it's measured in hours, and the hours are measured in money, and the money is measured in what's left. At the end of the month, of my salary — one thousand four hundred and eighty euros net — almost nothing was left: the rent for the studio on via Borsieri took nine hundred, the gym forty-nine, the pass thirty-nine, the food two hundred and sixty, the bills ninety-five, the phone ten. Ninety euros were left. Ninety euros for a whole life, and it wasn't little: it was the deal. You work to pay, and they leave you alone.

The client was a discount supermarket called IperRisparmio, and we made the flyers. Flyers, in Milan, are a trade: they're made by people with a degree, for people who don't read them. The thirty per cent discount, the yellow tag, the photo of the roast chicken. I spent my days lowering prices on paper, and my life raised them. It felt like a scam: the prices on paper went down, the prices of my life went up, and I was in the middle, billing both. The roast chicken, on the flyer, I looked at it every morning, and the chicken, on the flyer, cost seven ninety, and the chicken, in my life, cost nine twenty. That too was the deal: the prices on the flyer aren't the prices. Everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

The creative director was called Renzo, and his trade was presenting. He presented everything: ideas, agencies, himself. He had a way of entering rooms that was a trade in itself: he entered, and the room became his. The ideas that were born at the desks were born from the team, and the team was Renzo, and the ideas that were born from me were born from the team. I had been at Mercurio five years and my ideas were Renzo's, and I didn't even notice: habit is an anaesthesia, it puts your days to sleep one by one (Proust), and you wake only when you've finished sleeping your life.

I had lived five years as one should: project contract renewed every six months, the overtime you don't ask for, the extra hours that don't get billed. I was a woman like any other, living as she should (Tolstoy), and the woman like any other, in books, dies on the bed of an apartment she has paid for all her life. I wasn't dying. I was billing. It's different, and it's nothing different.

At noon I ate at my desk with a sandwich from the bar downstairs, four euros fifty, and the rest of the sandwich I threw away. In Milan you throw away food too, with the same ease with which you throw away hours: the two things you can't keep, and nobody keeps them. The sandwich, besides, I ate for the same reasons I looked at flyers: to fill the hours, not to live them. I didn't feel hunger: hunger, in agencies, is a detail you don't bill.

I was, though, someone who had studied. I had left the Statale with a degree in Classics, a thesis on Leopardi, and the Ancient Greek I still knew, the way you know the things that aren't needed: perfectly. On the shelf at home there was the Zibaldone, and I had opened it for the last time for the thesis, four years before. Every so often I passed by it and didn't touch it, as is their habit, books: you keep them, you don't use them. There was, though, one thing I remembered, and I remembered it well, because I had underlined it in pencil, at twenty-two, without knowing I would need it: that every human being has a thought that governs them (Leopardi, Zibaldone), and that the dominant thought isn't a cage: it's the thing that makes you walk straight. My dominant thought, at twenty-nine, was the calculation: how much it costs, how much is left, how much is missing. And the calculation isn't a thought: it's an account. Accounts don't make you walk straight. Accounts keep you still.

The thing I didn't say to anyone was Thailand. Two years before, in August, I had been there on holiday, two weeks, with a friend from university who later went back. There I had seen the life you don't see in Milan: the sixty-baht pad thai — one euro sixty —, the rent for a condominium with a pool at nine thousand baht — two hundred and fifty euros —, the mango you buy at the market for half of nothing. People smiled, and the sun set at half past six, and everything cost half of nothing. I had done the math on my phone, on the boat, in the sun, and the math said a thing I hadn't said to anyone, not even to myself, in two years: that there you could live on what I had put aside, for months. I didn't say it. The things you think at sea, usually, you forget: it was the only one I had kept. I kept it in a pocket of my phone, the way you keep rare things: I didn't use it, I knew it.

It was on Sunday evening, in that state of things — no headset, but the dashboard was there, and the calculation was there, and the flyers were us — that Martina's call came.

The phone rang twice and on the display it said *Martina*. I took the phone the way you take the phone on Sunday evening: without suspicion, with the hand that was looking for the remote. Martina was thirty, she was an analyst at a bank, she trained at six thirty in the morning, and she had just signed the mortgage for a two-room flat in Città Studi. We were three, on Sundays: Martina in Milan, Sara in Rome — she had won a civil service exam at the Revenue Agency, and stability, she said, was a religion —, Giulia in Berlin, who was an engineer at a startup and earned sixty-five thousand euros a year, and escaping, she said, is done well when you have a job and a plan. I was the fourth, and the fourth was me: the one who had studied the Greeks and billed hours.

— So — Martina said —, the mortgage is signed. Thirty years. I swear it, come on. —

— Thirty years — I said.

It was the measure of a life, and I said it the way you say measures: without thinking. Martina spoke of the mortgage with the voice of someone who has just won something, and she had won something: she had won thirty years of a house, and the house was hers, and the mortgage was hers, and everything was in her name. I had nothing in my name, except the calculation. The calculation was mine, and it was the calculation of someone who has nothing: how much it costs, how much is left, how much is missing.

— There's a gym near the house — Martina said. — At six thirty there's the class that isn't crowded. I joined: nineteen euros a month, a discount, come on. —

A discount. In Milan discounts, like credits, go up every month: it's the thing you don't discuss. Martina had a job that was a purpose, a mortgage that was a purpose, a gym that was a purpose: three purposes, and three purposes make a life. I had none, and no purpose, on a Sunday evening, pretends to be fine.

— And how are you? — Martina said.

— I'm great — I said.

It was the first thing I said to myself that wasn't true.
