# Chapter 4 — The Stamps

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai, Penang, Hang Dong · **Story time:** 12 November 2025
**Words:** ~1.200 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/04.md` ✅

---

## Draft

Thirty days always run out, and it's the thing nobody says about paradise: that it runs out, and it runs out fast, and that the hurry isn't the calendar's, it's life's. My thirty-day exemption expired on the twelfth of November, and the fear came with the app, which counted my remaining days every morning the way you count the days left to sentences: backwards, and without mercy. In Milan I had a contract that renewed every six months, and the renewal, in Milan, felt like the most anguishing thing in the world: six months of life, renewed in instalments, always halfway. Here the thing was called a stamp, and it lasted thirty days, and it felt even more anguishing: not six months. One.

The stamp, moreover, isn't asked for: it's put up with. In the weeks, the guesthouse told me the visa could be "fixed", and fixing, in Thailand, is a trade: there are people who do it, for three thousand five hundred baht.

Somchai was the name of the guy who fixed things. He wasn't the one who called it that: the guesthouse called him that, and the guesthouse was right. Somchai was forty, had a six-metre office with a printer, and fixed things for three thousand five hundred baht: the extensions, the forms, the photographs taken in a hurry. The photographs for the stamp, with the white background and the face that doesn't smile. In Milan I knew the face that doesn't smile: it was the badge photo, and the badge photo was the same for five years.

— Visa run — Somchai said, and with his hand he drew a line that left the country and came back: a circle, drawn by a finger, which was my life. — Cheaper than a new visa. And the new stamp, on the way back, is free. —

Free. In Milan nothing was free, not even the air: air, in Milan, you pay for with your lungs. There the stamp was free, and free, for someone who had overpaid for everything, is a word I didn't know: it seemed like a promise. It was a trick: the free stamp holds you for thirty days, and the thirty days, when they run out, you redo. Free is the longest instalment in the world.

The bus to Penang left at eight in the evening, and it took twelve hours, and the border arrived in the morning, with the queue. The queue at borders is the oldest queue in the world: it was there before borders, and it will be after. I stood in line with the passport in my hand, and the passport was full of stamps, and the stamps all said the same thing: you are here, but you are not from here. The bus was one of those buses you don't choose: with the seats that don't recline, the air conditioning at eleven degrees, and people who sleep the way things sleep: for real. I slept too, two hours, with my head on the window, and the window, at night, was freezing, and the cold, in a hot country, is the thing you don't expect: you put up with it.

Penang arrived in the morning, and Penang is an island of those you don't choose: with pagodas, markets, the smell of spices. The border you crossed on foot, and the crossing, in the morning, took an hour: the queue, the forms, the officer. The officer looked at the stamps, asked the purpose, and I said: *tourist*. It was the right word and it wasn't the truth, and the truth, at borders, isn't said: it's stamped. In Penang I ate a kebab, for three ringgit, and the kebab, on an island that wasn't mine, was the lunch of those who cross: you eat standing, and you don't know what you're eating.

The new stamp said thirty days. I went back to Chiang Mai, and in Chiang Mai there was Patcha, who asked *how are you*, and I said *fine*, and *fine* was true for the first time in three days. But it was true for three days: it was the stamp saying it. A life of thirty days, renewed in instalments: the renewal, in Milan, lasted six months, and I felt like a temporary worker. There it lasted thirty days, and I felt like a temporary of a temporary: I was never inside anything, neither there nor here. You can be temporary in two places for seven months, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom. In Milan the cage, at least, had taut bars: you knocked against them, and knocking is something you feel. There were no bars: there was the stamp, and you don't knock against the stamp. The stamp marks you, and passes.

Man, the books say, gets used to everything (Dostoevsky, *Memories from the House of the Dead*), and it seemed to me a sentence for the condemned. It was a sentence for everyone. I got used to the queue, the forms, the hurried photographs, the fear of the stamp that renews every morning with the app. I got used to it in two weeks. Fear, when you get used to it, doesn't end: it lowers, and it lowers under things, like the smell of tiredness in queues. It stayed there. I knew it.

It was in that state of things — the new stamp, the lowered fear, Somchai's three thousand five hundred baht — that I did the thing I shouldn't have done: I committed.

I found the condo rent on the app, the way you find things on the app: with the photo and the price. The condo was in Hang Dong, a neighbourhood that isn't a neighbourhood: it's a street that doesn't end, with the condominiums that don't end, with the pool. Nine thousand baht a month. Two hundred and fifty euros. The sixty-baht pad thai, and the rent at two hundred and fifty: the flyer of paradise. I took the condo for three months, and I took the scooter too, which was the vehicle I had never had: four thousand five hundred baht a month, with the deposit. The scooter was red, and the red, in the morning, you saw from afar: it seemed like a thing that was mine, and it wasn't mine: it was rented, like the stamp, like life.

I laughed the first day, and the first day the heat was a gentle wall, and the street didn't end, and it seemed I was going somewhere. I went from the market to the condo, from the condo to the market: a circle, drawn by Somchai's finger, which was my life. You don't see the circle you're inside: you see the one that's drawn. My circle had been drawn by Somchai for three thousand five hundred baht, and the drawing, in the morning, seemed like freedom.

In the evening, from the condo balcony, I looked at the pool. The pool was empty, and the water was blue, and blue water, in the evening, you look at the way you look at rare things. Freedom with blue water, and the rent at two hundred and fifty euros. It seemed I had found it, life.

I hadn't found it. It hadn't begun yet. In Recanati, once, a boy climbed a hill and looked beyond the hedge: unending spaces, superhuman silences (Leopardi, *L'infinito*). The infinite, without a hedge, is vertigo. I had the infinite, and I didn't have the hedge, and I didn't know the vertigo was coming: it came the way true things come. All at once, and without being asked.
