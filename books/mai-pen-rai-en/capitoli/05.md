# Chapter 5 — The Hedge

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (Hang Dong, Warorot market) · **Story time:** late November – December 2025
**Words:** ~1.400 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/05.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "Still dear to me was this solitary hill, / and this hedge, that from so great a part / of the last horizon excludes the sight."
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, *L'infinito*

The days melt, and it's the thing nobody says about life without work: that they don't empty. They melt. In Milan my days were full, and fullness seemed a sentence; here my days were empty, and emptiness, I thought, had to be freedom. It wasn't freedom: it was the opposite. In Milan my days had a shape: the shape was the dashboard, the M5, the thirty per cent. The shape jostled, and knocking is something you feel. Here my days had no shape, and the absence of shape you don't feel: you notice later, and later is made of evenings.

I made the routine that wasn't a routine: I slept till eight, swam at nine, did the market at ten, coffee at eleven. It looks like a routine. It isn't a routine: it's a list. Routines aim somewhere: mine aimed at nine, and at nine there was nothing. In Milan my day aimed at the evening, and the evening aimed at Saturday, and Saturday aimed at friends. Here my day aimed at nothing, and nothing you don't follow.

At the co-working, the third day, there was Mikkel. Mikkel was Danish, thirty-four, and he was a UX designer for a company I never saw, from a computer that is never off. Four hours of work a day, he said, and the four hours he did at the co-working, with the coffee, and the rest of the day was his.

— You should do like me — he said, without malice, with the kindness of the Nordics. — Get a job. Four hours. The rest is life. —

— And what do the four hours give you? — I asked, once, without having asked anything like it.

Mikkel thought about it, with the coffee in his hand, and answered the thing the books say:

— A reason to get up at nine instead of eleven. —

He laughed, and I laughed too, and the laugh was real, and it was the first real laugh in two weeks. But the sentence, in the evening, stayed with me: a reason to get up. In Milan I had a reason, and the reason seemed a sentence; here I had none, and no reason, I thought, was freedom. It wasn't freedom. Mikkel had the thing I lack: the reason. He didn't know it, and I knew it. His life, without the reason, would have been mine: four hours to pass, and the other twenty not to know what to do with.

I started the Thai lessons in the second week, so as not to be someone who does nothing: the lessons were on the fifth floor of a building that isn't a building, with the teacher who spoke softly. The tones, the teacher said, are the five ways of saying the same thing, and the same thing, said with the wrong tones, is another thing. I repeated the tones, and the tones didn't rise: they stayed flat, and flat Thai isn't Thai: it's a noise.

— Again — the teacher said, and *again*, at twenty-nine, is the most humbling word in the world.

The teacher said *good* out of kindness, and *good* out of kindness, at twenty-nine, is the thing you feel the way you feel damp in walls: I knew Ancient Greek, and I didn't know how to say *ice*. The first time in my life I failed at something, and failing, at twenty-nine, is a thing I didn't know. In Milan I always succeeded: I billed, I did the numbers, I wrote the thirty per cent. The things you succeed at, usually, are the things that don't count.

I did the Warorot market every morning, and the mango I bought from the mango woman. The mango woman was fifty, with the usual smile, and the usual prices: the prices, though, weren't usual. The mango cost double, and double was the farang price. The farang price isn't written, and you don't negotiate it with anger. I made the anger, the first time, on a Sunday: I said *double?*, and I said *why?*, and the woman laughed, and the laugh was kind, and kindness, with anger in your hand, is the thing you can't fight: you lose it.

Patcha, in the evening, explained the farang price to me, and she explained it with her voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly:

— Farang price — she said —, because we earn little. Double, for you, is half of nothing. Double, for us, is the month. —

The farang price, explained like that, wasn't a scam anymore: it was a justice upside down. In Milan I paid double too: the rent, the gym, the food. Double, in Milan, was my life, and there was nobody who fixed it. There double was a thing you could pay, and you could understand, and the thing, said by Patcha, felt like a calculation: double, for you, is half of nothing. It felt like a miscalculation, and I didn't know: double is never half of nothing. Double is double, and doubles aren't paid with half: they're paid with everything, or with meaning. I didn't know which.

At the temple, the second time, I did the thing you don't do: I went in with shoes on, and with my foot I touched the threshold. Feet, in Thailand, are the lowest part of the body, and the head is the highest part, and the two parts aren't confused: it's their law. Nobody said anything. Nobody ever says anything, there, when you're wrong: face is lost, and face is lost in silence, and silence, with lost face in your hand, is the cruellest thing: you feel that the other knows, and the other doesn't say. In Milan things are said, and things said, usually, wound: there things aren't said, and the unsaid, I thought, doesn't wound. It wounds. It wounds more. Face is lost in silence, and you find it only when the other says *how are you*, and *how are you*, in the evening, was Patcha.

It was in that state of things — the failed lessons, the double price, the lost face — that I understood the thing I understood late, and I understood it seven months late. In Recanati, once, a boy climbed a hill and looked beyond the hedge: unending spaces, superhuman silences, and the deepest quiet (Leopardi, *L'infinito*). And he understood a thing nobody understands at twenty-two: that the infinite doesn't free. It frightens. The hedge wasn't a cage: it was the measure that made the infinite possible. Without the hedge, the infinite is the void, and the void you don't look at: you cross it.

I had taken my hedge away, and I called freedom the void. In Milan the hedge was there, and I called it a sentence: the dashboard, the M5, the thirty per cent. The hedge jostled, and knocking, in the morning, is something you feel. But it was the thing that kept me: it told me where to look, and the infinite I made myself. There was no hedge, and the infinite was made by nobody: it stayed there, without measure, and the measure, in the evening, is the thing that's missing. I was missing a hedge (Leopardi, *L'infinito*), and I didn't know: I only knew the evenings were long, and the long evenings, in December, are the longest evenings in the world.

In the evening, from the condo balcony, I watched the sunset. The sunset was beautiful, and the sun set at half past six, and the sunset, two years before, had seemed to me the sunset of freedom. That evening it seemed to me a sunset. The void you don't look at. I watched the sunset, and the sunset told me nothing, and nothing, in the evening, answers nothing.

On Sunday evening, in Italy, it was the hour of the calls. The phone was there, on the table, and I didn't look at it. I didn't make the calls. And the calls not made, in the evening, are the calls you don't hear: you hear them later.
