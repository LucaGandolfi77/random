# Chapter 6 — The Happy Lives

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (Hang Dong) · **Story time:** December 2025
**Words:** ~1.400 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/06.md` ✅

---

## Draft

> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

On Sunday evening, in Italy, it was the hour of the calls. There was no Sunday evening: there was Sunday, and Sunday, at six, ended with the sunset, and the sunset wasn't the hour of the calls: it was the hour of nothing. But I made the calls anyway, so as not to be someone who does nothing: I made them with the time difference, at two at night there which was one in the afternoon here, and the time difference, at night, is the thing you feel: you're together and you're not together, and the two things you can't keep.

Martina called first. The mortgage had been signed two months, and Martina spoke of the mortgage with the voice of someone who has just won something: — Seventy thousand euros of capital — she said —, and the instalment is eight hundred. I got the gym near the house, at six thirty there's the class that isn't crowded. — The gym at six thirty in the morning, by choice: in Milan you choose tiredness too, and chosen tiredness, I thought, was the richest thing in the world. Martina had a job that was a purpose, and a mortgage that was a purpose, and the gym that was a purpose: three purposes, and three purposes make a life. I had none, and no purpose, on Sunday evening, pretends to be fine.

— And you? — Martina said. — How is it, there? —

— I'm great — I said. — Here it's cheap to live. The pad thai at one euro sixty, the rent at two hundred and fifty with the pool. —

It was the second time I said to myself that it wasn't true, and the second time, usually, is the time you learn to say it. I didn't say it well. Lies, when they begin, you hear: you hear them the way you hear damp in walls. Martina heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked, and the lies of friends are the heavy things that aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

— Will you do Christmas there? — Martina said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who doesn't say the thing she wants to say.

— I'll do Christmas there — I said.

It was the third lie, and the third was made with the voice of someone who doesn't know. I didn't know where I would do Christmas: Christmas, in Chiang Mai, was a Tuesday like the others, with the heat, and the heat, at Christmas, isn't called Christmas: it's called December. In Milan Christmas, in December, is the thing you wait for: the markets, the lights, the dinners. There nothing was waited for, and the thing, said to Martina, felt the heaviest of the evening: not Christmas. The waiting.

Sara called after. Sara was thirty-one, she was a clerk at the Revenue Agency in Rome, and stability, she said, was a religion: — Permanent contract, thirteen monthly salaries, and Saturday is always Saturday. — Saturday is always Saturday: in Rome Saturday, in the evening, is an aperitivo in Trastevere, and the aperitivo in Trastevere, in December, is the surest thing in the world. Sara had stability, and stability, I thought, was a legitimate boredom: the boredom of someone who knows why she does nothing. My boredom wasn't legitimate: it was the boredom of someone who doesn't know. — And you? — Sara said. — You'll get used to it. A year there and it'll seem normal. — And I said *yes, I'll get used to it*, and the *yes* was a lie, and the fourth lie, usually, is the time you stop hearing it.

Giulia called after, and Giulia had the escape done well. In Berlin she was an engineer at a startup, she earned sixty-five thousand euros a year, and escaping, she said, is done well when you have a job and a plan: — I left Italy, but I left with a contract. You left with a flight. — And the sentence was the true one, and the sentence wounded me, and the wound, on the phone, you don't feel: you feel it later, and later, at night, answers nothing. — What do you do, there? — Giulia said. And I didn't say: I do the market at ten, the pool at nine, the Thai lessons on the fifth floor. I do nothing. Nothing, at twenty-nine, isn't said: it's pretended.

— I'm seeing the country — I said. — I'm learning the language. —

It was the fifth lie, and the fifth was the worst: it was half true. Half-true lies, usually, are the lies you tell yourself. I knew Ancient Greek, and I knew the thesis, and I knew the thirty per cent, and I didn't know how to say *ice*. The Thai lessons I had suspended, in the third month, because the tones didn't rise, and flat tones, at twenty-nine, are the thing you feel in your legs. I didn't say it. Heavy things aren't said on the phone: they're kept, and they stay.

After the calls, on the sofa, I looked at the stories. Happy lives, on stories, are all alike (Tolstoj — Tolstoy): the gym at six, the Sunday brunch, the dog, the sunset seen from a terrace, the Christmas market in Berlin, the Christmas market in Milan. Mine resembled nobody's: there was the market at ten, and there was the pool, and the pool was empty. The void, on stories, isn't put: it's the thing nobody photographs. I tried to photograph the empty pool, and the empty pool, photographed, was beautiful, and the beautiful, on stories, is the thing you put: and I put it. And the empty pool, put on stories, felt like a lie. It was the sixth lie, and the sixth wasn't on the phone: it was to myself.

Happy lives, on stories, are all alike, and mine resembled none. My friends' lives all aimed: at the mortgage, at the exam, at the contract, at Christmas. Mine aimed at nowhere anymore. In Recanati, once, a boy climbed a hill on a Village Saturday, and happiness was in the waiting (Leopardi): the Saturday that aims, the week that aims at Saturday. My friends' lives were the Village Saturday: they waited, and the waiting was the happiness. Mine wasn't waited for anymore: it was crossed, and crossing isn't a happiness: it's a passage. The passage, on Sunday evening, isn't photographed: it's felt.

In the evening, after the stories, my mother's call came. The phone rang twice and on the display it said *Mum*. I took the phone the way you take the phone on Sunday evening: without suspicion, with the hand that was looking for the remote.

— Ciao amore, all good? You though, at least you're well, eh. —

It was the question she asked every Sunday, and the question was the true one: *but at least you're well?* My mother, in Cesano Maderno, asked the question the way you ask true questions: without knowing it was the true one. My father, in the background, said something about his vegetable garden, and the garden, in Cesano Maderno, was the thing my father had after forty years of factory: forty years at Falck in Sesto San Giovanni, one job in his whole life, and the garden, today, was his purpose. My father had forty years of one job, and didn't complain: he complained, but he knew why he worked. And someone who knows why he works, on Sunday evening, talks about the garden: and the garden is a purpose.

— I'm fine, mum — I said.

It was the seventh lie, and the seventh was the cruellest: it was made with the voice of someone who loves. My mother heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask. Heavy things aren't asked, and the lies of daughters are the heavy things that aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

The Sunday after, for the first time, I didn't call anyone. And nobody, for the first time, noticed the difference.
