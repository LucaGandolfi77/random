# Chapter 7 — The Hands

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (Patcha's guesthouse, Hang Dong) · **Story time:** January–February 2026
**Words:** ~1.350 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/07.md` ✅

---

## Draft

In January I started the Thai lessons again, and the tones began to rise. Not all of them: the mid tone rose, and the mid tone, in Thai, is the tone that has no way: it's the tone you say without thinking. *Mai* said with the mid tone means *new*, and the new mango, at the market, you buy with the mid tone. I said it right, in the third lesson, and the teacher said *good*, and the *good* wasn't out of kindness: it was, for the first time, a real *good*. At twenty-nine, the first real *good*, in a thing I didn't know, felt like a victory. Victories, usually, are the things that don't count: this one counted, and I didn't know it.

At the market, the mango woman recognised me. She didn't recognise me by my face: she recognised me by the tone. I said *mai* with the mid tone, and the woman laughed, and the laugh was the usual one, and the mango cost double minus a little. Double minus a little isn't the right price: it's the price you give to those who come back. To those who come back, usually, you give things: the right price you give to those who stay. I wasn't someone who stays: I was someone who comes back, and I paid double minus a little, and I paid it gladly, and the gladly, in the morning, felt like the richest thing: I didn't know it.

I did the ninety-day report alone, at the immigration office, in the queue with the passport in my hand. The queue was the usual one, and the number was thirty-one, and the counter was the usual one. The woman behind the glass took the passport, looked at the stamps, and asked me the purpose of my stay. — Tourist — I said. It was the right word and it wasn't the truth, and the truth, at borders, isn't said: it's stamped. But I did the stamp alone, and the stamp done alone, at twenty-nine, in a queue in an office of a country that isn't yours, felt like the most adult thing: it wasn't adult. It was a stamp. Stamps aren't adult: they're the things that mark you, and pass.

The thing that was true instead was the guesthouse. Patcha, one morning in January, asked me if I knew how to take photographs. She didn't ask it like that: she asked it with her voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly: — You have camera. I have guesthouse. Maybe? — The guesthouse was full of things you don't see: the veranda with the plumeria, the breakfast with the soup, the rooms with the fan. The things you don't see, on stories, aren't put: it's the law of stories. Patcha wasn't on stories. Patcha was on her feet from five in the morning, and five in the morning, in a guesthouse, is the five of someone who does everything: cleans, cooks, books, answers. I took the photographs every morning, for two weeks, at six, and six in the morning, in a guesthouse, is the most beautiful six in the world: the plumeria, the soup, the sun coming into the veranda. I put them on an Instagram that wasn't there, and the Instagram I made, and the descriptions in English I wrote, and the bookings began to arrive.

The first booking came at the end of January, and the booking was from an Australian couple who had seen the photos. — The garden photo — the woman said, on the phone, with the voice of someone who has seen things. — We booked because of the garden photo. — And the garden photo was mine: I had taken it, at six in the morning, with the plumeria and the January light. It felt like the smallest thing in the world. It was the truest: for the first time in seven months something happened because of me, and the because, at six in the morning, wasn't the dashboard. It was a photo. The things you make, in the morning, are the things you don't count: you see them in others.

It was the first thing in seven months that didn't make fear. I didn't know it, and I knew it in books: in a state, once, a man who had everything discovered that the only thing that doesn't make fear is the work with the hands (Tolstoj — Tolstoy, *War and Peace*). The work with the hands, in the morning, is the work you don't think: you do. And the work you do, in the morning, holds your life: it holds it the way the hedge holds the infinite. I didn't know it yet: I only knew the mornings, for the first time in seven months, were mine.

Patcha, in the evening, sat on the step, and the smile was the usual one. She said *mai pen rai* in the evening, after the work, not instead of the work: it's the thing I understood late, and I understood it seven months late. Patcha's *mai pen rai*, in the evening, was the truest thing in the country: it was the answer to everything, said by someone who had done everything. The answers to everything, said by someone who has done nothing, are the answers that answer nothing: they're mine. Patcha's *mai pen rai*, in the evening, answered.

Mikkel, at the co-working, one afternoon in February, asked me what I did. — Photos — I said. — For a guesthouse. —

— For free? — Mikkel said.

— For free — I said.

Mikkel laughed, and the laugh was kind, and he said the thing the books say: — You know, in Russia, once, a prince said that beauty will save the world (Dostoevskij — Dostoevsky, *The Idiot*). — And he laughed again, and said: — In Thailand, beauty doesn't pay the visa. — And he was right, and I didn't know it: beauty doesn't pay the visa, and the visa cost three thousand five hundred baht, and beauty, in February, felt like the richest thing. It wasn't the richest thing: it was the thing you don't buy, and it's its secret. Beauty you make, you don't buy, and the making, in the morning, at six, was mine.

It was in February, in that state of things — the tones that rose, the photos at six, the bookings that arrived — that I had the idea. The idea was the one I shouldn't have had: and what if I stayed? You can think you stay, and the thinking, in February, seemed like freedom. It wasn't freedom: it was the new habit, which anaesthetises like the old one (Proust): it puts your days to sleep one by one, and the days put to sleep, in February, look like a life. I thought I was cured. I was only busy. Busy things, in February, you don't count: you do. And the doing, in February, felt like a healing.

It was in February, though, also the thing I didn't say to anyone: the insurance. I had taken the health insurance in October, for thirty-nine euros a month, and in January I let it lapse. I didn't let it lapse for a reason: I let it lapse to save. Thirty-nine euros a month, and the saving, in February, seemed like the wisest thing. Saving, as always, you notice later. I noticed it in May, and in May it was too late: it was the thing I didn't know, and only the insurance knew it, and the insurance says nothing.

In the February evening, from the condo balcony, I looked at the pool. The pool was empty, and the water was blue, and the sunset set at half past six. The sunset, in February, felt like a sunset of those you keep. I thought I was cured.

I was only busy. In March the smoke came.
