# Chapter 10 — Thirty-Two Thousand Baht

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (the hospital, room 214) · **Story time:** May 2026
**Words:** ~1.450 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/10.md` ✅

## Draft

The fever came on the evening of the sixteenth, and the fever, when it comes, isn't a heat: it's a number. Thirty-nine and a half, then forty, then thirty-nine, and the numbers I counted with the thermometer, the way you count days: backwards, and without mercy. I did the test the day after, in a clinic that wasn't a clinic: it was a room with the bed and the test tube, and the result said *dengue*. Dengue. The word I knew from the guides, and the guides, in May, aren't read: they're put up with. Dengue is the fever of mosquitoes, and the mosquitoes, in May, are the mosquitoes of the monsoon, and the monsoon, in May, isn't chosen: it's crossed.

The ambulance took me to the hospital, and the hospital was the rich one: it was clean, and clean, in a hospital, is the thing you pay for. The room was two-fourteen, and the room had air conditioning, and the air conditioning, in room two-fourteen, cost. The drip was in the right arm, and the drip, at night, is the thing you don't take off: you keep it, like the cage. I was in room two-fourteen with the fever, and the fever, in the hospital, is the thing you count: you count it every four hours, with the thermometer, and thermometers say nothing: they say the numbers, and the numbers, in a hospital, are the numbers of your life.

At the reception desk they asked for the policy. They didn't ask it like that: they asked with the card, and the card said *insurance*. The policy had lapsed two months. I had saved thirty-nine euros a month, and the thirty-nine euros, in January, had seemed the wisest thing in the world. Saving, as always, you notice later: and later, in the hospital, was the later of someone who has the accounts in her hand and not the life. I said: — Expired. — The word was right and it wasn't the truth: the truth was that I had cut the line of the thirty-nine euros, like the flyers, and the thirty-nine euros, in May, cost thirty-two thousand baht.

The deposit was thirty-two thousand baht. Eight hundred and forty euros. I paid it with the card, and the card said *approved*, and *approved*, in a hospital, is the coldest thing: it's the number you pay, and the number, in a hospital, is the number of a life. I had done the accounts for seven months, and the accounts, in the hospital, I did for the last time: thirteen thousand five hundred euros saved to buy freedom, and freedom had cost me a test result. The test result, in a hospital, is the thing you keep: you don't use it, you show it. I still keep it.

The nurse was called Khun Fern, and Khun Fern was thirty, and English she knew little, like everyone. But Khun Fern, the second evening, said three words that weren't English: — Come stai? — The three words were Italian, and Italian, in room two-fourteen, was the farthest thing in the world: it was my language, said by someone else, and language, said by someone else, you don't hear: you hear it later, and later, in a hospital, is the later of someone who doesn't have the life. — Come stai? — Khun Fern said, and *come stai*, said by someone else, felt like the truest thing of the night. The three words she had learned for me: I knew it, and only I knew it. The things you learn for others, at night, are the things you don't forget: you keep them.

— How do you learn? — I asked her, the third evening, with the fever in my hand.

— From my patient — Khun Fern said, laughing softly. — An Italian lady, a year ago. She taught me *come stai*, and *grazie*, and *basta così*. —

*Basta così*. The three words were the three words of my life: *come stai*, *grazie*, *basta così*. The Italian lady, a year before, had been in room two-fourteen, or in a nearby room, and had taught the three words to the nurse, and the three words had arrived at me, a year later, the way true things arrive. All at once, and without being asked. The past, in a hospital, doesn't call: it arrives, by words, and the words of others, said for others, are the things you keep. I didn't know it. I knew it.

Martina's call came at three at night, which was one in the afternoon here, and Martina, on the phone, had the voice of someone who doesn't sleep: — Where are you? — she said. — You haven't answered in two days. —

— I'm in the hospital — I said. — I'm not well. I'm coming home. —

It was the first true thing I said on the phone in seven months, and the truth, on the phone, you feel the way you feel damp in walls: you don't see it. You feel it. Martina felt it, and didn't ask anything: heavy things aren't asked, and heavy things said for the first time, on the phone, are the things that aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

— When? — Martina said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who doesn't sleep.

— Next week — I said. — When I walk. —

Don't lie to yourself, the books say (Dostoevskij — Dostoevsky): those who lie to themselves and listen to their own lie come to a point where they can't distinguish the truth, inside or out. For seven months I had said *I'm great* to everyone, to myself, on the phone, on stories, and the lies, in the hospital, stop: they stop like the fever, and the fever, in the hospital, stops when it goes down, and when it goes down, the truth stays. The truth, in room two-fourteen, was one: I wasn't well. And the truth, said for the first time, on the phone, at three at night, felt like the heaviest thing in the world. The weight, though, was mine: it wasn't the dashboard, it wasn't the budget, it wasn't the stamp. It was mine, and my weight, at night, you don't take off: you keep it. I still keep it.

From the window of room two-fourteen, in the daytime, you saw the mountains, and the mountains, in May, were there, behind the monsoon, and the monsoon didn't let you see them. The postcard paradise, in May, wasn't there: there was the monsoon, and the monsoon, in May, isn't chosen: it's crossed. The days, in the hospital, weren't lived: they were crossed (Proust), and crossing, in room two-fourteen, is the thing you feel in the drips: you don't feel it. You notice later.

I cried the third evening, and crying, at thirty, I hadn't done for years: I did it the way you do the things you don't keep anymore. I didn't cry for the fever, and I didn't cry for the thirty-two thousand baht: I cried for the thing I had understood late, and I had understood it seven months late. I had saved thirteen thousand five hundred euros to buy freedom, and freedom isn't bought: you have it, or you don't, and you have it with the things you keep, and the things I kept, in the hospital, were one: Khun Fern's three words. Come stai. The three words were the thing you don't buy, and it's the secret of true things: they're enough, and when they're enough, you don't count them.

Patcha arrived the fourth evening, with the soup. The soup was jok, the sticky rice of the morning, and Patcha's soup tasted of those things you don't describe: you eat them. I ate it all, and for the first time in seven months I ate without counting: hunger, when it's real, you feel. Patcha didn't say *mai pen rai*. She only said: — You go home. You build. — And the sentence was the sentence of the book, and it wasn't the book: it was the sentence of a woman who had found it on her feet from five in the morning, and the discovery, in the hospital, with the soup in her hand, felt like the truest thing of the month.

Patcha's hands, that evening, I saw for the first time: they were the hands of someone who does everything, and the hands of someone who does everything, in the evening, are the hands that don't stop. Patcha's hands, in the hospital, felt like the truest thing in the country: they weren't beautiful, and they weren't rich. They were true. And true things, in a hospital, you don't buy: you see them, and when you see them, you don't forget.

The day after I called my mother.
