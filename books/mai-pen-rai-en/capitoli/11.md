# Chapter 11 — The Resignation from Paradise

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Chiang Mai (Hang Dong) · **Story time:** May 2026
**Words:** ~1.250 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/11.md` ✅

## Draft

I wrote the letter that same evening, in the Thai notebook, on page fifty, among the words I didn't know. I wrote it in Italian, and Italian, in the Thai notebook, was the farthest thing in the world: it was my language, come home. It said this:

*Dear Thailand,*

*I hereby resign from the role of inhabitant of paradise, with immediate effect.*

*Thank you for the sun, for the mango, for the sixty baht, for the pool, for the stamps, for other people's water.*

*Reason for resigning: there is nothing to do, and I am doing it.*

*While awaiting your mai pen rai, I send you my best regards.*

*Ludovica Acerbi, farang, thirty years old*

I didn't send the letter. Letters you don't send, usually, are the letters you keep: I still keep it, on page fifty, and I keep it the way you keep proofs: you don't use them, you show them. It was the truest letter I had written in years, and the truth, in letters, is the thing you don't send: you write it, and it stays.

I called my mother the morning after, at two at night there which was lunchtime here, and my mother, on the phone, had the voice of someone who doesn't sleep every evening for her daughters: — Mum — I said. — I'm coming home. —

My mother didn't ask why. True things aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay. She said: — All right, love. Come. — And the *come*, on the phone, at two at night, was the heaviest thing in the world: it was the house speaking, and the house, on the phone, says nothing: it says *come*, and *come*, in the morning, you don't take off: you keep it. — Your father says the garden is ready — my mother said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who doesn't sleep every evening for her daughters, and the garden, in Cesano Maderno, was the thing my father had after forty years of factory: the garden is a purpose. My father, with the garden, knew why he worked. And someone who knows why he works, on the phone, says the garden, and the garden, at two at night, felt like the truest thing.

— And what will you do, there? — my mother said, after a minute, with the voice of someone who asks true questions: without knowing it was the true one.

— Put my hands in something — I said. — In Milan. —

It was the first time in seven months I answered that question, and the answer was the smallest answer in the world: the hands, and something. Small answers, at thirty, are the answers you keep: the big ones are said, and stay in your hand. My mother didn't ask anything. True things aren't asked.

It was in that state of things — the letter written, the mother who said *come*, the garden ready — that the dominant thought came back. The dominant thought, the books say (Leopardi — from the *Zibaldone*), is the thing that makes you walk straight, and for seven months mine had been the nothing: nothing doesn't make you walk straight. Nothing lets go. The new thought was one, and it was simple: come home, and put my hands in something that gets built. Things that get built, in May, are the things you don't buy: you make. And the making, in May, was the thought that held my life: it held it the way the hedge holds the infinite.

The guesthouse photos I looked at all, in the evening, in one of those evenings: there were three hundred and forty, and the three hundred and forty were the photos I had taken, at six in the morning, for two weeks, and the three hundred and forty were the only thing made in seven months. Not spent: made. The difference, at thirty, is the difference of life: spent things, in May, are the things you count; made things, in May, are the things that stay. The three hundred and forty photos were of Patcha, and of Ploy, and of the plumeria, and of the morning soup, and Patcha's things, in the evening, were the truest things in the country: they were the things you don't buy. You see them.

I booked the flight on the nineteenth of May, and the flight was the return flight, and the return flight, at thirty, felt like the most adult thing: it wasn't adult. It was a flight. Flights, in May, are the flights you take, and mine was taken, and mine, for the first time in seven months, had a purpose: come home, and put my hands in something. The purpose wasn't big, and the purpose wasn't a plan: it was a thought. Thoughts, in May, are the things you keep.

I told Patcha in the evening, on the step, with her voice, the voice that says heavy things lightly:

— I'm going home — I said. — To Milan. —

Patcha didn't say *mai pen rai*. It was the first time. *Mai pen rai*, for small things, is said: it's the answer to everything, and the answer to everything answers nothing. For big things, there, it isn't said: it's done. Patcha said: — You go home. You build. — And the sentence was the sentence of the book, and it wasn't the book: it was the sentence of a woman who had found it on her feet from five in the morning, and the discovery, on the step, with the evening in her hand, felt like the truest thing of the month.

— See you in Milan — Ploy said, on the phone, laughing, and the sentence, at nineteen, isn't a joke: it's a hope. Hope, at nineteen, is the richest thing: she didn't know it.

In the evening, from the condo balcony, I looked at the stamp. The stamp was in the passport, and the passport was on the table, and the stamp said the exit: the twenty-ninth of May. The exit, at thirty, felt like the truest thing: for seven months the stamps had been the things that marked me, and the things that mark you, in May, you don't keep: you show. The last stamp, though, was different: it was the only one that said *I'm coming back*. For the first time, a stamp felt like a beginning.
