# Epilogue — The Fog

**POV:** Ludovica · **Place:** Milan (Sesto San Giovanni, via Borsieri) · **Story time:** autumn 2026
**Words:** ~1.300 / translation of `mai-pen-rai/capitoli/99-epilogo.md` ✅

---

## Draft

I have been in Milan for six months, and in Milan, in the morning, there is fog. I haven't left the fog anymore, and the fog, in the morning, is the most beautiful thing I have: I didn't know it, and I knew it only then, when I had left it. The lost paradise, the books say, is the paradise that we have lost (Proust), and my paradise had the smell of the fog, and the smell, in the morning, comes to me the way true things come: all at once, and without being asked.

I found the job in October, and the job isn't the one from before. I made the discount flyers, for an agency called Mercurio, and Mercury was the god of merchants and thieves, and it was the same god. Now I do communication and events for the museum of the factories of Sesto San Giovanni, and the museum is my father's: my father, in Cesano Maderno, has forty years of the Falck of Sesto San Giovanni, and the Falck, in the museum's books, is the factory that was a cage, but it was a factory. Inside there was a purpose, and the purpose, in the museum's books, is the thing you tell: you tell it to those who come, and the visitors come, and the visitors are the children of those who worked. My father, with the garden, knew why he worked. And someone who knows why he works, in the morning, doesn't feel the fog: the fog is the thing you cross, and crossing, in the morning, is the thing you do.

The job is on a contract, and the contract renews every year, and the year, at thirty, is the measure of life. The salary is fifteen hundred euros net, and the rent is nine hundred, and I left the gym, and the food is two hundred and sixty, and at the end of the month ninety euros are left. Ninety euros for a whole life, and it isn't little: it's the deal. You work to pay, and they leave you alone. The deal, at thirty, I know it, and I know it with Marcello's sentence in my hand: the factory is a cage, but it was a factory. The price of meaning, in the morning, you don't count: you pay. I pay it, and I pay it gladly, and the gladly, in the morning, is the richest thing I have.

On Sunday, at six, there is the pad thai with Martina, at the Thai restaurant on via Savona, which isn't the sixty-baht pad thai: it's the nine-euro pad thai. Nine euros, in Milan, for the same dish as always, with the same taste as always. I pay it gladly, and the gladly, on Sunday, is the richest thing: the price, at thirty, is never only a price. The price is the meaning, and the meaning, on Sunday, is Martina: Martina, at the pad thai, talks of the mortgage with the voice of someone who has just won something, and the mortgage, at thirty, is the house you win. She wins it, and she wins it for thirty years, and the house, on Sunday, is a purpose.

— And Christmas, this year? — Martina said, one of those Sundays, with the pad thai in her hand.

— In Milan — I said. — With you. —

It was the truth, and the truth, at the pad thai, you feel the way you feel damp in walls: you don't see it. You feel it. Martina felt it, and didn't ask anything: heavy things aren't asked, and the truths of friends, said for the first time, are the things that aren't asked: they're listened to, and they stay.

The Sunday calls have become true. I make them all, at six, and six, in Milan, is the most beautiful six: the rain, the pad thai, the phone call. Martina talks of the mortgage, and Sara talks of the wedding: the wedding in Ostia, in June, was full of things you put, and the *save the date*, on the phone, had arrived with email. I missed the wedding, and missing, at thirty, is the thing you don't get back: you repair. I repaired it with a letter, and the letter was the second one I didn't send, and letters you don't send, usually, are the letters you keep. Sara heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask: heavy things aren't asked. Giulia, in Berlin, talks of the contract, and the contract, at thirty, is the thing that renews: she renews it, and escaping, she says, is done well when you have a job and a plan. Giulia's escape is done well. Mine wasn't done well, and I knew it, and I know it: escapes without purpose, at thirty, are the escapes you pay back. I paid mine with a test result, and the test result, at thirty, is the thing you keep: you don't use it, you show.

Ploy arrived in Milan in September, and Ploy is nineteen, and the university, in Milan, is the thing Patcha pays on her feet from five in the morning. Ploy studies communication, like I studied, and the pad thai, for Ploy, is nine euros, and the nine euros, for Ploy, are the dearest thing: she didn't know it. At nineteen you believe that there it's cheap to live, and there it's cheap to live, and cheap, at nineteen, isn't the thing that holds: the thing that holds is the purpose. Ploy has the purpose: build something. She has it, and she has it with the hope in her hand, and hope, at nineteen, is the richest thing.

The small things, in Milan, have become the things you keep. The 8:11 M5, which is punctual like a sentence, I take every morning, and the sentence, at thirty, is the thing you cross: you cross it for a purpose. I still say *mai pen rai*, but I say it for the small things: the late bus, the rain, the mango that isn't there. For the big things I don't say it: big things you do. Big things, at thirty, are the things that get built, and the things that get built, in the morning, are the things you don't buy: you make.

In the West we work too much. Everyone says it, and it's partly true: the long hours, the rents that eat the salary, the food that goes up, the bills, the credits that go up like rents. I said it so loudly that I left everything, and I went there, where it's cheap to live, and I found the thing nobody writes on flyers: the problem was never the price. In the West we overpay for everything — it's true, and it isn't fair, and I don't say it to defend the nine hundred euros of via Borsieri: I say it because the nine hundred euros of via Borsieri are the price of meaning, and meaning, in the morning, is the thing you pay gladly. There, without a purpose, the days melt and nobody gathers them, and nothing, at thirty, isn't freedom: it's the opposite of freedom.

We work too much, but we have a purpose, and a purpose is a hedge, not a cage. It tells you where to look, and the infinite you make yourself (Leopardi, *L'infinito*). My hedge, at thirty, is the fog, and the fog, in the morning, is the most beautiful thing I have. In the West we work too much, but we know why. And those who know why they work, in the end — I write it now, and I write it for the first time — are well.

I am writing a book, in the evening, at the desk of the studio, with the fog at the window and the cold pad thai. I write it for the first time, and the first time, at thirty, is the richest thing: I didn't know it. The book is called *Mai pen rai*, and *mai pen rai*, at thirty, is the answer to everything, and the answer to everything answers nothing. I write it, and I write it for the small things: small things, in the evening, are the things you keep.

And the things you keep, in the evening, you don't count. You keep them.
