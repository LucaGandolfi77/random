# *Mai pen rai*

**Author:** [Name] · **Genre:** contemporary literary fiction · **Translated from Italian**

*To those who have thought of leaving everything.*


# Prologue — The Queue


> "the only true paradise is always the paradise that we have lost."
>
> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

The immigration office in Chiang Mai opens at eight in the morning, and by ten past eight the queue already reaches the gate. You stand in line with your passport in your hand, the way you hold a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of your own life, and that the numbers are never good. In front of me was a Frenchman who had married here, and behind me a couple of English pensioners who were discussing the cost of fruit. The floor was of clean tiles, and there was the smell of disinfectant, which smelled of order; and under the smell of disinfectant there was the smell of everyone who has been waiting for hours, which is the smell of tiredness left lying around. I knew that smell in Milan: it was the smell of Monday mornings on the underground, and I hadn't smelled it for seven months.

My number was forty-two. On the display, at the top, the numbers that were called: A037, B014, C009. I waited for mine with the feeling of waiting for a verdict. The passport was full of stamps: sixty days, then thirty, then sixty again, then the extension, then the ninety-day report. A stamp that stamps your life in instalments, and the instalments aren't yours to choose. In Milan I had a contract that renewed every six months, and I felt like a temporary worker. Here the stamps lasted thirty days, and I felt even more temporary: I was never inside anything, neither there nor here. You can be temporary in two places for seven months, and it felt like the exact opposite of freedom. In Milan the cage, at least, had taut bars: you knocked against them, and knocking is something you can feel. Here there were no bars: there was the stamp, and you don't knock against the stamp. The stamp marks you, and passes.

I came here because it's cheap to live. I used to say it to everyone in Milan, with the confidence of someone who has the numbers: the sixty-baht pad thai — one euro sixty —, the rent for a condominium with a pool at nine thousand baht — two hundred and fifty euros —, life at a third of the cost. Two years before I had been here on holiday, two weeks, and I had seen the markets, the mangoes, the people who smile, and I had done the math on my phone, on the boat, in the sun: thirteen thousand five hundred euros put aside in five years, almost fifteen months of life at flyer prices. I had thought that paradise rents out, and that there the rent costs less than the rent in Milan. It felt like a calculation. It was a miscalculation.

Because the calculation counted everything, except the main thing: that there, without a job and without a purpose, the days don't add up. They melt. In Milan my days cost me everything — the rent, the gym, the food, the bills — and they were mine. Here my days cost me almost nothing, and they belonged to nobody. Nobody waited for them, nobody bought them, nobody paid for them. Money counted out on the table of paradise, and nobody touches it: it is the loneliest thing in the world.

The number was called: A042, counter seven. The woman behind the glass took the passport, looked at the stamps one by one, the way you read test results, and asked me the purpose of my stay. The purpose of my stay. I stood there, holding the question, and I had nothing to answer that wasn't true.

— Tourist — I said.

It was the right word and it wasn't the truth. After seven months I wasn't a tourist, and I wasn't a resident, and I wasn't anything that has a name on a form. The woman stamped, handed me the passport back, and the stamp said thirty days. Thirty more days, and I didn't know what to do with them.

I went out, and outside there was the sun, high and white, the sun that knew how to be there for everyone. Two years before, on holiday, that sun had seemed to me the sun of freedom. That morning it seemed to me the sun of a car park.

Paradise, the books say, is the paradise that we have lost (Proust). I had arrived in paradise, and I was losing everything else: the language, the money, my people, the meaning. I didn't even eat the sixty-baht pad thai anymore: I looked at it on menus, and the calculation from two years before seemed to me the calculation of someone who had never been there.

This is the story of seven months, and of how a girl from Milan resigned from her own life so she would stop overpaying, and discovered that paradise has a queue, and that the queue is made of people like her.

I begin at the beginning, which was Milan, and it cost nine hundred euros a month.

# Chapter 1 — The Billed Hours


> "Ivan Ilyich's life was the most simple, the most ordinary, and therefore the most terrible."
>
> — Leo Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*

The 8:11 M5 was full as always, and full, in Milan, isn't called full: it's called normal. I got on at Isola with the pass that beeped and the bag on my shoulder, and from the bag stuck out the strap of the computer that touched people: in Milan you touch people every morning, and people touch you, and nobody says anything, as is their habit, the metros: you stand close without knowing each other, and it's fine, because knowing each other in Milan costs. At the Garibaldi stop got on the clothes I didn't know, and clothes, in Milan, you know them all: you see them on stories, on flyers, in shop windows. I wore the clothes of always, and the clothes of always, at twenty-nine, are the clothes you don't look at: you wear them.

The agency was called Mercurio, and it was in via Tortona, upstairs from a lamp show. The founder had chosen the name — he had studied the Greeks — and he had chosen it well without knowing: Mercury was the god of merchants and of thieves, and it was the same god. In our trade the two businesses happened every day, and nobody noticed.

The floor was an open space of thirty-two desks, and the thirty-two desks were thirty-two people with the computer on, all talking at the same time, softly, as is their habit, the agencies: you speak softly because shouting tires you, and tiredness, in agencies, is the only thing you don't bill. There was the usual noise: the voices, the keyboards, the hum of the computers, and every so often a laugh — one, stifled at once, as is their habit, the laughs in agencies: you can't, they last too long.

My trade was billing hours. On the screen, top right, there was the dashboard with my numbers: hours billed, hours to bill, variance. Fifteen hundred a year, which is the measure of a modern life: it's measured in hours, and the hours are measured in money, and the money is measured in what's left. At the end of the month, of my salary — one thousand four hundred and eighty euros net — almost nothing was left: the rent for the studio on via Borsieri took nine hundred, the gym forty-nine, the pass thirty-nine, the food two hundred and sixty, the bills ninety-five, the phone ten. Ninety euros were left. Ninety euros for a whole life, and it wasn't little: it was the deal. You work to pay, and they leave you alone.

The client was a discount supermarket called IperRisparmio, and we made the flyers. Flyers, in Milan, are a trade: they're made by people with a degree, for people who don't read them. The thirty per cent discount, the yellow tag, the photo of the roast chicken. I spent my days lowering prices on paper, and my life raised them. It felt like a scam: the prices on paper went down, the prices of my life went up, and I was in the middle, billing both. The roast chicken, on the flyer, I looked at it every morning, and the chicken, on the flyer, cost seven ninety, and the chicken, in my life, cost nine twenty. That too was the deal: the prices on the flyer aren't the prices. Everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

The creative director was called Renzo, and his trade was presenting. He presented everything: ideas, agencies, himself. He had a way of entering rooms that was a trade in itself: he entered, and the room became his. The ideas that were born at the desks were born from the team, and the team was Renzo, and the ideas that were born from me were born from the team. I had been at Mercurio five years and my ideas were Renzo's, and I didn't even notice: habit is an anaesthesia, it puts your days to sleep one by one (Proust), and you wake only when you've finished sleeping your life.

I had lived five years as one should: project contract renewed every six months, the overtime you don't ask for, the extra hours that don't get billed. I was a woman like any other, living as she should (Tolstoy), and the woman like any other, in books, dies on the bed of an apartment she has paid for all her life. I wasn't dying. I was billing. It's different, and it's nothing different.

At noon I ate at my desk with a sandwich from the bar downstairs, four euros fifty, and the rest of the sandwich I threw away. In Milan you throw away food too, with the same ease with which you throw away hours: the two things you can't keep, and nobody keeps them. The sandwich, besides, I ate for the same reasons I looked at flyers: to fill the hours, not to live them. I didn't feel hunger: hunger, in agencies, is a detail you don't bill.

I was, though, someone who had studied. I had left the Statale with a degree in Classics, a thesis on Leopardi, and the Ancient Greek I still knew, the way you know the things that aren't needed: perfectly. On the shelf at home there was the Zibaldone, and I had opened it for the last time for the thesis, four years before. Every so often I passed by it and didn't touch it, as is their habit, books: you keep them, you don't use them. There was, though, one thing I remembered, and I remembered it well, because I had underlined it in pencil, at twenty-two, without knowing I would need it: that every human being has a thought that governs them (Leopardi, Zibaldone), and that the dominant thought isn't a cage: it's the thing that makes you walk straight. My dominant thought, at twenty-nine, was the calculation: how much it costs, how much is left, how much is missing. And the calculation isn't a thought: it's an account. Accounts don't make you walk straight. Accounts keep you still.

The thing I didn't say to anyone was Thailand. Two years before, in August, I had been there on holiday, two weeks, with a friend from university who later went back. There I had seen the life you don't see in Milan: the sixty-baht pad thai — one euro sixty —, the rent for a condominium with a pool at nine thousand baht — two hundred and fifty euros —, the mango you buy at the market for half of nothing. People smiled, and the sun set at half past six, and everything cost half of nothing. I had done the math on my phone, on the boat, in the sun, and the math said a thing I hadn't said to anyone, not even to myself, in two years: that there you could live on what I had put aside, for months. I didn't say it. The things you think at sea, usually, you forget: it was the only one I had kept. I kept it in a pocket of my phone, the way you keep rare things: I didn't use it, I knew it.

It was on Sunday evening, in that state of things — no headset, but the dashboard was there, and the calculation was there, and the flyers were us — that Martina's call came.

The phone rang twice and on the display it said *Martina*. I took the phone the way you take the phone on Sunday evening: without suspicion, with the hand that was looking for the remote. Martina was thirty, she was an analyst at a bank, she trained at six thirty in the morning, and she had just signed the mortgage for a two-room flat in Città Studi. We were three, on Sundays: Martina in Milan, Sara in Rome — she had won a civil service exam at the Revenue Agency, and stability, she said, was a religion —, Giulia in Berlin, who was an engineer at a startup and earned sixty-five thousand euros a year, and escaping, she said, is done well when you have a job and a plan. I was the fourth, and the fourth was me: the one who had studied the Greeks and billed hours.

— So — Martina said —, the mortgage is signed. Thirty years. I swear it, come on. —

— Thirty years — I said.

It was the measure of a life, and I said it the way you say measures: without thinking. Martina spoke of the mortgage with the voice of someone who has just won something, and she had won something: she had won thirty years of a house, and the house was hers, and the mortgage was hers, and everything was in her name. I had nothing in my name, except the calculation. The calculation was mine, and it was the calculation of someone who has nothing: how much it costs, how much is left, how much is missing.

— There's a gym near the house — Martina said. — At six thirty there's the class that isn't crowded. I joined: nineteen euros a month, a discount, come on. —

A discount. In Milan discounts, like credits, go up every month: it's the thing you don't discuss. Martina had a job that was a purpose, a mortgage that was a purpose, a gym that was a purpose: three purposes, and three purposes make a life. I had none, and no purpose, on a Sunday evening, pretends to be fine.

— And how are you? — Martina said.

— I'm great — I said.

It was the first thing I said to myself that wasn't true.

# Chapter 2 — The Thirty Per Cent


> "Excessive consciousness is a disease, a real, thoroughgoing disease."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*

That was the month of the thirty per cent campaign. IperRisparmio had decided to lower everything, and we lowered everything on paper: the prices, the tags, the photos. I spent my days writing *-30%* in big letters, and the thirty per cent was the number of the month: I wrote it on presentations, on flyers, on stickers. The thirty per cent, written enough times, looks like a trade.

I had written the concept on a Tuesday, at my desk, at seven in the evening, when the office was empty and the extra hours didn't get billed: it was taken for granted. It had come all at once, the way true things come: I hadn't thought it, I had written it. It said that people don't want low prices: they want the pleasure of low prices, and pleasure isn't calculated, it's shown. I had written *-30%* next to a roast chicken, and below, in small, *the pleasure of paying less*. To myself, that evening, I said nothing. But I had written *the pleasure of paying less*, and the sentence, reread the next day, seemed written by someone else.

At the client meeting there was the thing I no longer tell anyone, because by now I tell it only here. The room was the usual one: the long table, the seats, the projector. The client had said he liked the campaign, that the concept was strong, that they had finally understood people. The concept was mine. I had written it at seven in the evening, and at seven in the evening there was nobody: so it had been born from the team. And the team was Renzo. Renzo presented it with his voice, the voice of someone who presents, entering the room the way you take things: without asking. The client said *well done everyone*, and the *well done everyone* was for Renzo, and Renzo didn't correct it. Nobody ever corrected it. In Milan credits, like rents, go up every month: it's the thing you don't discuss.

That same week there was the budget meeting. The numbers were mine, I made them, and the numbers said that to do the thirty per cent you had to cut. We cut where we could: you always cut where you can, and it's where it doesn't count. The thing that got cut, that week, was me: they hired an intern at six hundred euros a month, and the intern would do my part. They told her to sit next to me, and for two weeks I taught her the trade they were taking from me: the papers, the tags, the lists. The intern was called Giorgia, she was twenty-two, and she studied communication, like I had studied. She was me, twenty-two years before, at the price of six hundred euros. I cost one thousand four hundred and eighty. The difference was seven hundred and eighty euros a month, and the difference, in the budget, was a saving.

I trained my own replacement. I say it like this, because it's like this: I told her where the files were, how you make yellow tags, what the client wants. She said *thank you*, and the *thank you* was sincere, and it was the cruellest thing: the sincerity of people who are taking your place without knowing it. The third week Giorgia made the tags alone, and Giorgia's tags were mine, made by someone else, at someone else's price. In Milan people go down in price too, and nobody writes it on the flyer.

It was on a Friday, in that state of things — the stolen concept, the trained replacement, the unbilled extra hours — that the email came. The email was from the building administrator, and it said that from January the rent would go up by one hundred and twenty euros. One hundred and twenty euros a month, for the same studio as always, with the same window onto the inner courtyard. In Milan rents go up like credits: it's the same thing, done to people. And the market values of the neighbourhood, read twice, seemed to me the values of a merchant: Mercury again, fixing things without asking.

That evening I did the shopping at the supermarket downstairs, and the receipt said double. Not double of nothing: double of two years before. The same shopping, the same products, double. I looked at the receipt for a minute, the way you look at rare things, and the receipt was a rarest thing: it was the proof. I kept it. It's still here, in a pocket of a notebook, and I keep it the way you keep proofs: you don't use them, you show them.

That night I did the calculation. Excessive consciousness is a disease (Dostoevsky), and I had been sick for years: I did the calculation every evening, on the apps, on the receipts, on the flyers. That night I did it for a different reason: not to know how much was missing, but to know how much was left. Thirteen thousand five hundred euros were left. Thirteen thousand five hundred euros put aside in five years, living the reduced version of myself: the gym suspended, the discount shopping, dinners at home, presents made with reason. Thirteen thousand five hundred euros: at nine hundred and twenty euros a month — the new rent, life over there — that was almost fifteen months. Almost fifteen months of life, at flyer prices.

It seemed a better deal. In Milan the deal was: you work to pay, and they leave you alone. There the deal would have been: you don't work, and you're fine. Choosing between two deals isn't freedom: it's changing masters. But in the moment I did it, it seemed like freedom. I didn't say it to myself with these words: I said it with the app, booking the flight.

I handed in my resignation on a Friday, the way you hand in resignations: with the closed envelope and the hand that doesn't shake. The letter said I was leaving for "personal reasons", and personal reasons, in resignation letters, are the reasons you can't say: the stolen concept, the trained replacement, the hundred and twenty euros, the receipt. Renzo said it was a shame, that I had potential, that if I wanted to... He said the things people say, and none of them were true. Giorgia said *good luck*, and her *good luck* was sincere, and it was the cruellest thing of the day.

The friends' reactions all came, and they were all different. Martina said *are you sure?*, and repeated it three times, the way you repeat the things you don't understand: at twenty-nine, leaving a job that pays without having another, usually, isn't done. Sara said *but the CV?*, and she was right: a CV that stops at twenty-nine is a CV you don't take up again — I knew it, and she knew it, and neither of us said the rest. Giulia said *what will you do?*, and the question was the true one: in Berlin she had a job and a plan, and escaping, she said, is done well when you have a job and a plan. I had neither. I had thirteen thousand five hundred euros and a flight.

Happy lives, on stories, are all alike (Tolstoj — Tolstoy): the gym at six, the Sunday brunch, the dog, the sunset seen from a terrace that rents at a high price. Mine resembled nobody's: there was the sea over there, and there was nothing of mine. In Recanati, once, a boy climbed a hill on a Village Saturday (Leopardi — *Il sabato del villaggio*) and happiness was in the waiting: the Saturday that aims, the week that aims at Saturday. My friends' lives all aimed: at the mortgage, at the exam, at the contract. Mine aimed at nowhere anymore. It seemed like a victory.

There is, though, one thing I must say here, because it's the thing I understood late, and I understood it seven months late: a man, in books, did the most stupid things on purpose, just to prove to himself he was free (Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*). I resigned on purpose. Not for a better job, not for a love, not for a plan: to prove to myself I could. It seemed like a victory. It seemed like a victory for three weeks, which is the time it takes to understand that victories without an opponent aren't won: they're given, and they stay in your hand.

The flight left on the twelfth of October, from Malpensa. I still had six months to turn thirty. I would turn thirty in Thailand, but I didn't know that: only the flight knew it, and the flight says nothing.

# Chapter 3 — Sixty Baht


I left on the twelfth of October, from Malpensa, and at Malpensa there was fog. The fog of Milan had been with me every morning for five years, and that morning it was letting go, and I didn't know it was the last time for seven months. The flight was one-way, and a one-way flight, at the check-in desk, is the thing you're not asked about: you give it. The woman at the desk looked at the ticket, looked at me, and didn't ask about the return flight. The things you're not asked about, in the morning, are the heaviest things: you feel them, the way you feel damp in walls.

The plane took off, and the fog stayed below, and I looked at the fog from the window with the feeling of looking at a thing I didn't know I would miss. Fog, in Milan, is the thing you don't choose: you put up with it. And the things you don't choose, usually, are the things that keep you. Eleven hours of flight, and the eleven hours I counted, the way you count hours: one by one, and none of them got billed. It was the first thing in five years that didn't get billed, and the not billing, for someone who bills, is a freedom I didn't know: it seemed like a holiday.

Bangkok arrived at night, with a heat that isn't heat: it's a wall. The wet heat, at night, takes you the way things take you: without asking. I took the night train to Chiang Mai, and the train left at seven twenty, and it took twelve hours, and I slept in a berth with the noise of the rails, and the noise of the rails, at night, is the gentlest noise in the world: it tells you you're going somewhere. For five years in Milan I wasn't going anywhere: I was going to work. It's different. It seemed different.

Chiang Mai arrived at seven in the morning, and the old city presented itself the way old cities present themselves: with temples, low walls, sandy streets and dogs. The heat was a wall in the morning too, but it was a wall that didn't jostle you: it hugged you. It seemed like a wall that isn't there. I walked through the city with the backpack on, and the backpack was heavy, and for the first time in five years the weight was mine: it wasn't the dashboard, it wasn't the budget, it wasn't the thirty per cent. It was a backpack, and the weight of backpacks, when you no longer have the things that weigh, you choose.

The dogs slept on the streets, and dogs that sleep on streets, in the morning, sleep the way things sleep: for real. At the temples there were the monks, and the monks, in the morning, passed with bare shoulders and bowls for alms, and people put rice, and rice, in the morning, is the thing you give: you give without asking. For five years in Milan I had given nothing: I received a salary, and a salary isn't rice. Rice is given in the morning, and the morning, there, began at six.

The guesthouse was called Patcha's, because Patcha was the owner. Patcha was forty-seven, small, short-haired, with the hands of someone who does everything: cleans, cooks, books, answers. She greeted me with her hands together in front of her chest, which is the wai, and the wai is the greeting you make with your head bowed, and a bowed head, in the morning, is the gentlest thing: it tells you the other is lowering herself, and lowering herself for you. Nobody had lowered themselves to me for years, in Milan. Doors opened, but heads didn't bow.

The room was on the first floor, with a fan instead of air conditioning, and the fan cost half of nothing. On the veranda there was plumeria, and under the plumeria there was the smell. The smell was of flowers and rice, the rice of the morning that Patcha made for breakfast, and the smell came the way true things come: all at once, and without being asked. The past doesn't call: it arrives, by smell (Proust), and the smell took me two years back, on holiday, on the boat, in the sun, with the friend from university who hadn't yet decided to go back. Two years before I had smelled the smell, and I had forgotten it; and the smell, this time, told me the past isn't forgotten: you keep it, and it comes back when it's needed. It seemed a beautiful thing. It was a thing I didn't know: it was the past coming back, and the past comes back even when it isn't needed.

Breakfast was rice and soup, and the soup was Patcha's, and Patcha's soup tasted of those things you don't describe: you eat them. I ate it all, and for the first time in years I ate without looking at the phone: hunger, when it's real, you feel it, and in Milan I hadn't felt hunger for years. I ate for the hours. There I ate from hunger.

The pad thai I ate in the evening, at the night market, from a woman who made it in front of me. Sixty baht. One euro sixty. I had waited two years for it, and I had thought about it every evening in Milan, and I thought about it the way you think about things you don't have: perfectly. Pleasure comes only when desire is already dead (Leopardi — from the *Zibaldone*), and the pad thai came at the end, and it tasted of a spent desire. It was good. It was good the way good things are good: and it wasn't it. The pad thai I had thought about for two years was better than the pad thai in front of me, and the thing felt like a discovery: that the things you think about, when they arrive, arrive late. Desire dies waiting, and pleasure arrives at the dead. In Milan desire was the new rent, and the new rent had arrived, and it wasn't it. Here is what I had come to look for, and I didn't know: not the sixty-baht pad thai. The pleasure of living desire. And the pleasure of living desire, there as here, you can't buy: you have it, or you don't.

The language was the hardest thing in the country. Thai has five tones, and the tones are the five ways of saying the same thing, and the same thing, said with the wrong tones, is another thing. *Mai* means *new*, and it means *no*, and the difference is a tone: a wave that rises, a wave that falls. At the market, the first time, I asked for a mango with the wrong tone, and I said something that wasn't a mango: the woman laughed, and the laugh was kind, and I got the mango anyway, at double price. The double price was the farang price, and the farang price, in the morning, I didn't know yet: I only knew the mango cost double, and I didn't know why. I knew Ancient Greek, and I knew the thesis, and I didn't know how to say *ice*. The things you study, when they aren't needed, you know perfectly: it's their secret.

In the evening, on the wall of the old city, I thought about men read in books: in Petersburg, once, people walked alone at night, with nobody waiting for them, and they were called dreamers (Dostoevsky, *White Nights*). I was a dreamer, and I didn't know it: for two years, in Milan, I had planned this life from my phone, from the boat, from the sofa: the rent there, the mango, the pad thai, the sun. You plan a life you don't live, and it's called a dream, and the dream, in Milan, cost nine hundred euros a month. In Petersburg the dreamers walked, and they didn't even have a phone: they only had the night. I had the night, and the phone, and the plan. The plan wasn't the problem. I didn't know it yet: the plan, without a purpose, is a room that rents out long.

In the evening, coming back to the guesthouse, Patcha was on the step, and she was smiling, and the smile was the usual one.

— Mai pen rai — she said, without my asking, and without my having asked anything. I had said *sorry* for the noise of the door, or for the mango, or for nothing.

Mai pen rai. It doesn't matter. The answer to everything. I didn't yet know it was the answer to everything, and that the answer to everything answers nothing.

# Chapter 4 — The Stamps


Thirty days always run out, and it's the thing nobody says about paradise: that it runs out, and it runs out fast, and that the hurry isn't the calendar's, it's life's. My thirty-day exemption expired on the twelfth of November, and the fear came with the app, which counted my remaining days every morning the way you count the days left to sentences: backwards, and without mercy. In Milan I had a contract that renewed every six months, and the renewal, in Milan, felt like the most anguishing thing in the world: six months of life, renewed in instalments, always halfway. Here the thing was called a stamp, and it lasted thirty days, and it felt even more anguishing: not six months. One.

The stamp, moreover, isn't asked for: it's put up with. In the weeks, the guesthouse told me the visa could be "fixed", and fixing, in Thailand, is a trade: there are people who do it, for three thousand five hundred baht.

Somchai was the name of the guy who fixed things. He wasn't the one who called it that: the guesthouse called him that, and the guesthouse was right. Somchai was forty, had a six-metre office with a printer, and fixed things for three thousand five hundred baht: the extensions, the forms, the photographs taken in a hurry. The photographs for the stamp, with the white background and the face that doesn't smile. In Milan I knew the face that doesn't smile: it was the badge photo, and the badge photo was the same for five years.

— Visa run — Somchai said, and with his hand he drew a line that left the country and came back: a circle, drawn by a finger, which was my life. — Cheaper than a new visa. And the new stamp, on the way back, is free. —

Free. In Milan nothing was free, not even the air: air, in Milan, you pay for with your lungs. There the stamp was free, and free, for someone who had overpaid for everything, is a word I didn't know: it seemed like a promise. It was a trick: the free stamp holds you for thirty days, and the thirty days, when they run out, you redo. Free is the longest instalment in the world.

The bus to Penang left at eight in the evening, and it took twelve hours, and the border arrived in the morning, with the queue. The queue at borders is the oldest queue in the world: it was there before borders, and it will be after. I stood in line with the passport in my hand, and the passport was full of stamps, and the stamps all said the same thing: you are here, but you are not from here. The bus was one of those buses you don't choose: with the seats that don't recline, the air conditioning at eleven degrees, and people who sleep the way things sleep: for real. I slept too, two hours, with my head on the window, and the window, at night, was freezing, and the cold, in a hot country, is the thing you don't expect: you put up with it.

The Sunday calls have become true. I make them all, at six, and six, in Milan, is the most beautiful six: the rain, the pad thai, the phone call. Martina talks of the mortgage, and Sara talks of the wedding: the wedding in Ostia, in June, was full of things you put, and the *save the date*, on the phone, had arrived with email. I missed the wedding, and missing, at thirty, is the thing you don't get back: you repair. I repaired it with a letter, and the letter was the second one I didn't send, and letters you don't send, usually, are the letters you keep. Sara heard it, or didn't hear it, and didn't ask: heavy things aren't asked. Giulia, in Berlin, talks of the contract, and the contract, at thirty, is the thing that renews: she renews it, and escaping, she says, is done well when you have a job and a plan. Giulia's escape is done well. Mine wasn't done well, and I knew it, and I know it: escapes without purpose, at thirty, are the escapes you pay back. I paid mine with a test result, and the test result, at thirty, is the thing you keep: you don't use it, you show.

Ploy arrived in Milan in September, and Ploy is nineteen, and the university, in Milan, is the thing Patcha pays on her feet from five in the morning. Ploy studies communication, like I studied, and the pad thai, for Ploy, is nine euros, and the nine euros, for Ploy, are the dearest thing: she didn't know it. At nineteen you believe that there it's cheap to live, and there it's cheap to live, and cheap, at nineteen, isn't the thing that holds: the thing that holds is the purpose. Ploy has the purpose: build something. She has it, and she has it with the hope in her hand, and hope, at nineteen, is the richest thing.

The small things, in Milan, have become the things you keep. The 8:11 M5, which is punctual like a sentence, I take every morning, and the sentence, at thirty, is the thing you cross: you cross it for a purpose. I still say *mai pen rai*, but I say it for the small things: the late bus, the rain, the mango that isn't there. For the big things I don't say it: big things you do. Big things, at thirty, are the things that get built, and the things that get built, in the morning, are the things you don't buy: you make.

In the West we work too much. Everyone says it, and it's partly true: the long hours, the rents that eat the salary, the food that goes up, the bills, the credits that go up like rents. I said it so loudly that I left everything, and I went there, where it's cheap to live, and I found the thing nobody writes on flyers: the problem was never the price. In the West we overpay for everything — it's true, and it isn't fair, and I don't say it to defend the nine hundred euros of via Borsieri: I say it because the nine hundred euros of via Borsieri are the price of meaning, and meaning, in the morning, is the thing you pay gladly. There, without a purpose, the days melt and nobody gathers them, and nothing, at thirty, isn't freedom: it's the opposite of freedom.

We work too much, but we have a purpose, and a purpose is a hedge, not a cage. It tells you where to look, and the infinite you make yourself (Leopardi, *L'infinito*). My hedge, at thirty, is the fog, and the fog, in the morning, is the most beautiful thing I have. In the West we work too much, but we know why. And those who know why they work, in the end — I write it now, and I write it for the first time — are well.

I am writing a book, in the evening, at the desk of the studio, with the fog at the window and the cold pad thai. I write it for the first time, and the first time, at thirty, is the richest thing: I didn't know it. The book is called *Mai pen rai*, and *mai pen rai*, at thirty, is the answer to everything, and the answer to everything answers nothing. I write it, and I write it for the small things: small things, in the evening, are the things you keep.

And the things you keep, in the evening, you don't count. You keep them.

## A note on the allusions

The protagonist's reflections contain subtle allusions to Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Proust and Leopardi, marked in parentheses: they are not quotations — they are ways of thinking borrowed, the way languages are borrowed. The epigraphs at the start of the chapters are real quotations: Marcel Proust (*Time Regained*), Leo Tolstoy (*The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, *War and Peace*), Fyodor Dostoevsky (*Notes from Underground*, *The Brothers Karamazov*, *The Idiot*), Giacomo Leopardi (*L'infinito*, *A Silvia*, *The Village Saturday*, *Zibaldone*). *Mai pen rai* (Thai: it doesn't matter) is the answer to everything.
