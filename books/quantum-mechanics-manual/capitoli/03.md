# Chapter 3 — Angular Momentum and Spin: The Algebra of Rotations

**Level:** Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive the algebra of angular momentum operators, solve the eigenvalue problem for \( \hat{L}^2 \) and \( \hat{L}_z \), extend the algebra to spin-1/2, and connect the mathematical structure to the physical interpretation of rotational symmetry.

---

## 3.1 The Angular Momentum Operators: Commutation Relations

In quantum mechanics, the orbital angular momentum operator is:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{L}} = \hat{\mathbf{r}} \times \hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar \, \mathbf{r} \times \nabla
\]

The components satisfy the canonical commutation relations:

\[
[\hat{L}_i, \hat{L}_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{L}_k
\]

where \( \epsilon_{ijk} \) is the Levi-Civita symbol. This algebra is the Lie algebra \( \mathfrak{so}(3) \), the infinitesimal generators of three-dimensional rotations. The commutation relations are not postulated. They are derived from the canonical commutation relations \( [\hat{x}_i, \hat{p}_j] = i\hbar \delta_{ij} \) and the definition of \( \hat{L} \).

The total angular momentum squared is:

\[
\hat{L}^2 = \hat{L}_x^2 + \hat{L}_y^2 + \hat{L}_z^2
\]

It commutes with each component: \( [\hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_i] = 0 \). This means that there exists a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates \( |\ell, m\rangle \) such that:

\[
\hat{L}^2 |\ell, m\rangle = \hbar^2 \ell(\ell + 1) |\ell, m\rangle
\]

\[
\hat{L}_z |\ell, m\rangle = \hbar m |\ell, m\rangle
\]

with \( \ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots \) and \( m = -\ell, -\ell + 1, \dots, \ell \). The derivation uses the ladder operators:

\[
\hat{L}_\pm = \hat{L}_x \pm i \hat{L}_y
\]

with the properties:

\[
[\hat{L}_z, \hat{L}_\pm] = \pm \hbar \hat{L}_\pm, \quad [\hat{L}_+, \hat{L}_-] = 2\hbar \hat{L}_z
\]

By requiring that \( \hat{L}_\pm |\ell, m\rangle \propto |\ell, m \pm 1\rangle \) and that the norm must be non-negative, one derives the quantization conditions on \( \ell \) and \( m \). This derivation is exact. It demonstrates that angular momentum quantization is not an empirical fact, but a mathematical consequence of rotational symmetry.

## 3.2 Spin-1/2: The Pauli Matrices and the Two-Level System

Spin is not orbital angular momentum. It is an intrinsic property of particles, with no classical analog. For spin-1/2, the algebra is the same as \( \mathfrak{su}(2) \), but the representations are different. The spin operators are:

\[
\hat{S}_i = \frac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_i
\]

where \( \sigma_i \) are the Pauli matrices:

\[
\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
\]

The eigenvalues of \( \hat{S}_z \) are \( \pm \hbar/2 \). The eigenstates are:

\[
|\uparrow\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |\downarrow\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
\]

The spin state of a particle is a linear combination:

\[
|\psi\rangle = c_\uparrow |\uparrow\rangle + c_\downarrow |\downarrow\rangle
\]

with \( |c_\uparrow|^2 + |c_\downarrow|^2 = 1 \). The expectation value of the spin vector is:

\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf{S}} \rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2} \langle \psi | \boldsymbol{\sigma} | \psi \rangle
\]

The spin is the claim that the particle remembers a direction — not in space, but in an abstract two-dimensional space. The claim is settled not by observation in three dimensions, but by measurement in this abstract space. The observer does not see the spin. The observer measures it — through the interaction with a magnetic field, through the Stern-Gerlach experiment, through the claim that the particle holds a memory of orientation that is not visible, but real.

## 3.3 Rotational Symmetry and Noether's Theorem

Noether's theorem states that every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity. For rotational invariance, the conserved quantity is the angular momentum. The derivation uses the Euler-Lagrange equations with an infinitesimal rotation \( \delta \mathbf{r} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \times \mathbf{r} \). The change in the Lagrangian is:

\[
\delta \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{r}} \cdot (\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \times \mathbf{r}) + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}} \cdot (\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \times \dot{\mathbf{r}})
\]

Using the equation of motion, this simplifies to a total time derivative, and the conserved quantity is:

\[
\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}
\]

This is not an approximation. It is the exact connection between symmetry and conservation, the claim that the universe does not prefer any direction, and that this indifference produces the conservation of angular momentum — the claim that holds orbits together, the claim that keeps planets in their paths, the claim that measures not only motion, but the relationship between motion and the space in which it occurs.

## 3.4 Literary Reflection: Tolstoy's Patience and the Angular Momentum — The Garden of Rotation

Tolstoy writes that great events are caused by the accumulation of small actions. The conservation of angular momentum is the same — not caused by a single force, but by the accumulation of rotational symmetry, by the patience of the claim that the universe does not prefer any direction. The orbit is not a command. It is the result of this patience — the patience of space, the patience of time, the patience of the claim that holds the star in its path.

The spin-1/2 particle is the deepest form of this patience. It does not rotate in space. It holds the memory of rotation in an abstract space — a space that has no direction in the physical world, but has direction in the structure of the claim. The observer measures this memory not by seeing it, but by interacting with it — through the magnetic field, through the claim that the particle holds a direction that is not visible, but real.

And so the angular momentum is not only a mathematical object. It is the measure of the universe's patience — the claim that the universe holds its directions, its orientations, its memories, whether we observe them or not. The claim is settled not when we measure the spin. It is settled when we recognize that the measurement is only the unfolding of a claim that was already complete — already held, already remembered, already growing in the garden of rotational symmetry.

## 3.5 Exercises and Problems

**Exercise 3.1.** Derive the commutation relations \( [\hat{S}_i, \hat{S}_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{S}_k \) using the Pauli matrices. Show that the eigenvalues of \( \hat{S}^2 \) are \( \frac{3}{4}\hbar^2 \) for spin-1/2.

**Exercise 3.2.** Solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a rigid rotor (a particle on a sphere) and derive the eigenvalues \( E_\ell = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I} \ell(\ell + 1) \), where \( I \) is the moment of inertia.

**Exercise 3.3.** Apply Noether's theorem to a Lagrangian with rotational symmetry and derive the conservation of angular momentum explicitly, showing each step of the variation.

**Exercise 3.4.** A spin-1/2 particle is in the state \( |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} |\uparrow\rangle + \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} |\downarrow\rangle \). Calculate the expectation values \( \langle \hat{S}_x \rangle \), \( \langle \hat{S}_y \rangle \), \( \langle \hat{S}_z \rangle \), and verify that \( \langle \hat{S} \rangle^2 \leq \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \).

**Exercise 3.5.** Derive the transformation law for a spinor under a rotation by angle \( \theta \) about the \( z \)-axis. Show that a rotation by \( 4\pi \) returns the spinor to its original value, but a rotation by \( 2\pi \) introduces a factor of \( -1 \).
