# Chapter 4 — Perturbation Theory: Stationary and Time-Dependent Approximations

**Level:** Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive first- and second-order corrections to energy levels and eigenstates, to derive Fermi's golden rule for time-dependent transitions, and to apply these results to the Stark effect and to two-level systems.

---

## 4.1 Stationary Perturbation Theory: First and Second Order

Consider a Hamiltonian \( \hat{H} = \hat{H}^{(0)} + \lambda \hat{V} \), with \( \hat{H}^{(0)} |n^{(0)}\rangle = E_n^{(0)} |n^{(0)}\rangle \). Expand:

\[
E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2 E_n^{(2)} + \dots
\]

\[
|n\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda |n^{(1)}\rangle + \lambda^2 |n^{(2)}\rangle + \dots
\]

Substituting into \( \hat{H}|n\rangle = E_n |n\rangle \) and collecting terms by order yields the first-order energy correction:

\[
E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)} | \hat{V} | n^{(0)} \rangle
\]

The second-order correction is:

\[
E_n^{(2)} = \sum_{m \neq n} \frac{|\langle m^{(0)} | \hat{V} | n^{(0)} \rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}
\]

This result is exact to second order in \( \lambda \). The derivation requires only that \( \hat{V} \) is self-adjoint and that the unperturbed spectrum is non-degenerate (for the non-degenerate case). For degenerate states, one must diagonalize \( \hat{V} \) within the degenerate subspace.

## 4.2 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory: Fermi's Golden Rule

Consider a time-dependent Hamiltonian \( \hat{H}(t) = \hat{H}^{(0)} + \hat{V}(t) \). The state evolves as:

\[
|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t) e^{-i E_n^{(0)} t / \hbar} |n^{(0)}\rangle
\]

Substituting into the Schrödinger equation yields:

\[
i\hbar \frac{dc_k}{dt} = \sum_n \langle k^{(0)} | \hat{V}(t) | n^{(0)} \rangle c_n(t) e^{-i (E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) t / \hbar}
\]

To first order in \( \hat{V} \), with \( c_n(0) = \delta_{n i} \):

\[
c_k^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar} \int_0^t \langle k^{(0)} | \hat{V}(t') | i^{(0)} \rangle e^{i \omega_{ki} t'} dt'
\]

where \( \omega_{ki} = (E_k^{(0)} - E_i^{(0)})/\hbar \). For a sinusoidal perturbation \( \hat{V}(t) = \hat{V} e^{-i\omega t} + \hat{V}^\dagger e^{i\omega t} \), and for \( t \to \infty \), the transition probability per unit time is:

\[
W_{i \to k} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |\langle k^{(0)} | \hat{V} | i^{(0)} \rangle|^2 \delta(E_k - E_i - \hbar \omega)
\]

This is Fermi's golden rule. It connects the microscopic transition amplitude to the macroscopic transition rate, and it is the foundation of spectroscopy, scattering theory, and quantum information dynamics.

## 4.3 The Stark Effect: A Physical Application

For a hydrogen atom in an electric field \( \mathbf{E} \), the perturbation is \( \hat{V} = e \mathbf{E} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} \). The first-order correction to the ground-state energy is zero (by parity). The second-order correction is:

\[
E_{1s}^{(2)} = - \frac{9}{4} a_0^3 E^2
\]

where \( a_0 = 4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar^2 / (m_e e^2) \) is the Bohr radius. This result demonstrates that even in the absence of a linear Stark shift, the atom responds quadratically to the field — a claim settled by the structure of the Hilbert space and the symmetry of the unperturbed state.

## 4.4 Literary Reflection: Dostoevsky's Underground and the Perturbation — The Claim That Questions Itself

Dostoevsky's underground man asks whether freedom is worth the suffering. The perturbation asks the same question, but in the language of energy: whether the change is worth the correction. The first-order correction is the claim that the change is small, that the original state holds, that the claim is settled with only a slight adjustment. The second-order correction is the claim that the change is deeper — that the original state was not complete, that the claim must be settled not only by adjusting, but by remembering all the other states that were hidden, folded into the superposition.

The claim of perturbation theory is not that the world is simple. It is that the world can be understood by adding small truths to a large truth — by recognizing that the claim is not false, but incomplete, and that the completion requires not a replacement, but an accumulation. The underground man would approve: the claim is not destroyed by the question. It is completed by it. The claim is settled not when the perturbation disappears, but when the observer recognizes that the perturbation was always part of the claim — the part that held the question, the part that remembered the freedom, the part that kept the claim open.

## 4.5 Exercises and Problems

**Exercise 4.1.** Derive the second-order energy correction for a two-level system with \( \hat{H}^{(0)} = \frac{\hbar \omega}{2} \sigma_z \) and \( \hat{V} = \lambda \sigma_x \). Show that the exact solution is \( E = \pm \frac{\hbar}{2} \sqrt{\omega^2 + 4\lambda^2/\hbar^2} \) and compare with the perturbative result to second order.

**Exercise 4.2.** Apply time-dependent perturbation theory to a harmonic oscillator initially in the ground state, subjected to a sinusoidal perturbation \( \hat{V}(t) = V_0 \hat{x} \cos(\omega t) \). Calculate the transition probability to the first excited state using Fermi's golden rule.

**Exercise 4.3.** Derive the Stark effect for the \( n = 2 \) state of hydrogen (degenerate in \( \ell = 0, 1 \)). Diagonalize the perturbation matrix within the degenerate subspace and find the linear Stark splitting.

**Exercise 4.4.** A quantum system has Hamiltonian \( \hat{H} = \hat{H}^{(0)} + \epsilon \hat{V} \). Show that the first-order correction to the wave function is:

\[
|n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m \neq n} \frac{\langle m^{(0)} | \hat{V} | n^{(0)} \rangle}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}} |m^{(0)}\rangle
\]

**Exercise 4.5.** Prove Fermi's golden rule for a constant perturbation \( \hat{V}(t) = \hat{V} \) (not sinusoidal), taking the limit \( t \to \infty \) and using the identity \( \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{\sin^2(\alpha t / 2)}{(\alpha/2)^2} = \pi t \delta(\alpha) \).
