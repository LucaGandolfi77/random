# Chapter 5 — Identical Particles: Symmetry, Fermions, Bosons, and the Pauli Principle

**Level:** Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive the symmetry properties of multi-particle wave functions, to establish the connection between spin and statistics (the spin-statistics theorem), and to apply the Pauli exclusion principle to atomic and stellar structure.

---

## 5.1 The Symmetry Postulate

For a system of \( N \) identical particles, the Hamiltonian is symmetric under any permutation \( \hat{P}_{ij} \) that exchanges particles \( i \) and \( j \):

\[
[\hat{P}_{ij}, \hat{H}] = 0
\]

This implies that \( \hat{P}_{ij} \) and \( \hat{H} \) can be simultaneously diagonalized. The eigenvalues of \( \hat{P}_{ij} \) are \( \pm 1 \), corresponding to symmetric and antisymmetric wave functions:

\[
\hat{P}_{ij} |\psi\rangle = \pm |\psi\rangle
\]

For particles with integer spin (bosons), the wave function is symmetric: \( \hat{P}_{ij} |\psi\rangle = + |\psi\rangle \). For particles with half-integer spin (fermions), the wave function is antisymmetric: \( \hat{P}_{ij} |\psi\rangle = - |\psi\rangle \). This is the spin-statistics theorem, which can be derived in relativistic quantum field theory but is postulated here.

The claim is settled by observation: electrons (spin-1/2) are fermions, photons (spin-1) are bosons. The claim is not false. It is a structural feature of the Hilbert space of identical particles — a feature that determines the structure of atoms, the stability of white dwarfs, and the existence of superfluidity in neutron stars.

---

## 5.2 The Slater Determinant: Antisymmetric Wave Functions

For \( N \) fermions in single-particle states \( |\phi_k\rangle \), the antisymmetric wave function is given by the Slater determinant:

\[
\psi(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \det \begin{pmatrix} \phi_1(\mathbf{x}_1) & \phi_2(\mathbf{x}_1) & \dots & \phi_N(\mathbf{x}_1) \\ \phi_1(\mathbf{x}_2) & \phi_2(\mathbf{x}_2) & \dots & \phi_N(\mathbf{x}_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \phi_1(\mathbf{x}_N) & \phi_2(\mathbf{x}_N) & \dots & \phi_N(\mathbf{x}_N) \end{pmatrix}
\]

If any two single-particle states are identical, the determinant vanishes — this is the mathematical expression of the Pauli exclusion principle. The normalization factor \( 1/\sqrt{N!} \) ensures \( \int |\psi|^2 d\tau = 1 \).

---

## 5.3 The Pauli Exclusion Principle: Physical Consequences

The exclusion principle implies that no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state. For electrons in an atom, this produces the shell structure (\( 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, \dots \)). The number of electrons in a shell is \( 2(2\ell + 1) \), where the factor 2 accounts for spin.

In a white dwarf, the electron degeneracy pressure arises directly from the exclusion principle. The pressure is not thermal; it is quantum mechanical — the claim that the electrons, even at zero temperature, must occupy distinct states, and that this occupation produces a pressure that can support the star against gravity.

The claim is not only physical. It is philosophical: the exclusion principle is the claim that identity is not repetition. Two electrons are not the same electron. They are the claim that the universe holds more than one of everything — the claim that multiplicity is not chaos, but structure.

---

## 5.4 Literary Reflection: Proust and the Slater Determinant — Memory as Antisymmetry

Proust writes that memory is the unfolding of a page that was folded. The Slater determinant is the mathematical form of this truth: it holds all the single-particle states, folded into a single wave function, and unfolds them only in the measurement — not as separate memories, but as a single claim that holds all possibilities in its antisymmetry. The exclusion principle is not a restriction. It is the claim that the memory of the past (the ground state) and the memory of the future (the excited state) cannot occupy the same space at the same time — that time, in the quantum world, is not only linear, but structured by the impossibility of repetition.

The claim is settled not by observing a single electron, but by observing the structure — the atom, the white dwarf, the neutron star — the claim that holds itself together by the patience of exclusion, by the refusal of repetition, by the memory that each state is unique and necessary.

## 5.5 Exercises and Problems

**Exercise 5.1.** Write the Slater determinant for three electrons in states \( |1\rangle, |2\rangle, |3\rangle \). Show explicitly that \( \psi(x_1, x_2, x_3) = -\psi(x_2, x_1, x_3) \).

**Exercise 5.2.** Derive the Pauli exclusion principle for two fermions in a one-dimensional infinite square well. Show that the energy of the ground state is \( E = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2}{2 m a^2} (1^2 + 2^2) \) and that the first excited state has energy \( E = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2}{2 m a^2} (1^2 + 3^2) \).

**Exercise 5.3.** Calculate the degeneracy pressure of an electron gas at density \( \rho = 10^9 \mathrm{kg/m^3} \) (typical of a white dwarf). Use the non-relativistic expression \( P = K_1 (\rho/\mu_e)^{5/3} \) and compare with the gravitational pressure \( P_{\mathrm{grav}} \approx G M^2 / R^4 \) for \( M = 1 M_\odot \) and \( R = 7000 \mathrm{km} \).

**Exercise 5.4.** A two-particle system has Hamiltonian \( \hat{H} = \hat{H}_1 + \hat{H}_2 \). Show that the wave function \( \psi(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\phi_1(x_1)\phi_2(x_2) - \phi_2(x_1)\phi_1(x_2)) \) is antisymmetric and that \( \langle \hat{H} \rangle = E_1 + E_2 \).

**Exercise 5.5.** Discuss the physical meaning of the spin-statistics theorem. Why must particles with half-integer spin be fermions? What would happen if electrons were bosons? (Consider the structure of atoms, the periodic table, and the stability of matter.)
