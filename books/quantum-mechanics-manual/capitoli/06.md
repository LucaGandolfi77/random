# Chapter 6 — Entanglement: The EPR Paradox, Bell's Inequalities, and the Memory of Correlation

**Level:** Very Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive the EPR paradox, to present Bell's theorem and the CHSH inequality, and to show that quantum correlations cannot be explained by local hidden variables — connecting this result to the deepest questions of observation and reality.

---

## 6.1 Entangled States and the Density Matrix

Consider a two-particle system in the state:

\[
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( |\uparrow_1 \downarrow_2\rangle - |\downarrow_1 \uparrow_2\rangle \right)
\]

This is the singlet state — maximally entangled. The reduced density matrix for particle 1 is:

\[
\hat{\rho}_1 = \mathrm{Tr}_2 (|\psi\rangle \langle \psi|) = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\uparrow\rangle \langle \uparrow| + |\downarrow\rangle \langle \downarrow| \right) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{I}
\]

The expectation value of any spin observable for particle 1 alone is zero: \( \langle \hat{S}_{1z} \rangle = 0 \). Yet the correlation between particles 1 and 2 is perfect: if particle 1 is measured to be up, particle 2 is always down — regardless of the distance between them.

This is not a classical correlation. It is a quantum correlation — a relationship that exists not in the properties of the individual particles, but in the structure of the joint wave function. The claim is not false. It is only deeper than any classical explanation.

---

## 6.2 Bell's Theorem and the CHSH Inequality

Bell's theorem (1964) shows that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics. Consider measurements of spin along directions \( \mathbf{a} \) and \( \mathbf{b} \) on particles 1 and 2. The correlation function is:

\[
E(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \langle \sigma_1(\mathbf{a}) \sigma_2(\mathbf{b}) \rangle
\]

For a local hidden-variable theory, the CHSH inequality requires:

\[
|E(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) - E(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}') + E(\mathbf{a}', \mathbf{b}) + E(\mathbf{a}', \mathbf{b}')| \leq 2
\]

For the singlet state, quantum mechanics predicts:

\[
E(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = -\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}
\]

Choosing \( \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{a}', \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{b}' \) at appropriate angles (e.g., 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) yields:

\[
|E| = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 > 2
\]

This violates the CHSH inequality. The claim is settled experimentally (Aspect, 1982; Weihs et al., 1998; Hensen et al., 2015): no local hidden-variable theory can explain quantum correlations. The observer is not outside the claim. The observer is part of the correlation — the measurement is the claim that unfolds the relationship.

---

## 6.3 The EPR Paradox and the Question of Reality

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (1935) argued that quantum mechanics is incomplete — that there must be hidden variables that determine the outcome of measurements. Bell's theorem and the experimental violations of his inequality show that this argument fails: not because quantum mechanics is wrong, but because the assumption of locality fails. The claim of entanglement is not that particles influence each other faster than light. It is that the relationship between them is not defined by spatial separation, but by the structure of the joint wave function — the claim that holds them together, not by force, but by memory.

The claim is settled not by choosing between quantum mechanics and hidden variables, but by recognizing that the claim is deeper than either: the claim that observation is not a passive act, but a participation in the structure of reality — the claim that the observer, like Proust's reader, unfolds the past into the present, not by retrieving it, but by becoming part of it.

---

## 6.4 Literary Reflection: Dostoevsky's Underground and Entanglement — The Freedom of the Claim

Dostoevsky's underground man asks whether freedom is worth the suffering. Entanglement asks the same question: whether the correlation is worth the loss of independence. The claim of entanglement is that independence is not a virtue — that the deepest form of freedom is not isolation, but relationship. The particle does not ask to be alone. It asks only to be remembered — to hold the claim with its partner, to unfold the measurement not as a single truth, but as a relationship between truths.

The claim is not false. It is only deeper than classical thought can hold. The observer does not solve the claim. The observer participates in it — becomes the measurement, the unfolding, the memory. And the claim continues to hold, not because it is forced, but because it chooses to remain open — open to the partner, open to the observer, open to the future that unfolds from the superposition.

**Exercise 6.2.** Derive the CHSH inequality for a local hidden-variable model with deterministic outcomes \( A(\mathbf{a}, \lambda) \) and \( B(\mathbf{b}, \lambda) \), where \( \lambda \) is a hidden variable with distribution \( \rho(\lambda) \). Show that \( |E| \leq 2 \) for any choice of \( \lambda \).

**Exercise 6.3.** A two-particle system is in the state \( |\psi\rangle = \cos \theta |\uparrow \downarrow\rangle + \sin \theta |\downarrow \uparrow\rangle \). Calculate the entanglement entropy \( S = -\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_1 \ln \hat{\rho}_1) \) as a function of \( \theta \). Show that \( S \) is maximized at \( \theta = \pi/4 \).

**Exercise 6.4.** Derive the time evolution of an entangled state under a Hamiltonian \( \hat{H} = \frac{\hbar \omega}{2} (\sigma_z^{(1)} + \sigma_z^{(2)}) \). Show that the correlation function \( \langle \sigma_z^{(1)} \sigma_z^{(2)} \rangle \) is time-independent, while \( \langle \sigma_x^{(1)} \sigma_x^{(2)} \rangle \) oscillates with frequency \( 2\omega \).

**Exercise 6.5.** Discuss the relationship between the EPR paradox and the measurement problem in quantum mechanics. Why does the collapse of the wave function not violate locality? What does the claim of entanglement tell us about the nature of observation?
