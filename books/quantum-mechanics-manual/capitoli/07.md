# Chapter 7 — Path Integrals: The Feynman Formulation and the Memory of All Paths

**Level:** Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive the Feynman path integral from the time-evolution operator, to show its equivalence to the Schrödinger equation, and to apply it to the propagator of a free particle and the harmonic oscillator.

---

## 7.1 The Propagator: From Time Evolution to Path Integration

The time-evolution operator for an infinitesimal interval \( \epsilon \) is:

\[
\hat{U}(\epsilon) = \exp\left( -\frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{H} \epsilon \right)
\]

For a Hamiltonian \( \hat{H} = \hat{p}^2 / (2m) + V(\hat{x}) \), insert a complete set of position eigenstates \( \int |x\rangle \langle x| dx = \mathbb{I} \) and a complete set of momentum eigenstates \( \int |p\rangle \langle p| dp / (2\pi \hbar) = \mathbb{I} \). The matrix element is:

\[
\langle x_{j+1} | \hat{U}(\epsilon) | x_j \rangle = \int \frac{dp}{2\pi \hbar} \exp\left[ \frac{i}{\hbar} \left( p (x_{j+1} - x_j) - \epsilon \left( \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x_j) \right) \right) \right]
\]

Performing the Gaussian integral over \( p \) yields:

\[
\langle x_{j+1} | \hat{U}(\epsilon) | x_j \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i \hbar \epsilon}} \exp\left[ \frac{i}{\hbar} \epsilon \left( \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{x_{j+1} - x_j}{\epsilon} \right)^2 - V(x_j) \right) \right]
\]

Dividing the time interval \( t - t_0 \) into \( N \) steps with \( \epsilon = (t - t_0)/N \) and taking \( N \to \infty \) yields the path integral for the propagator:

\[
K(x_f, t_f; x_i, t_i) = \int_{x(t_i) = x_i}^{x(t_f) = x_f} \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \exp\left( \frac{i}{\hbar} S[x(t)] \right)
\]

where \( S[x(t)] = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} dt \left( \frac{m}{2} \dot{x}^2 - V(x) \right) \) is the classical action, and \( \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \) denotes the measure over all paths connecting \( x_i \) to \( x_f \). This is not an approximation. It is an exact reformulation of quantum mechanics — the claim that the particle does not take one path, but all paths, and that the quantum amplitude is the sum (integral) of the phases of all paths, weighted by the action.

---

## 7.2 The Free Particle Propagator and the Classical Limit

For the free particle (\( V = 0 \)), the action is \( S = \frac{m}{2} \frac{(x_f - x_i)^2}{t_f - t_i} \). The path integral can be evaluated exactly:

\[
K(x_f, t; x_i, 0) = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i \hbar t}} \exp\left( \frac{i m (x_f - x_i)^2}{2\hbar t} \right)
\]

This is the Green's function of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. The classical path (the straight line \( x_{\mathrm{cl}}(t) \)) dominates the integral in the limit \( \hbar \to 0 \), demonstrating the correspondence principle: quantum mechanics reduces to classical mechanics when the action is large compared to \( \hbar \). The claim is settled not by approximation, but by the structure of the integral — the claim that all paths contribute, but the classical path contributes the most.

---

## 7.3 Literary Reflection: Proust's Memory and the Path Integral — All Paths Remembered

Proust writes that the past is not lost; it is folded into the things we keep without knowing. The path integral is the mathematical form of this truth: the particle does not take one path from the past (\( x_i \)) to the future (\( x_f \)). It takes all paths — folded, hidden, remembered in the integral — and the present is the sum of all pasts, weighted by the action, unfolded into the amplitude.

The claim of Proust and the claim of Feynman converge in the measure \( \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \): the claim that memory is not linear, that the past is not a single line, but a collection of all possible lines, all possible choices, all possible claims — and that the observer, in measuring the particle at \( x_f \), does not choose one past. The observer unfolds all pasts, remembers all paths, and settles the claim not by selecting one, but by integrating over all.

The claim is not false. It is only deeper than the observer's single memory — deeper, like Proust's past, like Tolstoy's garden, like Dostoevsky's question — and it continues to hold, not because it is simple, but because it is complete: the claim that holds all paths, all memories, all possibilities, in the quiet patience of the path integral.

## 7.4 Exercises and Problems

**Exercise 7.1.** Derive the free-particle propagator by evaluating the Gaussian integral in the path integral expression explicitly. Show that the result matches the Green's function of the Schrödinger equation.

**Exercise 7.2.** Calculate the path integral for the harmonic oscillator using the method of stationary phase. Show that the result is:

\[
K(x_f, t; x_i, 0) = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i \hbar \sin(\omega t)}} \exp\left[ \frac{i m \omega}{2\hbar \sin(\omega t)} ((x_f^2 + x_i^2)\cos(\omega t) - 2 x_f x_i) \right]
\]

**Exercise 7.3.** Derive the semi-classical approximation (WKB approximation) from the path integral by expanding the action around the classical path to second order. Show that the result is proportional to \( \exp(i S_{\mathrm{cl}} / \hbar) \) multiplied by a fluctuation determinant.

**Exercise 7.4.** A particle of mass \( m \) moves in a potential \( V(x) = \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2 \). Calculate the propagator using the path integral and verify that it satisfies the Schrödinger equation.

**Exercise 7.5.** Discuss the physical meaning of the path integral formulation. Why does every path contribute? What does the measure \( \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \) represent? How does the path integral connect quantum mechanics to classical mechanics in the limit \( \hbar \to 0 \)?
