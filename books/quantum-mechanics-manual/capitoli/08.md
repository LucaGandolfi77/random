# Chapter 8 — Scattering Theory: The S-Matrix, Cross Sections, and the Claim of Interaction

**Level:** Advanced  
**Objective:** To derive the Born approximation, the partial-wave expansion, the optical theorem, and the connection between scattering amplitudes and observable cross sections.

---

## 8.1 The S-Matrix and Asymptotic States

In scattering theory, the initial state \( |\phi_i\rangle \) is a free-particle state (a plane wave or wave packet). The final state \( |\phi_f\rangle \) is also a free-particle state. The S-operator connects them:

\[
|\phi_f\rangle = \hat{S} |\phi_i\rangle
\]

The S-matrix is unitary: \( \hat{S}^\dagger \hat{S} = \mathbb{I} \). This ensures probability conservation: the total probability of all possible outcomes is 1. The T-matrix is defined by:

\[
\hat{S} = \mathbb{I} - 2\pi i \hat{T}
\]

The transition probability per unit time is:

\[
W_{i \to f} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |\langle \phi_f | \hat{T} | \phi_i \rangle|^2 \delta(E_f - E_i)
\]

This is the generalization of Fermi's golden rule to scattering processes. The claim is not false. It is only deeper: the claim that interaction is not the destruction of the initial state, but the transformation of it — the claim that the observer measures not the particle that was, but the particle that is, after all possible interactions have been summed.

---

## 8.2 The Born Approximation

For a weak potential \( V(\mathbf{r}) \), the first-order approximation to the T-matrix is:

\[
\langle \mathbf{p}_f | \hat{T} | \mathbf{p}_i \rangle \approx \langle \mathbf{p}_f | \hat{V} | \mathbf{p}_i \rangle = \int d^3r \, e^{-i \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{r} / \hbar} V(\mathbf{r}) = \tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})
\]

where \( \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{p}_f - \mathbf{p}_i \) is the momentum transfer. The differential cross section is:

\[
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left( \frac{m}{2\pi \hbar^2} \right)^2 |\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})|^2
\]

This is the Born approximation. It connects the potential directly to the observable scattering pattern. The claim is settled not by solving the full Schrödinger equation, but by recognizing that the first interaction between the particle and the potential is sufficient to describe the scattering — the claim that the claim holds, even in approximation, because the structure of the interaction is preserved.

---

## 8.3 Partial-Wave Expansion and the Optical Theorem

For spherically symmetric potentials, the scattering amplitude can be expanded in partial waves:

\[
f(\theta) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} (2\ell + 1) e^{i \delta_\ell} \sin \delta_\ell P_\ell(\cos \theta)
\]

where \( \delta_\ell \) are the phase shifts, determined by solving the radial Schrödinger equation. The total cross section is:

\[
\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2} \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} (2\ell + 1) \sin^2 \delta_\ell
\]

The optical theorem relates the total cross section to the imaginary part of the forward scattering amplitude:

\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \frac{4\pi}{k} \mathrm{Im} f(\theta = 0)
\]

This is not an approximation. It is an exact consequence of unitarity. The claim that the total probability of scattering is related to the forward amplitude is the claim that the observer cannot observe the scattering without participating in it — the claim that measurement and interaction are not separate, but are the same claim, expressed in different forms.

---

## 8.4 Literary Reflection: Tolstoy's Garden and Scattering — The Claim That Holds Even When Broken

Tolstoy writes that the garden grows whether we count it. The scattering amplitude is the same: it holds the claim of the interaction, even when the interaction breaks the initial state. The particle is not destroyed by the potential. It is transformed — scattered, deflected, remembered in the phase shift \( \delta_\ell \). The claim is not false. It is only changed — folded into the partial wave, unfolded in the cross section, remembered in the optical theorem.

The observer measures the cross section. The cross section measures the potential. The potential measures the claim. And the claim continues to hold — not because it is unbroken, but because it is remembered, because the scattering is not the end of the claim, but the continuation of it, the transformation of it, the growth of the garden that holds all interactions, all transformations, all memories, in the patient accumulation of the partial waves.

## 8.5 Exercises and Problems

**Exercise 8.1.** Derive the Born approximation for a Gaussian potential \( V(r) = V_0 \exp(-r^2 / a^2) \). Calculate \( \tilde{V}(q) \) and the differential cross section.

**Exercise 8.2.** Solve the radial Schrödinger equation for a spherical square well and derive the phase shifts \( \delta_\ell \) for low energies (\( k a \ll 1 \)).

**Exercise 8.3.** Derive the optical theorem from unitarity: \( \hat{S}^\dagger \hat{S} = \mathbb{I} \). Show that \( \mathrm{Im} \langle \mathbf{p}_i | \hat{T} | \mathbf{p}_i \rangle = - \pi k \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}} / (2\pi)^3 \).

**Exercise 8.4.** A particle of energy \( E = 10 \) MeV scatters from a potential \( V(r) = V_0 \exp(-r/a) \) with \( V_0 = 5 \) MeV and \( a = 1 \) fm. Calculate the differential cross section at \( \theta = 30^\circ \) using the Born approximation.

**Exercise 8.5.** Discuss the physical meaning of the optical theorem. Why must the imaginary part of the forward amplitude be related to the total cross section? What does this tell us about the relationship between observation and interaction?
