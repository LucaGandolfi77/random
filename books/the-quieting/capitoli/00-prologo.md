# Prologue — The Room of Return

The first thing the city taught its children was not how to be brave. It was how to come back.

In every school there was a Room of Return: a small room with a window, a sink, two chairs, a clock whose second hand had been removed. The rooms had no locks. There were blankets folded in cupboards and cards printed with ordinary instructions: Name five things you can see. Drink water. You do not have to explain yet.

The lesson was simple, and the city had made it sound like mercy: a mind could be hurt by too much distress, too often, without enough help. Not every grief. Not every panic. Not even the worst thing that could happen to a person. No one knew the exact measure. The Quieting was a risk that gathered in the dark, a name for what sometimes remained after the storm: a missing word, a lost sequence, a face recognized but not placed. Sometimes more. It was rare enough to be feared and real enough to shape a civilization.

So Bellwether learned to notice. It hired psychologists and psychiatrists by the thousands. It trained bus drivers to ask if a passenger wished for company. It paid people to stay home after a loss. It built clinics beside libraries and called them houses of return.

Then, as people do, it turned care into a rule.

A calm voice became proof of recovery. Tears became a private inconvenience. The city praised those who returned quickly, and did not ask what they had left behind in order to do it.

Dr. Mara Venn knew the difference between a person who was calm and a person who had merely become very good at appearing so. She knew because for twelve years she had listened to both.

She also knew because she had once been the second kind.

On the morning she met Nell Ilyan, the harbor bells rang for a ship that had not yet arrived. Mara stood in the corridor outside Interview Room Three, holding a paper cup of tea gone cold, and listened to the girl inside refuse to breathe on command.

“Good,” Mara said, though Nell could not hear her. “Keep your own time.”

Then she opened the door.
