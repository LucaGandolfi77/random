# Chapter 1 — The Shape of a Breath

Nell Ilyan had arranged the tissues into a square and then refused to use them.

She sat with her back to the window. At seventeen she had the grave, watchful face of someone who had been congratulated too often for being sensible. The evaluation form between them asked about sleep, appetite, memory, panic, and “capacity to resume ordinary function.” The last phrase made Mara think of a broken machine returned to service.

“What do you want me to write?” Mara asked.

“That I’m not dangerous.”

“I don’t think you are.”

“You have to write it down.”

Mara did. Nell's mother had died six weeks earlier, suddenly, on a footbridge in rain. Nell had suffered two episodes of acute panic since. One in the school map room; one on a bus. She could still name the streets she had mapped, but had forgotten the route home from the hospital. A temporary lapse, Mara believed. A mind under strain, not a mind condemned.

The city’s new assessment required a risk tier. Amber meant check-ins, a family liaison, and a short leave from her apprenticeship. Red meant mandatory residential stabilization. Mara left the box blank.

“Can grief make you stupid?” Nell asked.

“No,” Mara said. “Grief can make it hard to think. That isn't the same thing. And the Quieting isn’t a punishment for feeling. It’s a rare injury we don’t fully understand. We can lower some risks. We cannot predict a person’s future from two bad days.”

Nell looked toward the rain. “Everyone says they’re helping. Then they take your pencils.”

At the end of the session, Mara offered options: a weekly appointment, a quiet room at school, practical help with meals, and the number of a psychiatrist if sleep did not return. Nell chose the weekly appointments and refused the family liaison. Mara wrote that down too.

Outside, the corridor smelled of wet wool and chamomile. Sera Okafor waited beside the lift, silver hair pinned like a small, exact flame.

“You left her tier blank,” she said.

“I left it undecided.”

“The form won’t accept undecided.”

“Then the form is asking for a certainty we don’t have.”

Sera’s eyes softened, which was how Mara knew she was worried. “The city is frightened, Mara. Frightened people want a number.”

“Then we should be careful what number we give them.”

At the street door, Mara saw a man under the awning, holding a folded umbrella he had not opened. Jonah Vale looked older than the last time she had seen him and exactly as she remembered: attentive in a way that made evasions feel impolite.

He had come back to Bellwether. And he had brought rain indoors on his shoulders.
