# Chapter 1a — The School of Calm

Mara had learned the city’s rules in a schoolroom whose windows were always open, even in winter. The teacher said it was important to know what weather did to a body. They sat in a circle on the varnished floor and practiced naming sensations before they became emergencies. A tight throat. Warm ears. A thought that repeated itself until it seemed to have authority. The exercise was not foolish, and the adults who had designed it were not tyrants. The school had lost three children in the years before the Rooms of Return were built; no one wished to return to a time when a child in distress was sent home alone because a teacher did not know what else to do.

At ten, Mara liked the charts. She liked the small colored squares that made the invisible visible. Green meant settled. Yellow meant notice. Red meant ask for help. A scale was a kind of mercy when the world seemed too large to hold in the mind.

Ada had hated the charts. She would put the red square on her forehead and march around the classroom as a warning sign. The teacher scolded her, and Mara, embarrassed, told her to stop making everything into a joke. Their mother was ill then, not dangerously, only often enough to fill the kitchen with medicine bottles and worried pauses. Ada was the one who made those pauses laughable. Mara was the one who arranged the bottles by hour.

Years later, when the city began to praise calm as a civic virtue, Mara understood how a useful tool could be promoted into an ideal. The clinics measured recovery by function: whether a person could sleep, eat, remember an appointment, return to work. Those were sensible things to measure. A mind was not made of abstractions alone. Hunger, rent, medication, the school run, the appointment missed because a bus did not come—each could make a person’s suffering more dangerous.

But the city’s language had grown efficient. A person was “restored” when they returned to ordinary duties. Distress that lasted longer than the official leave was called persistent. A second crisis became recurrence. A third became a pattern. Pattern became risk; risk became a file that followed a person into the room before they did.

Mara had resisted this language in meetings and used it in reports. She could condemn a thing in principle and still employ it when a form required an answer. That was one of the more subtle forms of compromise: not the dramatic exchange of conscience for money, but the small consent given because the line had to be filled before lunch.

She had become a psychologist partly because the work mattered and partly because meaning, when made into a profession, could be scheduled. Patients came at nine, ten, eleven. They left with an appointment card and a plan. The plan did not keep grief away; it gave grief a chair and a time when someone would be present for it. Mara had once believed that being present was the whole work. Then the systems grew around the rooms, and the rooms grew rules, and she was asked to measure whether the presence had succeeded.

On the morning she met Nell, she found herself looking at the three colored squares beside Interview Room Three. Green. Yellow. Red. They had been printed for children, but adults passed them every day without noticing.

She thought of Ada’s red square on her forehead, and of the ridiculous crown of it. She thought of how easily a person could spend a lifetime mistaking the ability to sort pain for the ability to end it.

Then she put the squares away and entered the room where Nell was waiting.
