# Chapter 1b — The Intern’s Ledger

Imani Cole had been taught that a good question was one that could be entered into a field.

In the clinic’s training room, she sat beneath a clock that had been synchronized with the central registry and learned to conduct an intake in forty minutes. Sleep, appetite, panic frequency, memory, safety, support, functional impairment. The order mattered. The sequence had been designed after a study found that clinicians skipped practical questions when a patient began to cry. The discovery was humane; the schedule that followed it was less so. It allowed the person in the room to be heard before time ran out, provided that what they said fitted the available boxes.

Imani was not cynical. She had applied for the training because, at nineteen, her mother had gone through a year of grief so quiet that the family mistook it for composure. A psychologist had asked her mother not what had happened but what the mornings were like. The question opened something. Her mother had begun to eat breakfast at the table again. Imani believed in the work because she had seen one of its small, non-magical successes.

She also believed in evidence. Her father had been a salesman of certainty, able to turn a guess into a promise and a promise into a debt. Imani had sworn she would never persuade a person to trust her without knowing what her words could bear.

On her first week in the clinic, she observed Mara interview Lio Marr, who had arrived with salt on his jacket and a folded overtime notice in his pocket. Lio worked dispatch at the harbor, where a shift began at dusk and ended when the incoming crew appeared, whether or not they appeared on time. His panic came most often at the console, with the lights of six monitors arranged before him like windows into separate emergencies. He had started forgetting radio call signs. He had also been sleeping four hours a night since his father’s funeral.

“Do you think I’m losing my mind?” Lio asked.

Mara did not answer with the categorical reassurance Imani had learned to distrust. She asked what he meant by losing. Lio said he could hear a vessel call and know the sound, but the name would arrive late. He said he was afraid of making a mistake that put a crew in danger. He said the union supervisor had told him to stop making trouble by asking for a lighter shift.

Mara asked about immediate safety, medical evaluation, sleep, and what work adjustments might be possible. She told him that symptoms under severe strain did not tell them what his future would be; she also told him they had to take the memory lapses seriously enough to investigate. She offered a referral to a psychiatrist, a medical check for sleep and other possible causes, and a letter requesting temporary changes at work. She asked which step he wanted first.

Lio chose the letter. He did not want to speak with a psychiatrist yet. Mara noted the refusal without marking it as resistance.

After the session, Imani looked at the form. Mara had written *patient requests work accommodation; cognitive complaints to be assessed over time* in the box marked “presenting risk.”

“The registry won’t count that as a completed intervention,” Imani said.

“Then the registry has a narrow idea of intervention.”

“Shouldn’t we make the record consistent?”

“Consistent with what?”

Imani felt her cheeks warm. She understood that Mara was not asking her to abandon measurement. She was asking her to notice when measurement concealed its own choices.

That evening Imani opened a private training notebook and wrote down what she had seen: the questions asked, the options offered, the one Lio chose. She did not write his name. At the top of the page she wrote, *A person may refuse one kind of help and still be asking for help.*

She looked at the sentence for a long time, uncertain whether it was wisdom or merely a good sentence. The difference, she suspected, would be decided by what she did next.
