# Chapter 1c — The Follow-Up Call

Imani called Dena at six in the evening because Dena had asked the clinic to call after six. The request was written twice in the file: once by the first clinician and once by Dena herself, who had insisted on seeing the note before she left. At 5:58 Imani sat with the phone in her hand and watched the clock turn. She had never before understood that punctuality could be an ethical act.

Dena answered from a bus. She was on her way home from the hospital, where her infant had been discharged that morning. The baby was asleep against her chest. Dena said she was grateful, then said she was angry that people kept congratulating her as if surviving the week were a performance she had given for them.

Imani asked how her husband was doing. He had attended one psychiatric appointment and spoken with a therapist twice. He still cried sometimes. The episodes were shorter. He had returned to work for half-days, though their supervisor had asked when he would be “normal again.” Dena said she had stopped answering that question.

“Do you feel safe at home?” Imani asked.

“Yes.”

“Are you sleeping?”

“Not much. The baby is a baby.”

“Do you want support for yourself too?”

There was a pause in which Imani could hear the traffic through the phone. Dena said she did not know. She had spent the week being asked about her husband and the baby. The question felt both generous and late.

Imani offered a peer group and a separate appointment, making clear that she did not have to decide on the call. Dena asked whether the clinic would record her as a caregiver at risk. Imani explained what would be recorded and why, then asked whether Dena wanted the information in writing. Dena said yes. She had become good at asking for proof, not because she distrusted every person but because she had learned that an assurance could disappear when a different person opened the file.

When the call ended, Imani did not immediately complete the form. She sat with the silence and realized that the call had gone well by every measure she could name, except that Dena had not sounded relieved. Perhaps relief was not always the result of being offered the right thing. Perhaps a person could receive care and remain angry at the circumstances that made it necessary.

In supervision, Imani told Mara that she had been afraid to ask about Dena’s needs because it might seem intrusive.

“What made you ask?” Mara said.

“I remembered she had asked to be called after six. It felt like a small thing we could get right.”

“Small things are not small to the person whose life is made of them.”

Imani wrote the sentence down, then crossed it out. It sounded too much like a maxim. She wanted to remember the actual call: the bus noise, the baby breathing, Dena saying yes to safety and no to sleep, the unanswered question of whether she wanted help. The details were harder to quote and less likely to become a slogan. They were also the part she trusted.
