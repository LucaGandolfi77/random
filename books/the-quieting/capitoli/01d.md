# Chapter 1d — The First Case

Mara’s first patient as a trainee was a man who brought a paper bag of oranges to the clinic and refused to set it down.

He had lost his wife the previous year and had begun arriving at work an hour early, then sitting in his car until the shift began. His supervisor had described him as reliable, which was why the change had taken so long to notice. Mara was twenty-six, and she believed that if she listened closely enough she would find the sentence that opened the person. She had prepared questions, made notes, and arranged the chairs at an angle that her supervisor said felt less like an interview.

The man held the bag in his lap. Each time he spoke of his wife, his hand moved against the paper, making a dry, papery sound. He said she had liked oranges. He said the first winter after she died he bought them every week and left them in a bowl, though he had stopped eating them. He said his daughter thought this was strange.

Mara asked if he had considered giving the oranges away.

The man looked at her as if she had asked him to give away the wife. He stood, took the bag, and left the room. Mara remained sitting, mortified by the ease with which a question could become an injury when it had been meant as a suggestion.

Her supervisor, Dr. Okafor, did not tell her the question was fine. She asked what Mara had wanted the man to do with the oranges.

“I wanted to help him make the grief less painful.”

“Did he ask for that?”

Mara could not remember. She had been listening for what the patient needed and, without noticing, had begun to confuse need with the outcome she believed would be best.

The man returned the following week. He apologized for leaving. Mara apologized for the suggestion. He said he did not want to keep the oranges forever. He wanted, for now, to choose which ones he ate and which ones he let spoil. The choice itself mattered because there had been so many decisions made for him after his wife’s death: the funeral, the paperwork, the daughter’s move, the change in the apartment. The bowl of fruit was one of the few things he had not yet been asked to explain.

Mara remembered this when she came to the Service years later and saw how a well-intended intervention could become another demand. She had learned to ask before offering, but learning did not abolish the old impulse. It only gave her a moment in which she might recognize it.

After the man’s appointment, she wrote a note about the conversation. She did not write that he had made progress. She wrote that he wanted to choose what happened to the oranges. It was a small record, but for the first time she understood that a clinical note could either preserve a person’s agency or replace it with the clinician’s idea of a useful outcome.

The oranges, she later learned, had all been eaten by spring.
