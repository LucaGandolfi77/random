# Chapter 1f — The Sister Who Would Not Be a Case

When Mara first began her clinical training, Ada asked her not to talk like a psychologist at home.

It happened in the kitchen, after their mother had gone to bed. Ada was sitting on the counter with one bare foot swinging above the tile. She had been late to dinner and missed the bus, and their mother had asked too many questions when she came in. Mara, wanting to help, had said that their mother’s worry might be making Ada feel watched.

“I know what she’s doing,” Ada said. “I live here.”

“I’m only trying to understand.”

“You understand me like I’m a person you met in a room for an hour.”

Mara had felt attacked, then ashamed, then defensive because shame was a feeling she could not yet use without turning it into a claim of injury. She told Ada that her training had taught her to listen. Ada said listening was not the same as collecting information. She climbed down from the counter and left the kitchen.

The next morning Mara apologized, but she apologized in the language she had acquired: she said she had been too quick to interpret, that she had not made enough room for Ada’s perspective. Ada said, “You’re doing it again,” and then laughed, so Mara could not tell whether the apology had reached her.

Years later, in supervision, Mara would learn to distinguish understanding from explanation. Understanding asked what a person meant; explanation could take the answer away and return it as a theory. The distinction seemed simple in a seminar. It was harder with someone she loved, because love made her feel entitled to know, and fear made her believe that knowing might protect them.

When Ada’s panic began, Mara sometimes heard the training phrases arrive before her sister’s words had finished. She would ask about triggers, sleep, frequency, impairment. Ada would answer, then look at her with an expression that made Mara feel both useful and excluded. The questions were not wrong. The order in which Mara asked them often was.

After Ada died, Mara believed this failure explained everything. It did not. It was one part of their life together, neither the cause of the death nor a detail that could be excused by Mara’s intentions. She had tried to understand Ada and had sometimes turned her into a problem she could solve. She had also loved her with an attention that was not always articulate, had gone to the hospital, made soup, taken calls, and once crossed the city at midnight because Ada had asked her to bring a particular coat.

The fullness of the record was difficult to bear. It refused the clean division between the sister who had failed and the sister who had loved. Mara learned that the mind prefers such divisions because they make grief actionable: condemn yourself, forgive yourself, repeat. The truth asked more of her. It asked her to remember Ada as a person who had argued, joked, refused, requested, and changed her mind, not as the lesson that Mara’s profession had taught her.

Sometimes, when a patient used a phrase she recognized from her sister, Mara felt the old urgency rise. She tried to wait until the patient finished speaking. It was not a perfect form of love. It was the form of listening she could offer in that room.
