# Chapter 2 — The Archive of Small Weather

Jonah did not kiss her hello. He held out a paper bag.

“Pear pastry,” he said. “You used to say the bakery made them best on wet days.”

“I used to say many things.”

“Yes. I brought the least dangerous one.”

They sat in the clinic’s public garden, where rain ticked against the glass roof. Jonah had been gone for four years, first to the northern archive, then to a smaller town where the records had not yet learned to lie. His brother Tomas had been gone longer. The official report said Tomas’s cognitive decline followed “personal instability and noncompliance.” Jonah had spent his adult life trying to find the person inside that sentence.

“I found the original ledgers,” he said. “The Service logged adverse outcomes, then removed them from the annual reports. Not all of them. Enough to make the risk look smaller, and the protocol look more successful.”

“Who signed the revision?”

He slid a copy across the table. The signature was Sera Okafor’s.

Mara read the page twice. The first time she saw ink. The second time she saw her supervisor, teaching interns to sit beside people in silence, keeping the clinic open through a funding crisis, bringing soup to Mara the winter Ada died.

“Why show me?”

“Because you know the work is real. And because you know how the forms are used.” He looked down at his hands. “I don’t want to make the clinics disappear. Tomas needed help and there was none. But this is not help. This is a story about help, used to keep people quiet.”

Mara folded the page along its old crease. “If these files are authentic, they include private patient histories.”

“I know.”

“Then you can’t publish them.”

“I haven’t.”

A gull struck the roof with its shadow and passed on. The ordinary world kept arriving, indifferent and beautiful.

“You think I chose the Service over you,” Mara said.

“I thought you chose being useful over being known.”

The words landed without force. They did not need force.

Mara wanted to explain that after Ada, usefulness had been the one shape grief could take without frightening everyone. Instead she said, “I’m not sure I know the difference.”

Jonah broke the pastry in two and offered her the larger half. She took it. For a moment, the sweetness was almost unbearable: ripe pear, butter, the faint metallic taste of rain in the air.

“Help me verify the ledger,” he said. “Before I decide what to do with it.”

She should have said no. She heard herself say, “Bring the rest tomorrow.”
