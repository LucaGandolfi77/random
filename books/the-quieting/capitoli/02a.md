# Chapter 2a — The House of Sunday

When Ada was twenty-two, she called Mara every Sunday at precisely half past four. Not because they had agreed on the hour, but because Ada had once called at half past four and found Mara on the balcony, watching pigeons gather on the roof opposite, and from then on the hour became theirs.

They spoke about small things. Whether the bakery had changed its almond paste. Whether their father’s old watch had stopped because the battery was dead or because he had worn it until the mechanism gave up. The latest person Ada was seeing, described with so much detail that Mara could have recognized him by the shape of his silence. They did not speak much about their father’s death. The family had arranged itself around that absence like furniture around a pillar: everyone knew it was there, and no one wanted to collide with it.

Mara was in her first year of clinical training when Ada began having panic attacks. Her sister called them “the heart’s fire drill.” She said the first had come in a supermarket, among the oranges, when the fluorescent lights seemed to intensify until each piece of fruit was a small sun. The second had arrived in a train station. Afterward Ada remembered the platform, the smell of coffee, and the number on the departing train, but not the name of the city she meant to visit.

Mara gave her the number of a clinic. She told herself she had done what a sister could do. She was still learning what a psychologist could do, and it was easier to believe that the boundary between the roles would protect them both.

“Will you come with me?” Ada asked.

Mara had an exam. She had a seminar on risk. She had the early, blinding confidence of a person who believes the most important things will wait because they love you.

“I’ll come next week,” she said.

The following Sunday, Ada did not call. Mara was relieved at first, then irritated, then ashamed of being irritated. She called on Monday. Ada answered and said she had slept badly. The appointment had been moved. There was nothing to worry about, she insisted, and Mara believed her in the way one believes a person who needs to be believed in order to keep speaking.

Their last long conversation took place in a kitchen that smelled of burnt sugar. Ada had attempted a tart and forgotten it in the oven while explaining a film’s plot. The crust was black, the fruit collapsed, and she laughed at herself. Mara was tired from work and told her she should take better care of her routines.

Ada laughed again, more softly. “You always want there to be a routine where there’s a person.”

Mara, offended because the sentence found its mark, said, “And you always want there to be a person where there’s a problem.”

They made peace before they hung up. Families are capable of being both forgiving and careless; the two qualities often wear the same face.

For years Mara remembered that last conversation as proof of failure. Then she learned to remember it also as what it had been: two sisters, hungry and tired, trying to reach each other through habits that had once helped and now obstructed. Ada had not been a warning. She had been a person, and the loss of her could not be made useful without being made smaller.
