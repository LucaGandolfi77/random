# Chapter 2b — The Night Dispatcher

Lio’s accommodation request was denied before the week was out. The harbor authority said there was no equivalent post available and that dispatch required full concentration. The notice was signed by a person whose office closed at four, while the shift Lio worked began at six.

He brought the letter to the clinic folded into quarters. Mara read it once, then again, as if the second reading might reveal a humane clause hidden between the lines. Imani watched Lio watch them. He had the expression of someone observing a ritual he did not trust but had agreed to attend.

“They say they’re concerned about safety,” he said. “I’m concerned about safety. That’s why I asked for less time at the console.”

Mara asked if he would allow the psychiatrist to evaluate the memory lapses and sleep. He said yes, but only if the appointment did not go into a shared employer record. The psychiatrist, Dr. Chen, explained what would remain confidential and what would have to be shared if there were an immediate danger to him or others. Lio asked the question twice, in two different ways. Chen answered both times without irritation.

There was no simple diagnosis to place over him. The forgetfulness might relate to chronic sleep deprivation, grief, medication, anxiety, another medical issue, or several things at once. The clinician arranged a medical assessment and helped him make a short-term plan for the nights he felt too disoriented to work. The plan depended on the harbor authority honoring a safe leave, which it had not yet done. The gap between a clinically sound plan and a life that could obey it was plain enough to make Imani ashamed of every training exercise that had used the word compliance as though it were a quality of character.

Lio accepted two therapy appointments and the medical referral. He declined medication for now, saying he wanted to understand the options first. He agreed to call the union representative and gave permission for the clinic to send a letter that described his functional limitations without naming a speculative condition.

When the session ended, he asked Imani if she was the one who would write the letter.

“I can help draft it,” she said. “Mara will review it.”

“Will you make me sound worse so they listen?”

Imani was caught off guard. “No.”

“Good. I’m tired of having to choose between sounding broken and sounding fine.”

She thought about that sentence all afternoon. At university, they had been taught that symptoms needed to be made legible for services to respond. But legibility had a cost. To be believed, a person might have to describe themselves in the language of damage. To be granted privacy, they might have to endure the very conditions that made privacy impossible.

The letter did not win an accommodation. It did, however, prompt a meeting between the clinic, the union, and the harbor authority. Imani attended with Mara. The official from the authority spoke of operational requirements; the union representative spoke of a rota that had been understaffed for six months; Lio sat quietly until asked what he wanted.

“I want to keep my job,” he said. “I want enough sleep to do it properly. I want you to stop treating those things as opposites.”

The authority agreed to a two-week adjustment, subject to review. It was not justice. It was not nothing. On the way back, Imani felt the uncomfortable satisfaction of a practical result and wondered whether she was already learning to call small victories adequate because large ones were difficult to obtain.
