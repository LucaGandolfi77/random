# Chapter 2c — The Console

Lio returned to the harbor after three weeks on adjusted shifts. On his first night back, a vessel called in with a transmission that broke beneath the wind. Lio heard the channel, recognized the captain’s voice, and lost the name of the ship. For a moment he was again at the clinic, watching the word “risk” appear on a screen while everyone around him discussed what it meant.

He asked a colleague to confirm the call sign. The ship waited. The answer came through. Nothing happened. Still, when his shift ended, Lio sat in the locker room with his boots on and could not make himself stand.

He had told himself that the return would be a test. He had not noticed how much he wanted to pass it. A symptom had become an examiner; every ordinary lapse could be presented as evidence that the clinic had been wrong to support him, or that the authority had been right to worry, or that he had exaggerated, or that he had not exaggerated enough. The possibilities multiplied until they occupied the bench beside him.

His supervisor, Havel, came in to collect a radio. He did not ask if Lio was all right. He said the shift adjustment was temporary and the authority needed to know whether Lio could resume full duty by the end of the month.

“I don’t know yet,” Lio said.

“That’s not useful.”

“It’s true.”

Havel leaned against the locker. He was not a cruel man. He had worked the harbor for thirty years and believed that uncertainty at the wrong moment could cost lives. He also had a rota with three empty spaces and a wife who had begun to ask why he was never home before dawn. His concern was real; so was the way it pressed down on Lio.

The union representative arranged another meeting. The psychiatrist’s report made no prediction about Lio’s future. It described his current symptoms, the improvement in sleep, and the need for a gradual return with periodic review. The authority wanted a definitive answer. The clinicians could not provide one without pretending to know more than they did.

Lio sat through the meeting with his hands clasped between his knees. At last he said, “I can tell you what I can do tonight. I can’t tell you what I’ll be able to do in a month.”

Havel said the harbor did not operate one night at a time.

“Neither does my life,” Lio answered. “You’re asking me to promise a future so you don’t have to change the schedule in the present.”

There was no eloquence in his voice. He had not prepared the sentence. It came out blunt, almost embarrassed. But something in the room shifted. The union representative proposed a six-week review, the harbor authority agreed to maintain the shorter shift, and Havel accepted, though he looked unconvinced.

Lio left the meeting angry with Havel and guilty for being angry. He knew what it was to keep a service operating with too few people. He knew what it was to be the person the system asked to absorb the missing capacity. Knowing why a person did something did not make the thing harmless. He wished someone had told him that sooner, before compassion became another way of silencing himself.

He returned to the console the following night. The ship names arrived on time. One arrived late. He called for confirmation, and the harbor answered.
