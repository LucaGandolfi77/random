# Chapter 2d — The Near Miss

The near miss happened on a Thursday, during Lio’s fourth week back at the harbor.

A tanker called in with a change of berth. The channel was crowded, the weather turning, and one of the senior dispatchers had stepped away to speak with a pilot. Lio caught the vessel’s first message but lost the number of its assigned lane. He asked for confirmation. The reply came over two channels at once, one voice interrupting the other. Lio entered the wrong digit, saw it, corrected it, and requested a read-back before the order went out.

No ship moved into the wrong berth. No one was injured. The correction took less than a minute.

Afterward, the supervisor called it a performance concern. Lio called it a near miss. Both descriptions were accurate; neither contained the whole event. The console had been understaffed, the message had been ambiguous, and Lio had been tired. He had also made an error that could have mattered more than it did. He could not pretend otherwise in order to protect his pride.

Havel called him into an office. The report required the authority to review the incident. Lio feared that they would use it to end his accommodation and feared, too, that he might make another mistake if he returned to the full shift. The second fear embarrassed him more. He had wanted the workplace to acknowledge its part; now the evidence included his own.

At the clinic, Dr. Chen listened as Lio described the event. She asked how much he had slept, what he remembered of the radio traffic, whether he had experienced panic before or during the call. He said his hands had been steady until afterward. His mind had worked. Then, once the danger had passed, the anxiety had arrived all at once, as if it had been waiting for permission.

“Does that mean I shouldn’t be there?” he asked.

“It means we should review the conditions and the event,” Chen said. “It doesn’t answer by itself what you can do.”

The authority wanted an immediate fitness decision. The clinic refused to offer a certainty it did not have. The union requested an independent safety review. Havel resisted, then agreed when the harbor’s own protocol required it. For a week, Lio was assigned to training and documentation, work he considered a demotion until he discovered how many outdated call procedures were hidden in the manuals.

He marked them carefully. He did not want to be praised for turning distress into service to others. He wanted the hazardous procedure corrected because it was hazardous, not because his pain could be made useful.

At the review, the team concluded that the event involved fatigue, understaffing, confusing channel practice, and an individual lapse. No single cause explained it. The authority introduced a second-person verification for lane changes and agreed to keep Lio on adjusted hours while the changes were tested. He accepted the decision without calling it a victory.

That night, at home, he woke from a dream of a ship sliding toward a dark wall. He was awake enough to know where he was and frightened enough not to trust that knowledge. He called the clinic’s after-hours line. A counselor stayed with him while he named the room, the radiator, the cup on his bedside table. In the morning, he was still anxious. He went to his appointment anyway.

Nothing about the near miss proved that Lio was broken. Nothing about the correction proved he was ready for every demand the harbor could make. The space between those claims became the difficult place where his care had to happen.
