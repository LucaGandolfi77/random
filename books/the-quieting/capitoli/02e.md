# Chapter 2e — The Safety Committee

The harbor’s safety committee met in a windowless room on the second floor of the dispatch building. Lio attended with a union representative and a printed copy of the near-miss report. Havel sat at the other end of the table, his hands flat on the wood, looking less like an adversary than a man who had slept badly and did not want that fact entered into evidence.

The committee had reviewed the lane-change procedure and recommended that a second dispatcher confirm all altered berths during high traffic. The recommendation required another paid position on the night shift. The authority said it could not afford the post until the next budget cycle. The union said the next budget cycle was when the harbor had promised to hire two people the year before.

Lio listened as the argument moved around him. The near miss had begun as a question about whether he was fit to work; now the committee was deciding whether the work itself had been designed safely. He was relieved by the change in focus, then uncomfortable with the relief. He did not want the authority to use the new procedure as proof that he had been the problem all along. He did not want the union to use him as proof that every staffing concern was a crisis. He wanted the channel to be clear and the shift to have enough people.

A committee member asked if the incident would have happened if Lio had been fully rested.

“I don’t know,” Lio said. “I know I was tired. I know the message came in over two channels. I know I entered the wrong number, caught it, and asked for a check before anyone acted. I’m not going to tell you I did nothing wrong. I’m also not going to tell you the system was fine.”

Havel looked up. Lio had expected him to object. Instead Havel said he had signed the rota and knew it was short. He had done so because the harbor could not close and the authority had told him no replacements were available. He had hoped the team would manage. The confession did not repair the danger. It made the choice visible.

The committee recommended the second-person check immediately, with the cost shared between the authority’s safety budget and the labor fund. The union accepted, though its representative said the arrangement should not become permanent if the authority refused to hire. Havel agreed to a review date in six weeks.

Afterward, he asked Lio to stay behind. “I don’t think you should be back on full shifts yet,” he said. “I don’t know whether that’s because of the near miss or because I’m afraid of being responsible if something happens.”

Lio appreciated the honesty more than he wanted to. “I don’t know either.”

They arranged a gradual return and a check-in with occupational health, with Lio’s consent and clear limits on what the employer would receive. It was not a settlement between them. It was a way to work without pretending they trusted one another completely.

Walking home, Lio felt his thoughts returning to the incident, arranging themselves into new accusations. He stopped at a bakery and bought a roll. He ate it on a bench beside the harbor, watching cranes swing slowly over the water. For half an hour he did not try to decide what the event proved about him. The uncertainty remained. So did the roll’s warm, yeasty taste.
