# Chapter 2f — The New Dispatcher

Lio’s first trainee was nineteen and called Farah. She had learned the harbor’s language from manuals, where every instruction appeared to have been written by someone who knew the water would obey. In the control room, the water was never obedient. It carried fog, delayed vessels, broken radios, and the human voices of people who were tired enough to miss a word.

Lio showed Farah the console and explained the second-person check for berth changes. She asked whether the extra confirmation made the work slower. He said sometimes. She asked if it was worth it. He said it depended on what you counted as slow: the extra fifteen seconds, or the hour spent untangling a mistake.

She watched him work for several nights. On the fourth, he forgot the name of a tugboat he had known for years. He paused, checked the roster, and continued. Farah saw him do it. She did not ask whether he was all right in the middle of a call. After the shift she asked whether he wanted to talk about the lapse or whether he preferred to leave it there.

“Leave it there,” he said. Then, after a moment, “Not forever. Just tonight.”

Farah nodded. She had a brother who had been treated as if his panic made him incapable of deciding anything. She did not tell Lio this as a reason he should trust her. She told him only because he had asked why she had been careful.

Lio found himself uneasy with the role of trainer. The authority’s report described his transition as a successful return to work, though he had not returned to his old shift. The phrase suggested a completed arc. He still woke some nights with the memory of the near miss; he still checked the call signs twice. He also enjoyed teaching Farah how to listen across a noisy channel.

In a meeting with the union, he asked that the report say “transition to a training role by agreement,” not “full recovery.” The communications officer argued that the phrasing was awkward. Lio said his life had not become less awkward because the sentence was shorter.

The report was amended. The correction did not alter the committee’s budget, but the next training manual included the second-person check and a section on fatigue as a workplace condition rather than a personal weakness. Farah helped write it. She included no story about Lio. The procedure did not need his name to be useful.

One night, after a difficult shift, he and Farah stood on the dock while the fog lifted. She asked if he missed dispatch. He said yes. She asked if he regretted taking the training post. He said no, then reconsidered. “I miss parts of it. I’m not sorry I changed.”

Farah said she understood. The answer was not perfectly consistent, and neither corrected it. A life could contain a loss and a choice without turning one into proof that the other was wrong.
