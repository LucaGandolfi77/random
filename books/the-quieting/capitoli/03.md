# Chapter 3 — A House with No Curtains

Nell’s apartment stood above a closed shoe shop, its windows facing the harbor wall. The living room had no curtains. Her mother, Ilyana, had disliked hiding the weather.

Mara came for a home visit because Nell had stopped attending the map workshop. She found the girl at the kitchen table, drawing the route between home and the hospital from memory. The line wandered through three streets, doubled back at the market, and ended at the sea.

“I know the way,” Nell said. “I just can’t make my hand believe it.”

Mara sat opposite her. “That sounds frightening.”

“It’s embarrassing. People look at me like I’ve already gone.”

A kettle clicked off. Neither moved to pour it.

Mara asked whether Nell was eating, sleeping, seeing friends. Nell answered in practical units: half a bowl, four hours, no. She had been invited to a residential program. She did not want to go. Her aunt thought refusing meant she was getting worse.

“What do you think?” Mara asked.

“I think they want to solve me before I become inconvenient.”

Mara felt the old professional reflex: reassure, name the cognitive distortion, offer a plan. But Nell was not a worksheet. “I think you deserve care that listens before it decides.”

“Does that exist?”

“It should.”

Nell stared at the map. “My mum used to say a map was a promise. Not that the road would be easy. That someone had been there and wanted you to find your way.”

Mara thought of Dostoevsky, whose characters could turn one feeling into a courtroom and summon the whole soul to testify. She had once loved that intensity. Then Ada had grown ill, and Mara had learned to distrust anything that could not be measured before it hurt someone.

“Your mother sounds like she knew what maps were for,” she said.

Nell’s fingers pressed the pencil until its point snapped. Her face tightened; she did not cry. Mara did not praise her for that. She placed a sharpener on the table and waited.

When Nell began again, the new line traveled toward the harbor, then curved inland. It was not the route. It was a route.

At the door, Nell said, “If I have another panic attack, don’t tell me I’m brave.”

“What should I tell you?”

“Tell me where I am.”

“I can do that.”

“And don’t tell me my mum would want me to get better.”

Mara nodded. “I won’t pretend to know what the dead want.”

Outside, the harbor wind smelled of salt and rust. Mara realized she had made a promise without a treatment plan attached to it.
