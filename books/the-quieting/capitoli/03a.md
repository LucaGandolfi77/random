# Chapter 3a — The Room Behind the Records

Jonah brought the remaining pages in a canvas satchel that had once belonged to Tomas. The leather strap had been mended with blue thread. In the archive reading room, he set the bag down as if it contained something sleeping.

He was careful with records. It was a habit people mistook for reverence, though reverence had little to do with it. Paper could outlive its subject and become a second authority, one that spoke without correction. A clerk’s adjective could travel farther than a person’s own voice. Jonah had spent years discovering how the adjective “difficult” could be copied from one file into another until it seemed to describe a nature rather than a circumstance.

Tomas had been nineteen when their father died. Jonah was twenty-three, old enough to be called responsible and young enough to resent the title. Their father had been a soft-spoken man who repaired radios and believed that a household could be held together by listening to it. When he died, Tomas stopped repairing anything. He slept through afternoons and walked the city at night, then returned with stories about strangers he had met at bus stops. Jonah, who had learned to love through practical acts, brought home groceries and called the doctor. Tomas said he did not need a doctor. Jonah said he would make the appointment anyway.

There was nothing wrong with wanting the appointment. There was something wrong with believing the appointment was all that was needed.

The first time Tomas entered the service, the clinic was understaffed. He waited through an hour and left before anyone called his name. A month later he returned. The note said he was “guarded and intermittently uncooperative.” Jonah remembered the interview differently. Tomas had answered every question, but he had asked what would happen to the answer. The clinician, young and exhausted, had said it would be used to help him. Tomas had asked, “Who decides what help means?”

No one wrote that question down.

“Did he tell you about the Quieting?” Mara asked.

“He told me he was losing his place in things. He’d enter a room and forget the reason. Sometimes he couldn’t make a sentence arrive in order.” Jonah turned one page. “They called it stress. Later they called it noncompliance. By then he had stopped trusting the service, which they recorded as further evidence that he needed it.”

Mara read the case notes. Each observation seemed plausible in isolation. The injury, if that was what it was, emerged in the intervals: the referral that went nowhere; the missed call; the work supervisor who refused leave; the brother who thought persistence meant he was helping. A life could be failed by a chain in which every link looked ordinary.

“Do you hate me because I worked there?” she asked.

Jonah kept his eyes on the paper. “I hated that you could explain everything and still not be there. I knew it wasn’t fair. I hated you anyway.”

Mara accepted the sentence without attempting to improve it. The archive’s air was dry, the lights steady, the silence almost complete. Between them lay a man neither could bring back, and the small, available task of not lying about what had happened.
