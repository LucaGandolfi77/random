# Chapter 3b — The Blank Shore

Nell’s map had a blank stretch along the western shore. The first time Mara asked about it, Nell said she had not drawn it because the tide charts were missing. The second time, she said the coast was boring. The third time, she admitted the blank covered the place where her mother had died.

They were in the workshop above the old shoe shop. A dozen maps lay across the long table: bus routes, school districts, a nineteenth-century chart of the inlet, Nell’s own careful work in blue pencil. She had begun to mark the places where people could rest without being asked for a reason. The shopkeeper kept a kettle warm. A librarian had listed quiet corners. A youth worker had identified benches where no one got moved along for sitting too long.

“People keep asking me to put the bridge on it,” Nell said. “It’s the most direct way to the hospital.”

“And you don’t want to?”

“I don’t know. If I draw it, it becomes the route everyone expects me to take.”

Mara considered the map. “A line can show that a way exists. It can’t require you to follow it.”

“Then why do maps feel like orders?”

“Because people often use them that way.”

Nell turned the paper toward herself. “My mum used to cross that bridge to walk home. I keep thinking if I draw it, I’m claiming the place. If I don’t, I’m letting the worst thing that happened there have it.”

Mara wanted to offer something wise, and the wish itself warned her away from speaking. There were losses that resisted the elegance of an answer. “You can leave it blank until you know what you want the map to say.”

Nell’s mouth tightened. “That sounds like what adults say when they want you to get over it on your own.”

It did. Mara could hear the resemblance. “Then let me say it differently. I’ll come back next week. We can leave it blank together, if you want. Or we can draw the bridge and erase it. You don’t have to decide alone, but it is yours to decide.”

Nell watched her, judging not whether the sentence was kind but whether Mara would keep it.

The workshop group began to arrive. A retired bus driver named Mr. Daven brought a list of public toilets and the hours they were open. A girl from Nell’s school suggested marking the places where adults tended to ask questions. They argued about whether “quiet” meant silence or simply the absence of surveillance. The argument grew lively enough to make Nell smile.

At the end, she took a blue pencil and drew the bridge as a dotted line. It crossed the blank shore but did not touch the far bank. Beside it she wrote, *One possible route.*

Mara felt an unreasonable surge of pride, then corrected herself: the map was Nell’s work, and it did not exist to reassure Mara about the girl’s progress. She had to learn, again, that witnessing a person’s choice was not the same as owning its meaning.

When they left, Nell walked the long way home, along the harbor wall. Mara did not follow. She watched her go until the crowd absorbed the green sleeve of her coat, and thought how many forms of care began with the difficult discipline of not turning another person’s direction into one’s own.
