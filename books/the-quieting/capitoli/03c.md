# Chapter 3c — Tomas’s Notebook

The notebook had been in Jonah’s desk for nine years. He had found it among Tomas’s belongings after the funeral and placed it in the drawer with the intention of reading it when he could bear to. The intention became a habit; the habit became a kind of respect; the respect, eventually, a door he was afraid to open.

After Mina’s account, he took it to the archive. He did not tell Mara. He wanted one hour with the handwriting before the world of forms claimed it.

The first pages contained sounds, just as Mina had remembered: the bell at the bakery, the ferry cable, the old elevator. Tomas wrote descriptions that were less like notation than attention. The ferry cable had a “metallic throat”; the bakery bell sounded “like a spoon tapped against a glass in a house where someone is sleeping.” Jonah could hear his brother’s voice in the phrasing, not as sound but as a way of looking: a willingness to give an ordinary thing more time than it seemed to require.

Halfway through the notebook, the lists stopped. There were sketches of streets and fragments of dialogue. A page was filled with names of birds, each crossed out and rewritten. Near the end Tomas had written: *I go into the kitchen and forget the room I meant to enter. I have begun to distrust doors.*

Jonah had to put the notebook down.

He was ashamed of the relief he felt at finding the sentence. It gave shape to what he had known and doubted. It proved that Tomas had tried to tell someone, though not necessarily him. The proof did not bring Tomas closer. It only made the distance more exact.

He wondered whether to show the page to Mara. The note was not a clinical record, but it was private. It might help explain why Tomas had turned away from the clinic; it might also become another fragment used to construct a story Jonah preferred. He could not ask Tomas what he had meant. To publish the sentence would be to take the answer into his own hands.

He closed the notebook and went to Mina’s apartment. He did not bring the page. He told her what it said, as nearly as he could remember, and asked whether she thought Tomas would have wanted the notebook preserved.

“Preserved, yes,” Mina said. “Published, I don’t know. Those are different verbs.”

Jonah wrote the distinction on the inside cover of his own notebook. The words looked too plain to contain the force he felt. He realized that he had wanted an archive to be a machine for producing certainty. Instead, it kept returning him to the line between what could be known and what could only be respected.

That evening he placed Tomas’s notebook in a conservation box and entered it into the restricted catalogue. In the field marked “public description,” he wrote: *Personal notebook. Contains observations and private reflections. Access requires family consultation.* He did not include the sentence about doors.

For the first time since his brother died, he felt that keeping something from the public might be an act of fidelity rather than fear. The distinction did not absolve him. It gave him a choice he could live with for one day.
