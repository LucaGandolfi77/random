# Chapter 3d — Ilyana’s Map

Nell found her mother’s old maps in a drawer beneath the sewing machine. They were not maps of the city but of walks Ilyana had taken: a line from the apartment to the market, another from the bus stop to the sea, a third that went nowhere in particular and returned by a different street. On the back of each sheet were notes about the weather, the price of pears, which neighbor’s dog had barked.

Nell had assumed that her mother made maps because she liked knowing where she was. Looking at the scattered routes, she understood that Ilyana had drawn them because she liked being surprised by the place she already knew.

One map ended at the footbridge. The line approached the crossing, stopped, then began again on the far side. Ilyana had written, *Wind from the east. Smell of wet rope.* The note was dated two weeks before she died.

Nell sat on the floor with the paper in her hands. The map seemed to contain a life that had continued without permission from Nell’s grief: her mother had crossed the bridge, smelled the rope, walked on. The ordinary facts did not comfort her. They made the absence more specific. She could imagine the wind against Ilyana’s face, the coat collar turned up, the map folded in a pocket. She could not imagine the last moment without turning it into a scene she would be forced to watch forever.

She called Reva. Her aunt came over and sat beside her, not asking to see the map until Nell offered it. Reva recognized the route. She and Ilyana had walked there together after an argument about their mother’s house. They had not spoken for half the way, then stopped to buy hot chestnuts from a cart. Reva had forgotten the walk until she saw the marks.

“Did you make up?” Nell asked.

“We went home together.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No. It wasn’t.”

Nell traced the line with one finger. She wanted to know what they had argued about. Reva remembered only the shape of it: money, the future, the old difficulty of being sisters who had grown into different kinds of caution. The particulars had dissolved. What remained was that they had walked together while still angry.

Nell wondered if she should include the bridge on her public map. The old route was her mother’s private paper, but the bridge itself belonged to everyone and the memory belonged to no one cleanly. She decided not to copy the line. Instead she marked the bridge as a crossing, without note or symbol. The map would show a way over the water. It would not say what the way meant to her.

At the workshop, she placed Ilyana’s sheet in an envelope and wrote on the front: *Family papers. Ask before copying.* It was not a memorial and not a refusal to remember. It was a boundary around the fact that love did not make a person’s every trace public property.

When she next passed the footbridge, the wind came from the east. The rope smell was gone, replaced by diesel and salt. Nell stopped midway across, then continued to the other side. She did not feel brave. She felt cold, and present, and unwilling to make the walk stand for more than the walk.
