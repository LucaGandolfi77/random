# Chapter 3e — The Map in the Window

The library’s display window showed Nell’s map beside a printed statement explaining that the locations were suggestions, not guarantees. Someone had written beneath it in black marker: **WHO IS THIS FOR?** The question remained on the glass for three days, because the library director decided it should not be erased until the workshop had considered it.

The first answer came from a wheelchair user who said the map listed three places she could not enter. The second came from a boy who said he used the library because no one at home allowed him to sit in silence. A market vendor said the map made people think his shop was a public service. A bus driver said the marked route ignored the stop that was technically accessible but had no curb ramp.

Nell took each comment as a correction and each correction as a possible judgment of her. She had drawn the first version with care. She had also drawn it from the reach of her own body and knowledge. The map had seemed generous because she had not yet seen where its margins fell.

At the workshop, they did not erase the question. They added a second line: **WHO IS MISSING FROM THE MAP?** Then they invited people to walk the route with them. The process was slow. One location had to be removed because the owner did not want to be listed. Another was added after the library installed a ramp. The shopkeeper offered her kettle but asked not to be described as a place of care. They put a small icon for water and left the meaning open.

Nell disliked how long every decision took. She wanted the map to be finished so she could stop carrying it everywhere in her mind. Mr. Daven said the trouble with public work was that it belonged to the public even when you had drawn it yourself. Nell said that was a fine thing to say when he was not the person who had to fix all the mistakes.

“You’re right,” he said. “I’m not.”

His agreement deprived her of the argument she had prepared. She went outside and walked the route alone, stopping where the bus ramp should have been. The missing curb forced her to take the longer crossing. She counted the extra minutes, then imagined making that detour every day while already tired, already frightened, already expected to be grateful for a map at all.

When she returned, she added the longer route and marked the crossing as difficult. She did not label it unsafe. She did not know whether it was unsafe for everyone. The map acquired a small note in the corner: *Conditions change. Tell us when they do.*

Mara read the revised copy and asked whether Nell wanted her name on it. Nell said yes, but only as one of the contributors, not as the person who had “created the map.” She did not want people to mistake the work for a recovery story about a grieving girl.

The display remained in the window through the spring. The question on the glass was eventually cleaned away, but Nell copied it into the map’s margin, where it could continue to be asked.
