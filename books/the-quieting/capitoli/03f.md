# Chapter 3f — The Shore in Two Directions

On the survey trip, Nell learned that a map could be read backward. The apprentices had walked the coastline in the morning, marking the place where each tide pool began and ended. In the afternoon they followed the route back, checking whether the marks still made sense from the other direction. The shoreline looked familiar, then strange. A rock that had been an obstacle on the way out became a landmark on the return.

Nell thought of her mother’s map of the footbridge. She had copied the route in memory but not on paper. The path toward the bridge had been ordinary: apartment, market, crossing. On the way back, it might have been a different geography entirely. The maps of the living and the maps made afterward never quite aligned.

The instructor asked the apprentices to mark where they were uncertain. Nell drew a small open circle beside a stretch of coast where the tide had covered the earlier line. She had once thought uncertainty meant she had not measured carefully enough. Now she saw that it could mean the place itself had changed, or that her measurement belonged to a particular hour rather than to the world forever.

At night, she missed home. She did not miss the apartment exactly; she missed knowing which sounds belonged to it. The survey station creaked in the wind. Someone coughed in the next room. A pipe clicked behind the sink. Nell lay awake and wondered if she should tell Reva. She had promised herself she would not make the trip into evidence of recovery, and feared that admitting homesickness would be treated as evidence against her independence.

She called anyway. Reva answered, asked if she wanted to talk, and did not ask whether anything was wrong. Nell said she missed her mother. Reva said she missed her sister too. They listened to one another breathe over the phone. After a while, Reva asked what the coast looked like at night. Nell described the tide pools, dark and silver under the station lights. She described a seal turning its head toward the rocks, then slipping under the water.

“I wish your mum could see it,” Reva said.

Nell felt a familiar anger at the phrase, because it made the dead into an audience. Then she remembered that Reva was not claiming to know what Ilyana wanted. She was describing her own wish.

“I wish she could too,” Nell said.

The next morning the tide had changed again. Nell added the new edge to her field sheet, using a different color to show the hour. The line doubled back where the water had covered it. She did not erase the first mark. Both measurements remained true to the conditions in which she had made them.

When she returned to Bellwether, she brought the sheets to the workshop. The city map still showed the bridge, the benches, and the clinic. She added no private routes. But when someone asked whether the map was finished, she said, “It’s finished for this version.”

The word *version* pleased her. It allowed the map to be useful without claiming it was final, and allowed her mother’s old paper to remain what it had been: one particular walk, taken on one particular day by someone who had been alive.
