# Chapter 4 — The First Kindness

They met at the old observatory, which the city had turned into a library because the dome was too expensive to repair and the stars too far away to sue.

Jonah brought six ledger pages. Mara brought a list of what could be verified without exposing patients. They sat beneath a brass model of the solar system and argued over consent, redaction, and the difference between evidence and revenge.

“You’ve become very good at making a cage sound like a room,” Jonah said.

“And you’ve become very good at calling every locked door a conspiracy.”

He smiled, not because either of them was pleased. “We’re still compatible.”

That was the first kindness: not forgiving each other in advance.

The pages showed a pattern. The Stillness Protocol had begun as a voluntary sequence of support after repeated crises—protected leave, therapy, medical assessment, meals delivered, a person assigned to stay. Over time, its recovery measures had been simplified. A person who returned to work quickly counted as a success. A person who needed months of help became a cost. Cognitive impairment was recorded in private case notes and absent from public summaries.

“Maybe they were trying to protect the program,” Jonah said.

“They were protecting its budget.”

“Sometimes those are the same thing.”

Mara looked at him. There it was: the old argument, made more difficult by the fact that he might be right.

They had loved each other in the years after Ada died. Jonah had sat on Mara’s kitchen floor while she stared at a bowl of untouched soup. He had learned which silences were company and which were an attempt to vanish. Then she began turning every hard conversation into a clinical problem. He left after she described his grief for Tomas as “a persistent loop.”

“I’m sorry,” she said now. “For that sentence.”

Jonah’s gaze lowered. “I’m sorry I made you prove you were grieving correctly.”

Outside, the evening bells marked the hour. In the library dome, the brass planets hung in patient orbit, each one fixed to a ceiling that could not move.

Jonah reached for her hand, stopped halfway. Mara closed the space between them. His palm was warm and a little rough from archive boxes.

Their fingers rested together, not quite holding.

It was enough to begin with.
