# Chapter 4a — The Funeral Without Rain

Nell’s mother had been buried beneath a sky so blue that the mourners seemed to have arrived for the wrong occasion. Ilyana Ilyan had loved weather, according to the stories people told after her death; she had liked to stand at a window and announce rain before the clouds had decided. On the day they buried her, the sky offered no answer.

Nell had worn her mother’s green coat. The sleeves were too long, and she kept folding them back. Her aunt held an umbrella though it was not raining. A neighbor brought a dish no one remembered to take home. The minister said Ilyana had been generous, which was true, and that she had been at peace, which no one could know.

Nell remembered a detail from the service that she had not told Mara: a fly had landed on the polished coffin and walked across it as if crossing an empty table. She had watched it until it lifted away. For an instant, the death seemed less powerful than the fly’s small, untroubled body. Then she felt ashamed, because people were crying and she was thinking about an insect.

Afterward, everyone asked if she was all right. She learned to say yes because the question was not a question. It was a door someone needed her to pass through so that they could continue with the day. At home, her aunt took the knives out of the kitchen drawer. She did it gently, as if the knives themselves had become dangerous. Nell understood why and resented being understood in that way.

She had not wanted to die. She had wanted the world to stop demanding that she prove she could remain in it.

At their next appointment, Nell told Mara about the fly.

“Do you think that was a terrible thing to notice?” Mara asked.

“I think it means I’m awful.”

“Or it means you noticed a fly.”

Nell’s mouth tightened. “You always make things sound smaller.”

Mara accepted the rebuke. “I may have done that. I don’t mean your grief is small. I mean one strange thought doesn’t tell us who you are.”

“Everyone keeps asking what my mother would want.”

“What do you think she wanted?”

Nell looked down at her hands. “She wanted to know whether I’d eaten. She wanted me to stop apologizing when I was late. She wanted me to keep drawing. She wanted me to call when I got there.”

“That sounds like a lot of wanting.”

“It sounds like being her.”

They sat with that. Mara resisted the urge to convert the list into a resilience exercise. There was comfort in the remembered particulars, but it was not a cure. The fly, the too-long sleeves, the unclaimed casserole: grief preserved the trivial with the solemnity of a witness.

When Nell left, she did not appear relieved. She appeared accompanied. Mara had come to believe this was sometimes the more honest outcome.
