# Chapter 4b — Mina’s Version

Mina Ro agreed to meet Jonah in a public garden and refused to let him record the conversation.

“You can take notes afterward,” she said. “You can quote me if I approve the sentence. You cannot make my memory into evidence before I have decided what I’m giving you.”

Jonah accepted. Mara came because Mina had asked for someone from the Service to hear her account. She had been Tomas’s friend before he began missing work, before the clinic wrote “guarded” beside his name, before Jonah learned that a brother could be present every day and still not know how much a person was hiding.

Mina remembered Tomas as funny in an exact way, someone who could imitate a person’s manner without cruelty. He had carried a small notebook and made lists of sounds: the whine of the ferry cable, the chime at the bakery door, the particular cough of the elevator in his building. When his concentration began to fail, he stopped writing them down. He said he did not want a record of everything he was losing.

“He did ask for help,” Mina said. “But he didn’t always want the help people offered. The first counselor kept telling him to call his brother. He was already ashamed of needing Jonah. He wanted somebody who didn’t know the family to sit with him.”

Jonah’s hands tightened around his cup. “He never told me that.”

“He told me not to tell you.”

“Why?”

“Because he loved you. Because he didn’t want your worry to become another thing he had to manage. Because he was twenty and proud and scared. Pick the answer that lets you sleep least.”

The sentence was sharp, but Mina’s voice was not. Jonah looked toward the path. Mara knew the effort it took him not to defend himself, and the effort it took her not to soften Mina’s words on his behalf. A person’s tenderness did not make another person’s boundary unkind.

Mina had contacted the clinic after Tomas’s death. She asked that the records be reviewed, and she remembered receiving a letter that thanked her for her concern and explained that the service could not discuss another person’s care. The letter was correct. It was also a closed door written in courteous language.

“Do you want the record opened?” Jonah asked.

“I want the pattern known. I don’t want his private words printed because they make the report stronger.” She looked at Mara. “Can the city admit it failed him without turning the details of his worst days into a public lesson?”

Mara said the review could describe the system’s actions, preserve patient privacy, and seek consent from those still living. It could not make every consequence clean. Mina listened, then said she would join the community review panel if patients and families had real power to refuse disclosure.

On the walk back, Jonah was quiet. Mara did not hurry him toward forgiveness or anger.

At last he said, “I thought the archive would give me the truth.”

“It gives you a record.”

“And the difference?”

“A record can tell you what was written. The truth includes what the person chose not to tell anyone.”

He nodded, though she could see that he disliked the answer. So did she. It was not enough to heal a loss; it was enough to keep the archive from pretending it could.
