# Chapter 4c — Reva’s Night Watch

Reva stopped sleeping outside Nell’s room after Mara asked her to. She moved back to her own bed, though she left the door open and the hall light on. For several nights she woke whenever the apartment settled, listening for the small sounds of Nell moving through the dark.

She had not expected guardianship to make her feel so much like a sentry. Before Ilyana died, she and her sister had argued about everything from money to whether a person ought to own more than one winter coat. Reva had called Ilyana reckless. Ilyana had called Reva a person who could make a checklist for a thunderstorm. They had loved one another with the particular impatience of sisters who believed there would always be another Sunday in which to make peace.

Now Reva was responsible for Ilyana’s daughter, and the responsibility had turned every remembered disagreement into evidence. Had she been too hard on her sister? Had she failed to notice how lonely Ilyana was? Had she, in trying to protect Nell, begun treating the girl as the last piece of her sister rather than as a separate person?

One night Nell came into the kitchen and found Reva sitting at the table with a cup of tea gone cold. She did not ask why her aunt was awake. She poured herself water and leaned against the counter.

“I’m not going to disappear because you sleep in your room,” Nell said.

Reva felt a flush of shame. “I know.”

“You don’t know. You hope.”

The distinction was exact enough to make Reva laugh once, a broken little sound. She started to cry. Nell looked toward the hallway as though considering whether to leave, then stayed.

“I don’t know how to look after you without trying to keep you where I can see you,” Reva said.

“You could ask me.”

“What you need?”

“What I want. Sometimes they’re different.”

Reva thought of the many times she had asked Nell whether she had eaten, slept, taken the appointment, answered a message. Practical questions offered a structure for care, but they could become a wall behind which a person hid from the answer to the more frightening question: what do you want from me?

“I want you to stay alive,” Reva said.

Nell looked down at her glass. “I know. I want that too. But I can’t make every day about proving it.”

Reva stood on the other side of the kitchen table, unable to cross the distance without making the moment about her own need for reassurance. At last she asked whether Nell wanted company. Nell said yes, but only until the kettle boiled. They waited together. The kettle clicked off. Neither moved immediately.

The next morning Reva called a bereavement counselor. She did not describe the call to Nell as an act of strength. She said she had an appointment. Nell said, “Good,” and went back to her map.

The apartment remained a place where grief could interrupt the night. It no longer needed to be guarded against as if it were an intruder.
