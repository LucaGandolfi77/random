# Chapter 4d — The Radio in the Kitchen

Jonah’s father repaired radios at the kitchen table. He laid the components on a cloth so the screws would not roll under the cupboards, and he listened to each receiver before opening it, as if a machine might reveal the nature of its trouble by the sound it made when no one was speaking.

Tomas had inherited the habit of listening. Jonah had inherited the cloth, though he did not know that until after his father died and he found it folded in the tool drawer. It smelled faintly of dust and solder. For years he kept it because throwing it away seemed equivalent to discarding the last practical object that had belonged to the man. Later he understood that the cloth had become a way to postpone asking what else he might keep.

When the boys were children, Tomas would sit on the kitchen floor and hold the flashlight while their father worked. Jonah, older by four years, was expected to be elsewhere—homework, washing up, taking responsibility. He envied Tomas the pleasure of being useful without being accountable. He thought his brother was loved more easily because he was charming. He did not see how much of Tomas’s charm was attention: he knew which relative wanted a question and which wanted the subject changed.

Their father died in a hospital room with a radio beside the bed. It stopped working the week before he died. Tomas tried to fix it, but the receiver remained silent. Jonah told him to leave it; the nurses needed the table clear. Tomas obeyed. After the funeral, Jonah found the radio in a box marked for charity and took it home.

Years later, when Tomas began missing appointments and losing words, Jonah brought him the radio. He said he had repaired the wiring. Tomas turned it on, listened to the low static, and laughed. The station was wrong; it was broadcasting weather from a town far inland. For a moment they were boys again, except the laughter came with a knowledge neither could set down.

“Do you think he knew?” Jonah asked Mina once, after Tomas had died. He meant their father, meant the illness, meant the way the family had failed to name what was happening. Mina said she did not know. Jonah had hated the answer because it left his questions alive.

Now, in the archive, he wrote a description of the radio for the family catalogue: *Portable receiver, repaired by Tomas Vale; retains intermittent static.* He did not add that the static sounded to him like their father’s kitchen, because the description belonged to an object and the feeling belonged to Jonah.

He had learned that separating those things did not make the feeling smaller. It let him hold it without pretending it was a fact about the radio.

Some evenings, he turned the receiver on. It found a station, then another. He listened not for a voice from the past but for the small, unstable music of the present, arriving through distance and interference, audible only when he stayed still long enough.
