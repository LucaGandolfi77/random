# Chapter 4e — Elias and the Unindexed File

Elias found the file because it had no index number.

It had been placed in a box marked duplicate, though it was not a duplicate of anything. The folder contained a draft report from the early years of the service, with handwritten comments and a list of patients whose follow-ups had been delayed. One entry referred to a woman who had asked not to have her family contacted. Beside the name, someone had written: *Contact mother? She may know what to do.* A later hand had crossed it out and written: *Patient explicitly declined. Do not contact.*

The sequence was unremarkable. It was also the kind of small conflict the new privacy review was meant to uncover: a clinician’s wish to involve family set against a patient’s stated boundary. The folder did not say what had happened to the woman. It did not show whether anyone had respected the correction.

Elias brought it to Jonah. “This is the sort of thing that makes the archive look worse than it is,” he said. “Or better, depending on which line gets quoted.”

Jonah asked him to restrict the file until the review panel could assess whether the person was still living and whether the record contained identifying details. Elias bristled. He had spent years watching inconvenient files become invisible through caution. Jonah said caution could also be used to protect an institution from embarrassment. He knew the argument sounded familiar because it had once been his own.

They brought the question to Mina. She said the record should be reviewed by a patient advocate before anyone decided how it could be used. Elias called this another delay. Mina said that a delay imposed on an institution was not the same as a delay imposed on a person seeking care.

The file was placed in a restricted collection. The public report would describe the policy question without reproducing the name or the exchange. The woman’s refusal had been recorded, which was more than Jonah expected. Whether it had been honored remained unknown.

That uncertainty bothered him. He wanted a conclusion: someone had ignored her, or someone had corrected the record in time. The archive would not give him one. He had to accept that the evidence might end where the answer began.

Elias accused Jonah of becoming overprotective. Jonah replied that he was trying not to confuse access with entitlement. Neither withdrew the accusation. They agreed to the review procedure and continued working together, an uneasy collaboration that was more honest than their old ease.

At home, Mara asked how the day had gone. Jonah told her about the file. She said the existence of a correction mattered, even if it did not answer whether the correction had changed anyone’s behavior. Jonah said he hated that an institution could preserve both the error and the attempt to repair it and call the record complete.

“Maybe completeness is the wrong promise,” Mara said. “Maybe the promise is that we don’t hide the gap.”

He was not comforted. He wrote the gap into the catalogue anyway.
