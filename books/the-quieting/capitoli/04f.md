# Chapter 4f — The Years They Did Not Speak

For the first year after Jonah left, Mara kept his number in her phone and did not call it. At first she told herself she was respecting the boundary he had set. Then she told herself he had made the boundary permanent. The explanations differed only in who was responsible for the silence.

They had not broken apart in one argument. Their separation had been made from many smaller scenes: Jonah asking whether Mara wanted to come to his brother’s appointment, Mara saying she had a report due; Jonah returning from the appointment and trying to tell her what Tomas had said, Mara asking whether he had discussed a safety plan; Jonah saying he wanted his brother back, Mara explaining that grief often sought impossible reversals. Each sentence could be defended. Together they made a room in which neither man could speak without being interpreted.

The final argument came after Tomas missed a week of work and refused to return Jonah’s calls. Jonah said he did not want another treatment recommendation; he wanted Mara to sit with him while he was afraid. Mara told him that his fear was understandable but could not become her responsibility. She had meant that she could not save Tomas by making herself available every hour. Jonah heard that she did not want to be responsible for him at all.

He left that night. The next morning he sent a message saying he needed distance. Mara read the message in the clinic stairwell, where the paint smelled faintly of bleach. She replied, *I understand.* The words were true in the narrow sense that she understood the request. They were also a means of not asking him to stay.

For months she imagined what she might have said instead. The imagination gave her an alternate conversation in which she confessed fear, Jonah admitted anger, and neither of them mistook love for an obligation to prevent all harm. She learned, slowly, that the imagined conversation was not evidence that the real one could have ended differently. It was only evidence of what she wished she had been able to say.

When Jonah returned to Bellwether, he found her at the clinic. She had spent four years practicing sentences in which she sounded less afraid. The first time they spoke, she said the pear pastry was still good on wet days. It was not the sentence she had rehearsed. It was the sentence that had survived the years.

Now, when she thought of their separation, she did not call it a failure of love. Nor did she call it necessary. It had been a thing they had done, through fear and limited understanding, with consequences they could not undo. The relationship they rebuilt would not be the old one restored. It would have to include the knowledge that tenderness could fail to protect them from hurting one another—and the possibility that they might still choose it.
