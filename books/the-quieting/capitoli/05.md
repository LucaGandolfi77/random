# Chapter 5 — The Name in the Ledger

Sera did not deny signing the revisions.

She read the copy in her office, one hand resting on the desk beside a mug of tea. The walls held no certificates, only photographs: the first Room of Return, a crowd outside the clinic on its opening day, Sera at thirty with a paper banner in her hands.

“We were going to lose the service,” she said. “The council wanted proof it worked. We had patients who were alive because we could reach them early. We had people sleeping in corridors before the rooms existed. The report was a bridge.”

“You removed outcomes that made the bridge look unsafe.”

“I removed identifying details and unresolved cases. Then someone changed the summary. I should have fought harder.”

“Did you know about Ada?”

For the first time, Sera looked away.

Mara’s sister appeared in memory not as a body or a final day, but as a girl stealing cherries from the market and leaving the pits on Mara’s windowsill. Ada was funny, then tired, then ill in ways the family had mistaken for stubbornness. Her panic episodes had come after their father’s death, then after a relationship ended, then after years in which every problem had been treated as a new beginning. She had received help late. She died by suicide at twenty-nine. Mara had never believed that grief alone had caused her death. She had spent years afraid that it had been allowed to become unbearable.

Sera opened a drawer and removed a thin folder. ADA VENN was typed on the tab.

“I kept this out of the main archive,” she said. “I told myself it was for privacy.”

Mara could not touch the folder.

Inside was a clinical note from the week Ada first came to the Service. She had asked whether a mind could become too tired to recognize its own life. The note marked her “highly verbal, low immediate risk.” It recommended an appointment in three weeks.

Three weeks. Mara had been in another city, finishing her training. She had said she would call on Sunday. She had called on Monday.

A second document showed an administrative transfer: Ada’s follow-up had been deferred during the budget freeze. No one had erased the event. They had simply made it too ordinary to notice.

“I’m sorry,” Sera said.

Mara looked at her. “You don’t get to apologize for all of it.”

“No. But I can apologize for my part.”

Mara took the folder. It weighed almost nothing. It contained a life reduced to paper, and a thousand things the paper could not say.
