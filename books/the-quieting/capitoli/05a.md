# Chapter 5a — Sera’s First Room

Sera Okafor had been twenty-six when she first saw a Room of Return that worked.

It was not called that yet. It was a storage room in a school, with a broken radiator and two chairs borrowed from the teachers’ lounge. A girl of fourteen had locked herself in after a panic episode. The principal was outside the door, frightened and angry. Sera, then a junior psychiatrist, sat on the floor in the corridor and talked about the rain.

She did not ask the girl to open the door. She did not tell her she was safe, because Sera did not know whether the girl felt safe. She said the corridor smelled of wet coats and chalk. She said there was a radiator clicking as it cooled. She said she would stay there until the girl wanted to speak, or until the next person came to relieve her, whichever happened first.

After forty minutes, the girl opened the door. She was embarrassed, then hungry. Sera went with her to the cafeteria and found a sandwich.

It seemed a modest success, but the girl returned to school. Her mother later wrote to thank the clinic. The letter was photocopied and passed around offices until the story acquired a moral: stay, listen, help the person come back. When the first dedicated room opened, Sera stood in the doorway and thought that the city had found a way to institutionalize kindness without diminishing it.

The change had not come all at once. It had come through budget lines, training manuals, quarterly outcomes, revised definitions. The first report had counted whether someone felt heard. The next counted whether they completed a plan. The next counted return to work. The figures were not meaningless. Politicians funded what they could defend, and the clinic had to survive the language of defense.

Sera had told herself that the compromised measure was temporary. She had signed the summary because the service was threatened. She had signed again because a second delay would be harder to explain than the first. She had learned to make small moral bargains with the confidence of someone who knew precisely what the money would buy: a night nurse, another psychiatrist, the door that would stay open.

It was this knowledge that made Mara’s accusation hard to answer. Sera could not retreat into innocence. She had helped people and misled them. The one truth did not cancel the other, which was exactly why she had wanted a cleaner story.

On the evening after their conversation, she went into the original Room of Return, now used for staff meetings. The chairs had been replaced. The radiator had been repaired. On the wall hung a framed photograph of the opening ceremony, Sera’s younger face bright with the conviction that a good institution could be kept good by the intentions of its founders.

She took the frame down. Not to hide it. To see the dust it had left behind.
