# Chapter 5b — Her Mother’s Kitchen

Mara’s mother still lived in the house where the girls had grown up, though she had given away most of the furniture and moved her bedroom to the ground floor. The stairs had become difficult after a fall. She told Mara it was not worth discussing, as if the body were a political opponent one could defeat by declining debate.

Mara visited on a Sunday afternoon. Their mother had made lentil soup and forgotten the bread in the oven. The kitchen smelled of cumin and scorched crust. It was the smell of the family’s old life: a meal rescued at the last moment, a complaint turned into laughter, Ada reaching across the table to steal the crisp corner.

“You look tired,” her mother said.

“I am.”

“Are you eating?”

“Sometimes.”

“Sometimes is not an answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

Her mother frowned, then put another spoonful of soup in Mara’s bowl. The gesture contained love and an old refusal to accept the limits of what she could do. Mara had inherited both.

They did not speak of the hearing at first. Her mother asked about Jonah, in the tone of someone asking about a familiar street that might have been rebuilt. Mara said they were seeing one another. Her mother asked whether it was serious. Mara said she did not know. Her mother said that at her age people ought to know. Mara nearly explained that the question was not a diagnosis and did not require a rating. Instead she watched the steam lift from the soup and vanish.

“I used to think if I kept the house steady, you girls would be safe,” her mother said. “After your father died, I made everything into a schedule. Meals, school, the bills. Ada hated it. You liked it.”

“I liked knowing what came next.”

“Your sister liked making sure it didn’t.”

Their mother’s eyes shone, but she did not cry. Mara wondered whether she was waiting for permission, or whether she had simply learned that crying in front of her daughters created work for them. The city’s unspoken rules had begun at home long before the protocol had a name.

“Do you blame yourself?” Mara asked.

“For what?”

“For not seeing how bad things were.”

Her mother stared at the table. “I blame myself for many things. It doesn’t mean I’m right.”

Mara felt her own breath slow. The sentence was more useful than the reassuring answers she had spent years giving to other people. Her mother was not offering a conclusion. She was admitting that remorse, like grief, could be both sincere and inaccurate.

They ate the bread, scraping away the black edges. Her mother asked if the city would listen at the hearing. Mara said she did not know. When she left, her mother pressed a jar of soup into her hands and said she would call on Sunday.

“Half past four?” Mara asked.

Her mother looked confused, then remembered. The old hour had belonged to Ada, but the family had let it remain after her death, an empty appointment no one had formally canceled.

“If you want,” her mother said.

Mara carried the jar home with both hands, as if it were warm enough to keep a memory from spilling.
