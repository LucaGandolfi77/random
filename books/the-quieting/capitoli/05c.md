# Chapter 5c — The Hour That Stayed Empty

On the first Sunday after the discovery of Ada’s file, Mara called her mother at half past four. Her mother answered on the second ring, though she had been standing beside the telephone since four.

Neither said why the call mattered. They talked about the soup, a neighbor’s new dog, the price of pears. The conversation moved around Ada as a boat moves around a buoy: not touching it, never forgetting where it is.

At last her mother asked whether Mara had read the note in the file.

“Yes.”

“Did it say she wanted me?”

“It said, ‘Call her sister.’ It didn’t say which sister.”

Her mother breathed in, then out. “I always assumed she meant you.”

“So did I.”

“Did anyone call?”

“No.”

The silence that followed was not the one Mara remembered from childhood. Her mother did not fill it with a practical question. Mara could hear the television in the next room, turned low. Somewhere outside, a child shouted and was answered by another child. The world made its small insistence on continuing.

“I should have noticed,” her mother said.

Mara was tempted to say that no one could have known, that illness was complicated, that the service had failed, that grief distorted memory. Every sentence was true. Together they would have been an escape route.

“You did notice some things,” Mara said. “You didn’t know what they meant.”

“Is that enough to forgive myself?”

“I don’t know if forgiveness works like a payment. I don’t know if we can decide it once and be done.”

Her mother was quiet. Mara imagined her in the kitchen, seated at the table, with the old watch on its shelf. She pictured the house as it had been when all three women lived there: Ada singing badly in the bathroom, their mother pretending not to hear, Mara setting the clock forward five minutes because she thought punctuality might protect them from something.

“What would you have done?” her mother asked.

Mara knew she meant if she had understood. She wanted to say everything. She wanted to say she would have gone to the appointment, called the clinic, brought Ada home, sat beside her all night. The fantasy of competent rescue was seductive because it turned love into a power one could wield.

“I would have tried to help,” she said. “I might still have made mistakes.”

“Your father used to say trying was all a person had.”

“He said that because he was always late.”

Her mother laughed, and then they both cried. Not dramatically. Their voices broke, stopped, began again. Mara felt no revelation, only the labor of staying on the telephone while her body wanted to retreat into professional language.

When they hung up, the hour remained half past four. It had not become Ada’s again, exactly. It had become a time her mother and daughter could inhabit together without pretending their grief was identical. Mara sat with the receiver in her hand until the line went silent.
