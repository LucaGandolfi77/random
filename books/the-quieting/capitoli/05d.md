# Chapter 5d — The First Time Imani Lied

Imani’s first lie at the clinic was not in a report. It was in the answer she gave her mother when asked how the work was going.

“Fine,” she said, though she had spent the day replaying Lio’s near miss and wondering whether she had missed a sign, whether her advocacy for his accommodation had influenced how she saw the incident, whether she had become attached to being the clinician who understood him. Her mother heard the strain and asked if something was wrong. Imani said no.

She had grown up in a house where distress was treated as a weather system: real, inconvenient, and best not made worse by discussing it. Her mother had not been cruel. She had worked two jobs after Imani’s father left and had learned that some mornings required everyone to get up regardless of what they felt. When Imani’s mother was grieving, she cleaned. When Imani was frightened, she made lists. The family’s care had been practical and sincere, and it had also taught Imani that feeling could be postponed until the work was done.

At the clinic, she had decided that she would not repeat that lesson. She would listen, stay, invite every feeling into the room. She did not see at first that this ideal could become its own command: a patient who declined to talk could make her feel rejected; a person who left before the session ended could make her wonder whether she had failed.

Mara noticed the change before Imani did. In supervision she asked why Imani had offered Lio a second appointment on a day he had already said he needed to sleep.

“I thought consistency would help.”

“Did he ask for another appointment?”

“No.”

“What did you want the appointment to do?”

Imani looked at the floor. She had wanted to make certain he was safe. She had wanted the case to remain inside the clinic where she could see it. She had wanted relief from the fear that something might happen beyond her knowledge.

The admission embarrassed her. It also made her feel less alone. Mara did not praise her for insight; she asked what she would do differently. Imani decided to tell Lio she had over-offered, clarify that he could choose the rhythm of follow-up, and consult the team about the near miss without treating it as proof that he required more supervision.

That night she called her mother again. She said the work was hard, and she was afraid she was making mistakes. Her mother was quiet for a moment.

“Are you helping people?”

“Sometimes.”

“Are you getting help?”

“I’m trying.”

“Then tell me the part you can tell me.”

Imani described the long shifts and the forms, not the patient details. Her mother listened. She did not fix the story. Before they hung up, she said she had been wrong to treat every difficult feeling as something that could be scheduled for later.

Neither of them knew whether the confession would change the past. The next morning, Imani arrived at the clinic with less certainty and a little more room to hear what a person was actually asking.
