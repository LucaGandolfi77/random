# Chapter 5e — The Chair by the Window

Mara’s mother had moved the chair beside the kitchen window. She said she wanted more light for reading, although the afternoon sun fell on the chair only in winter. Mara suspected the change had more to do with the view of the street, where the neighbor’s dog crossed every morning at the same time and the delivery van stopped beneath the elm.

They spent the afternoon sorting papers from the cupboard. Her mother had kept old appointment cards, school reports, receipts, and a note in Ada’s handwriting reminding her to buy tea. Each object seemed to offer a reason to pause. Mara’s mother held the note a long time, then asked whether she could keep it.

“Of course.”

“I don’t want to keep everything,” she said. “I don’t want to throw away the wrong thing.”

Mara thought of the archive, with its formal rules for what was preserved and what was restricted. Families had no archivist. They had a cupboard and the knowledge that one day someone else would have to decide what the papers meant.

They found a bundle of letters from Mara’s father to their mother, written before the girls were born. Some were ordinary—train delays, a recipe, a complaint about a hotel bed. One contained a passage about being afraid that he would be a bad father because he did not know how to make a home. Mara’s mother read it twice.

“He was very good at fixing radios,” Mara said.

“He was better at fixing radios than people.”

“Most of us are.”

Her mother gave her a look. “Don’t make a rule out of him.”

Mara realized she had been preparing to turn the letter into an origin story: the father’s fear, the daughters’ different temperaments, the family’s habit of practical care. The temptation to find a pattern was not false in itself. It became false when the pattern claimed to explain the person.

They kept the letter. They discarded a stack of expired warranties and a school newsletter. Her mother asked whether Mara thought the service would survive the public review. Mara said she did not know. Her mother wanted to believe that the work Mara did would be protected because it mattered. Mara wanted that too, and knew that importance did not guarantee funding.

“Do you ever wish you’d done another job?” her mother asked.

“Sometimes I wish the job had been easier to love.”

Her mother looked toward the street. “You don’t have to love the work every day to have done good work.”

Mara thought of the old man with the oranges, the patients who had refused treatment, the chart that could not say whether a person’s return to work had been chosen. She thought of Ada, whose file had become a case in a public argument she would never hear.

“No,” Mara said. “But I have to keep asking who it’s good for.”

Her mother nodded. The light moved across the table. They placed the letters in a clean box, not because they had decided what the letters meant but because they wanted them to remain available for another reading.
