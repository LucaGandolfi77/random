# Chapter 5f — What the Family Would Not Publish

The review panel asked Mara’s mother whether she wanted to contribute a statement about Ada’s case. The request was carefully worded. It said the report could include family perspectives and that participation was voluntary. It also included a deadline, a word limit, and an authorization form. The language was respectful, and the form still made the family’s grief appear to be a resource waiting to be used.

Mara’s mother read it at the kitchen table. She said she did not want Ada’s last months described in public. She did want the report to name the missed follow-up. She wanted the clinic to say that the appointment had been delayed and no one called back. She did not want the city to print Ada’s questions, her diagnosis, or the details of the day she died.

Mara agreed. She had wanted, at first, to say more: to make the report impossible to dismiss by giving it the intimate force of Ada’s voice. She had imagined the public would understand if they could hear her sister’s words. Then she asked herself who would benefit from that exposure, and whether Ada had ever agreed to become the evidence Mara now wanted.

“She was my daughter,” her mother said. “I don’t want her made into a symbol. But I don’t want the service to pretend nothing happened.”

Mara felt a complicated tenderness for the sentence. It contained the same two desires that had divided her own mind: to protect Ada’s privacy and to make the failure known. Neither desire canceled the other. Neither was pure. Her mother wanted to spare herself the spectacle of her daughter’s private life. She also wanted the city to take responsibility. Mara wanted the truth made public, and wanted to control the telling so it would remain truthful.

They drafted a short statement together. It said the family had sought support, that a follow-up was delayed, and that the family had not been contacted afterward. It did not name the reason Ada had first come to the clinic. It did not describe her illness or death. Her mother signed it. Mara did not.

The panel accepted the statement and agreed to include it without the family name. The report would say that a family member had confirmed the gap in follow-up. Mara disliked the anonymity, then understood that she disliked not being able to manage the meaning of the loss. The panel had kept the boundary her mother requested. Mara would have to let that be enough.

At the hearing, a journalist asked whether the family statement referred to Ada Venn. Mara declined to confirm. The reporter said the information was already circulating. Mara said circulation was not consent. She did not raise her voice. She watched the reporter write the sentence down, uncertain whether it would appear in print.

That evening her mother called and asked if the statement had been used. Mara said yes, in the form they had agreed to. Her mother said, “Good.” Then, after a pause, “I’m glad it’s not all of her.”

Mara understood. No report could contain a whole person. Privacy did not make the record false; disclosure did not make it complete. Somewhere between them was the small, difficult act of telling only what one had the right to tell.
