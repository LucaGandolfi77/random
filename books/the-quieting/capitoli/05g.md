# Chapter 5g — The Appointment Card

Mara found an old appointment card in her coat pocket while waiting for a bus. It had been folded in half and softened by years of carrying. The name was Ada’s, the date from the winter before she died. On the back, Mara had written *pick up coat / ask about Saturday* in her own handwriting.

She could not remember which coat. She could not remember what Saturday had meant. The card returned no scene, only the impression of a day full of errands and the assumption that there would be another one. She held it beneath the bus shelter while rain tapped the plastic roof. A woman beside her shifted a grocery bag from one hand to the other. The city went on with the ordinary insistence that had always seemed cruel to Mara and now sometimes seemed merciful.

She brought the card to her mother. They sat at the kitchen table and tried to place the date. It fell within the period when Ada’s clinic appointment had been deferred. Mara’s note suggested she had planned to see her sister that weekend. Her mother remembered that Ada had come to dinner. She remembered the coat was red, perhaps. The details did not make a story.

“Do you want to keep it?” her mother asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Then keep it until you know.”

The simplicity of the answer unsettled Mara. Her mother was offering neither a symbolic meaning nor a demand to let go. She placed the card in a small envelope marked with the date. It was not a medical record. It was not a message from Ada. It was evidence that Mara had once expected to ask her something on Saturday.

In the following weeks, she found herself thinking about the card during sessions. Patients often carried objects that had not been chosen as symbols: a receipt, a key, a folded letter. Clinicians asked what the object meant. Sometimes the answer was that it was in the pocket. Mara learned not to demand a meaning before the person had decided whether there was one.

One patient brought a bus ticket from the day she had left a hospital. She kept it because she had been afraid she would forget the route home. Another kept a grocery list written by her father. Neither wanted the objects interpreted. They wanted a place to mention them without being asked to turn the mention into a lesson.

Mara understood that her own attention to the appointment card could also become a kind of private ritual, and that ritual did not need to be justified. She could keep the card because she wanted to. She could also one day discard it without betraying Ada.

For now, it remained in the envelope. The appointment on its front had passed years before. Saturday had come and gone. The card did not tell Mara whether she had picked up the coat or asked about the plan. It told her only that she had intended to. The intention was imperfect and human, and she let it remain so.
