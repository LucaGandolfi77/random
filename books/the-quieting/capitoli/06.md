# Chapter 6 — The Grammar of Being Well

Sera asked Mara to come to the clinic before opening. The corridors were empty, the lights softened for the morning shift. In Interview Room Three, a child’s drawing showed a blue house with a yellow door. Someone had taped it crookedly.

“I am not asking you to lie,” Sera said. “I’m asking you to see the consequences of truth delivered without care.”

“You mean the council could cut funding.”

“I mean the clinics could close. The people who need a psychiatrist at two in the morning will not be rescued by a correct report.”

Mara remembered her first night on the crisis line: a man who could not say his daughter’s name, a nurse who stayed on the phone until dawn, the quiet miracle of finding a bed. None of it was propaganda. Neither was the concealed ledger.

“Why didn’t you publish the full numbers after the funding stabilized?”

“Because it didn’t stabilize. Because every year there was another budget, another election, another person promising that care could be delivered cheaper if we made it stricter.” Sera pressed her thumb against the rim of her mug. “I believed a flawed shelter was better than no shelter. I still believe that. I no longer know whether the shelter has started to resemble a locked room.”

Mara thought of Tolstoy’s Ivan Ilyich, whose life had looked correct from outside and felt, at the end, like a mistake. She had read the story in medical school, when she thought its warning belonged to other people: that a life could be well arranged and still be false.

“You made the form the measure of the person,” Mara said.

“No. The council did.”

“And you let them.”

“Yes.”

The honesty was more painful than defense.

Sera stood and crossed to the crooked drawing. She straightened one corner, then stopped. “I helped build the rooms because I wanted people to have somewhere to go. I never wanted the rooms to become a way of keeping them useful.”

Mara saw the old doctor beneath the official, the woman who had sat with her after Ada’s funeral and refused to call the death a lesson.

“If we disclose this,” Sera said, “we have to tell the whole truth. The service saves lives. It also failed people. The risk is not a reason to silence people, and distress is not proof they’re damaged. We have to say all of it.”

Mara nodded. “Then that is what I’ll say.”

Sera gave a small, tired smile. “You always were better at the hard sentence.”

“No,” Mara said. “I’ve just had a long time to practice avoiding it.”
