# Chapter 6b — The Price of the Night Shift

The clinic’s night shift had three people for a building designed to need seven. The shortage was not visible in the annual report because the report counted appointments completed, not the hours during which no one could answer the phone.

Imani worked one of those shifts with Mara. Near midnight, a caller named Dena asked if her husband’s panic could make him permanently stupid. She used the word as if it were a verdict she had already received from someone else. Mara answered carefully: the episodes did not allow them to predict such an outcome; persistent changes in memory or concentration deserved proper assessment, and support was available. She asked whether Dena’s husband was safe, whether he was with her, whether he had been able to sleep or eat. The practical questions calmed neither of them at once. They made the situation describable.

Dena’s husband had been crying for days after their infant’s hospitalization. The baby was expected to recover. Dena was frightened by the sight of a man she loved unable to stop shaking, and more frightened by the relief she felt when he went to work, because his absence allowed her to pretend the shaking belonged to somewhere else.

Mara arranged an urgent appointment with a psychiatrist and a home visit from the crisis team. She did not promise that treatment would erase the experience. Imani watched her record each choice and wondered how much of care could be captured by a box marked “plan agreed.”

At two in the morning, they sat in the staff kitchen. Mara drank tea gone cold. Imani asked whether the service was doing enough.

“Enough for whom?” Mara said.

“For the people who call.”

“No.”

The answer was so plain that Imani looked up.

“We do useful work,” Mara continued. “That matters. We also turn people away, lose referrals, make mistakes, and sometimes protect the institution before the patient. You must be able to see all of those things at once, or you’ll end up worshipping the work because you need it to be good.”

Imani felt a defensive heat in her chest. “If I think about every failure, I won’t be able to do the job.”

“Then don’t think about every failure all at once. Think about the one in front of you. Then come back tomorrow and ask what made it likely.”

There was fatigue in Mara’s face that Imani had not noticed before. It did not make her less authoritative. It made her authority less magical, which was perhaps a necessary correction.

At the end of the shift, Imani found a note from the staffing office: the night rota would be reduced again next month. She read it twice, then photographed it for the union representative. The decision felt both insignificant and rebellious. She had not yet learned whether a person could change a system without letting the system change the shape of her conscience. She suspected the question would take more than a training year to answer.
