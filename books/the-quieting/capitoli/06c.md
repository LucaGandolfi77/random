# Chapter 6c — The Review Board

Sera sat before the review board without her old title on the nameplate. She had chosen to wear no badge. The room was smaller than the council chamber, with a table shaped like a narrow boat and a pitcher of water that no one touched. The board’s chair, a retired judge named Pell, asked whether Sera believed the public summaries had misrepresented the evidence.

“Yes,” Sera said.

“Did you know they were misleading when you approved them?”

“I knew they were incomplete. I told myself the omissions protected the program until we could gather better data.”

“Did you tell the public that?”

“No.”

She did not add that the public had been angry, that the clinics had been threatened, that people had been waiting for care in rooms without doors. Those circumstances mattered; they were not a defense that erased the decision. Sera had spent years describing the pressures as if they were actors in the room, though she had been the person who signed the paper.

The board asked about the deferred follow-ups, about the decision to measure recovery by return to ordinary function, about patients classified as uncooperative. Sera explained that the service had begun with a language of support and gradually adopted a language of compliance. She had not written every policy. She had trained the people who used them. The distinction was not the shelter she had hoped it might be.

After the hearing, Mara waited with her outside the room. Neither had been asked to sit together, and neither moved away.

“Do you think they’ll dismiss me?” Sera asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Would you want them to?”

Mara felt the difficulty of the question. She wanted accountability, and she wanted the woman who had taught her how to stay in a room with grief. She did not want one desire to disguise itself as the other.

“I want the review to be honest,” she said. “I don’t know what consequence would make it honest.”

Sera nodded. “That is more difficult than wanting me punished.”

“I’m not sure it is.”

For the first time in months, Sera laughed. It was a brief, tired sound. She told Mara about the first patient she had helped in the borrowed schoolroom, the girl who opened the door after forty minutes. Sera had kept a copy of the thank-you letter. She had once read it as proof that the service was good. Now she understood it proved only that one act had helped one person. The rest of the institution had to be judged by what it did after the letter was framed.

Mara asked whether she still believed the clinics should exist.

“Yes,” Sera said. “More than ever. That is why I cannot defend the things we did to keep them untouched.”

They walked down the stairs together. At the exit Sera turned toward the street where the clinic stood. “Do you think a person can outlive a mistake without pretending it was small?”

Mara thought of Ada, of the note, of her own absence. “I hope so.”

The answer was not a verdict. It was all she could offer.
