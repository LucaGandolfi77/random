# Chapter 6d — A Letter Returned

Sera’s letter to the former patients was returned unopened. The envelope had been redirected twice and marked **NO LONGER AT THIS ADDRESS** in a hand she did not recognize. She held it in the clinic office and felt the familiar urge to interpret the refusal: perhaps the person had moved, perhaps they did not want contact, perhaps someone else had opened the mail. The possibilities were an invitation to make the event easier than it was.

She wrote to the review panel and asked that the address not be searched further. If the person wanted to be found, there were channels to contact the service. The letter would remain sealed in the restricted file, not as evidence of reconciliation but as evidence that an apology had been attempted and had not been accepted.

A week later, another response arrived. The writer said they remembered the clinic. The care had helped them through a season in which they could not sleep. They also remembered being told that returning to work quickly would show they were recovering. They had returned because their contract was ending. The clinic had congratulated them. They did not know whether the staff had understood the pressure.

The letter ended: *I do not want a meeting. I want the report to say that I needed more time and did not get it. Do not use my name.*

Sera read the letter alone. She felt gratitude that the person had written and shame that the report had not already said what they were now asking it to say. She passed the request to the panel and asked for an independent editor to verify that the case could not be identified by the combination of dates and details. It was a modest act of compliance with a boundary. She could not know whether the writer would regard it as enough.

At the review meeting, a former colleague argued that Sera was being made a scapegoat for the council’s demand to show results. He had worked beside her in the first clinic and knew what they had done with too little money. He said the service might not exist if Sera had refused to sign the summaries.

“That may be true,” Sera said. “It does not make the summaries honest.”

He accused her of accepting blame to preserve the institution. She said perhaps she was doing that too. The possibility was uncomfortable, but Sera no longer trusted herself to identify a pure motive before acting.

The board’s final recommendation was that Sera be removed from executive leadership for two years, required to participate in an external accountability process, and permitted to continue clinical work under supervision. The council accepted the recommendation. She read the decision once, folded it, and put it in her bag.

Mara met her at the clinic door. “How are you?”

“Angry,” Sera said. “Relieved. Ashamed. I don’t think those cancel one another.”

“No.”

Sera looked through the glass at the waiting room. “I would still build the rooms.”

“Would you build them the same way?”

“No.”

It was not the comfort of redemption. It was a beginning with consequences attached.
