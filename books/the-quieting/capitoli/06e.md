# Chapter 6e — The Patient Who Wanted a Rule

A young woman named Kira came to the clinic asking for a rule that would tell her when it was safe to cry.

She had read about the Quieting online. Her father had been diagnosed with cognitive impairment after a stroke, and Kira feared that any intense emotion might take her in the same direction. She had begun suppressing tears, then experienced panic when suppression failed. Her friends told her to let it out. A counselor at school told her to breathe. Neither answer gave her what she wanted: certainty that feeling would not destroy her.

Imani led the intake with Mara observing. She explained what the service knew and did not know, emphasizing that grief, tears, and panic did not make a person doomed or stupid. Kira asked the question again in a different form: how many times could she cry before it became dangerous?

“There isn’t a number I can give you,” Imani said.

Kira’s eyes filled. “So you can’t help me.”

Imani felt the urge to make the answer more comforting. She did not. “I can help you with the panic and the fear. I can help you get an assessment if you’re having ongoing memory or concentration problems. I can’t promise you a life without risk.”

“That’s not help.”

“It may not be the help you wanted.”

The sentence sounded harsher than Imani intended. She wanted to take it back, but Kira was looking at her, and she let the words stand. Kira said she wanted someone to tell her that crying could not harm her. Imani said that emotion by itself was not a countdown, but she would not pretend to know what every repeated severe episode might mean for every person. She could offer a psychiatrist’s assessment and a plan for when Kira felt overwhelmed, not a guarantee against the future.

Kira chose the appointment and asked to bring her father. Imani checked that she wanted him present for the whole visit. Kira said yes, then said she might change her mind once they arrived. They wrote the choice into the appointment note with the possibility of changing it.

After Kira left, Imani asked Mara whether she had sounded cold.

“You sounded clear,” Mara said. “It may have felt cold because she asked for certainty and you refused to invent it.”

“Could I have said it more gently?”

“Probably. But gentleness isn’t measured by whether the person likes the answer. It’s measured partly by whether you leave them alone with it.”

Imani made a note to call Kira the next day and check whether she wanted the appointment. She did not call to persuade her. The distinction mattered. Kira might decide that uncertainty was intolerable and decline care. She might return. Imani would have to let the answer belong to her.

That evening Imani thought of the word “safe,” and how often people used it to mean both “not in danger” and “not frightened.” The two meanings overlapped but were not the same. The work, she was learning, was not to eliminate fear by naming a person safe. It was to remain with them while they found out what the fear could and could not tell them.
