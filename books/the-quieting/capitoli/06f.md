# Chapter 6f — The Clinician Who Left

Caz Mendel resigned on a Tuesday and returned her clinic badge in an envelope. She had worked the night shift for nine years, first as a counselor, then as a supervisor. She left no dramatic letter, only a short statement saying she could no longer provide care at the standard she believed patients deserved under the existing staffing conditions.

The director called it a personal decision. Caz’s colleagues called it burnout. Both phrases contained part of the truth and concealed the rest. Caz had been sleeping badly, had begun to dread the telephone, and had missed her own appointments. She had also asked for another staff member on nights for three years. Each time the request had been deferred to the next budget review.

Mara found Caz in the small garden, sitting on the stone wall. She asked if she could sit beside her. Caz said yes, then said she did not want Mara to persuade her to stay.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You might have.”

Mara accepted the correction. She had thought of Caz as indispensable, which was another way of making a person into a function. The clinic would suffer when she left. That fact did not entitle the clinic to keep her.

Caz said she still believed in the work. She no longer trusted herself to do it while angry at every person who needed her. The anger had begun to travel ahead of the patient, arriving in the room before the person did. She was afraid of becoming the kind of clinician who called a boundary compassion because she had run out of capacity.

“Are you going somewhere else?” Mara asked.

“To my brother’s town. I’m going to sleep, and then I’ll decide.”

The answer was not a professional plan. It sounded like a life.

When Caz left, the night rota became harder to cover. The clinic’s managers asked staff to work additional shifts while a replacement was recruited. Imani objected that the interim plan would reproduce the conditions Caz had named. The director said there was no immediate alternative. Imani asked whether the clinic could reduce appointments rather than reduce the time given to each patient. The director said that too would harm people.

The meeting ended without agreement. Mara felt the familiar temptation to say that every option was bad and therefore the staff must simply endure. She recognized how easily necessity could become a permanent moral permission.

The clinic canceled two group sessions and used the freed hours for crisis coverage. Patients were informed and offered alternatives. Some were angry. One person said they had finally begun to trust the group. Mara apologized without claiming the apology restored what had been interrupted.

Caz wrote once, three months later, to say she was sleeping again. She did not say whether she would return to the profession. Mara read the note, then placed it in the staff file with Caz’s permission. It recorded no recovery metric. It recorded that a person had left a harmful arrangement and was finding out what came next.
