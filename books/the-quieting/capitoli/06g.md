# Chapter 6g — The Clinician’s Meeting

The staff meeting after the petition was tense enough that the director asked everyone to keep their voices low. No one raised a voice. The tension lived in the pauses, in the papers aligned at the edge of the table, in the way each person addressed the director rather than one another.

Some clinicians believed the revised protocol had made them less able to act. They described patients who refused follow-up, families who called in fear, and moments when waiting for consent felt like an abandonment disguised as ethics. Other clinicians said the old categories had let the service override people who were distressed but not incapable, and that the categories had often made care contingent on being visibly unwell.

Mara listened until she recognized that both groups were describing the same fear from different positions: the fear of being responsible for harm one could not prevent. The policy could distribute that responsibility, but it could not abolish it. The question was whether staff had enough support to make decisions together, and whether patients could challenge decisions after the urgency passed.

A young clinician said she had spent the previous night wondering whether a patient who left the room was safe. She had called the patient twice, then felt ashamed that the calls might have been intrusive. A senior nurse said she had been in that position for twenty years and still did not always know what to do. She was not proud of the uncertainty. She was tired of people pretending that experience had cured it.

Sera sat near the wall, no longer chairing the meeting. She spoke once: “The protocol cannot be asked to protect clinicians from every difficult judgment. We built it partly because we wanted a defense against blame. Patients paid for that wish.”

A psychiatrist objected that the service needed thresholds or it would become arbitrary. Sera agreed that thresholds mattered. The disagreement was not between rules and no rules, she said, but between a rule used as a guide and a rule used as a substitute for attention.

The meeting ran past its scheduled end. They agreed to create a rotating consultation group so a clinician faced with uncertainty could speak to another professional before deciding, where time allowed. The group would document disagreement rather than forcing a false consensus. The plan required paid hours the clinic did not yet have. The director said she would take the request to the council.

Afterward, Imani asked Mara whether the meeting had helped.

“It made the problem more visible.”

“That’s not the same as helping.”

“No.” Mara collected her notes. “But a hidden problem can only be solved by the people who already know it exists.”

Imani looked toward the door where the staff were leaving in pairs. “And if they don’t solve it?”

“Then we have to say that too.”

The answer sounded less like hope than an obligation. Mara had begun to suspect that hope, when made useful, was often the willingness to remain answerable after the first good intention had failed.
