# Chapter 6h — The Wrong Name

Tavi Sen came to the clinic because she had begun calling objects by the wrong names. Not often, she said, and never in a way that made her unsafe. She had called a spoon a key, then laughed; she had called her partner’s sister by their mother’s name. The errors frightened her less than the thought that she might be asked to prove she was frightened correctly.

She had experienced several panic episodes during the year after a fire in the building where she lived. No one had died, but three apartments were destroyed, and she had watched smoke fill the stairwell while waiting for the firefighters to reach an older neighbor. Since then she had slept badly, sometimes waking to check the stove. She had been prescribed a medication by a walk-in physician and was unsure whether it affected her concentration. Her employer had moved her to a quieter office after she asked for fewer interruptions, but she worried they would see the request as evidence that she could no longer do her job.

“Is this the Quieting?” she asked Mara.

Mara felt the room contract around the word. “I can’t tell from what you’ve said. The name describes a possibility, not a diagnosis we can make from one symptom. We need to understand the sleep, the medication, your health, and what changed after the fire.”

Tavi looked disappointed. “I wanted you to say no.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

Mara did not pretend to. “I know why you would want a clear no. I can’t give you one honestly. I can help you find out what may be happening and what support you want while we do.”

Tavi agreed to meet with Dr. Chen and to speak with her prescribing physician about the medication. She did not agree to a formal cognitive test yet. She asked whether the service would record her as a risk. Mara explained what the record required and who could see it. Tavi asked the same question in three forms. Mara answered each without shortening the explanation.

At the end, Tavi said she did not want her partner contacted. She loved him, she said, but he had begun checking her speech after every conversation, waiting for another wrong word. She did not want to turn their home into an examination room.

Mara wrote down the boundary, then asked if Tavi wanted a copy of the note. Tavi said yes. The request made the appointment longer. Mara did not regard the extra time as a failure of efficiency.

After Tavi left, Imani asked whether the symptoms sounded serious.

“They’re serious because they frighten her and affect her life,” Mara said. “That doesn’t tell us what caused them.”

Imani looked at the chart. “So we keep several possibilities open.”

“We keep the person in front of us. The possibilities are there to help us ask better questions, not to replace her.”

Mara heard the sentence as she spoke it, and wondered whether she had finally begun to understand the lesson Ada had tried to teach her. Understanding did not mean possessing the explanation. It meant staying with a person while the explanation remained unfinished.
