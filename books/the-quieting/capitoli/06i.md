# Chapter 6i — The Apartment After the Fire

Tavi invited Mara and Imani to a home visit after the building manager scheduled repairs to the stairwell. She wanted the clinicians to understand why she had begun avoiding the landing where the smoke had gathered. She did not want them to see the apartment as evidence of a disorder. She wanted them to see what the apartment had become.

The new paint was a shade too white. The building had replaced the alarm panel and put a printed evacuation plan beside the lift. The plan listed the nearest exits, but not the neighbor who used a wheelchair and could not take the stairs. Tavi had added a handwritten note beneath it with the number of the building’s night porter. The management company had removed the note once, then restored it after Tavi complained.

“I’m tired of being the person who remembers what the sign leaves out,” she said.

She showed them the kitchen. The stove was electric, the smoke alarm newly installed. She had stopped checking the knobs six times a night, though she still checked them twice. She slept on the sofa sometimes because the bedroom window faced the stairwell. Her concentration worsened after nights with little sleep, and improved when she managed a full stretch. She had not yet asked her physician to review the medication; she had been afraid the appointment would require her to describe the fire again.

Mara asked what she wanted the home visit to accomplish. Tavi said she wanted the clinic to document the building’s conditions, because her employer had suggested the problem was entirely personal. She also wanted to know whether the service could help her request a temporary shift adjustment. She did not want a clinician to tell her partner that she was safe or unsafe.

They discussed each request. The clinic could write a letter describing the effect of poor sleep and sensory interruptions on Tavi’s work, without naming the Quieting or the fire. It could not inspect the building as a safety authority. It could refer her to a tenant advocacy group. It would not contact her partner without permission.

Imani asked if Tavi wanted to discuss the panic episodes. Tavi said not that day. The answer felt like a refusal and a direction. Imani accepted it.

Before they left, Tavi showed them the stairwell. She stood at the landing with one hand on the rail, looking toward the new emergency light. Her breathing changed. Mara asked whether she wanted to go outside. Tavi said no. She wanted to stay until she could look at the stairwell without needing to leave immediately.

They remained there, not as an exposure exercise prescribed from outside, but because Tavi had chosen the length of the moment. After a while she turned toward the apartment.

“Can you write that I stayed?” she asked.

“I can write what happened,” Mara said. “Would you like to see the note?”

“Yes.”

On the way downstairs, Imani said the visit had felt less like therapy than advocacy. Mara said it was both, and that the distinction mattered less than whether they had done what Tavi asked and been honest about what they could not do.

The building still had a stairwell. The fire had still happened. Tavi still slept badly. But the complaint about the missing wheelchair plan had been entered into the building’s official record, and the tenant group had asked to meet with her. Nothing was repaired completely. Something had become less private than the blame.
