# Chapter 6k — The Call from Caz

Caz called Mara from her brother’s town on a Sunday morning. She had been away from the clinic for five months and had begun working part-time at a community college, teaching first aid to students who planned to work in care. She said she was sleeping better. She did not say she wanted to return.

The clinic’s night rota had improved by one position. Two clinicians shared the after-hours line. The service still canceled some appointments. The change had made the work more possible, not easy. Mara told Caz this because she had once expected her to ask whether things were better. Caz said she was glad the request had led to something, and sorry that it had taken her leaving for the council to listen.

“Do you think you’ll come back?” Mara asked.

“I don’t know.”

Mara felt a wish to persuade her, then understood that the wish belonged to the clinic’s need, not Caz’s. She asked what Caz wanted the call to be about.

Caz said she wanted to tell Mara she had started seeing a therapist. At first she had resisted. She did not want to become another person in a care system, especially one she had spent years working inside. Her therapist had asked what she feared would happen if she were a patient. Caz had said she would lose authority. The therapist had asked if authority was the same as being well.

Caz laughed at the memory. “I hated that question. It was good.”

Mara told her she had begun to understand some of the same things in her own treatment. They did not compare notes. Caz had not called to become Mara’s patient or colleague again. They spoke about the old clinic and about the people who still worked there. When they hung up, neither had promised anything about the future.

The next week, the director asked Mara if she would encourage Caz to apply for a supervisory role when she was ready. Mara said she would not ask Caz to return for the clinic’s convenience. She would tell her the role existed if Caz wanted to hear about it.

The director said the clinic needed experienced staff. Mara said it also needed staff who could leave without being treated as deserters. The director looked tired. She agreed, though the budget still depended on people accepting more responsibility than the hours allowed.

Mara wrote Caz a short message: *There may be a supervisory position in autumn. No pressure to consider it; I can send details if you want them.* Caz replied two days later: *Send the details. I’m not promising anything.*

The message pleased Mara. It left Caz free to be interested without being recruited into someone else’s story of recovery. She forwarded the job description, then returned to the patient waiting outside her office. The work did not wait for its staff to become resolved. It required them to keep making choices while they were still changing.
