# Chapter 7 — The Orchard of Lost Things

Jonah took Mara to the island orchard where the city stored trees that could not survive the sea wind. Each one had been planted from a cutting saved after a storm, a fire, a road widening. The orchard was a museum of continuance.

He brought a book of Proust, swollen from damp. Tomas had carried it through three apartments and never finished it.

“Your brother liked Proust?” Mara asked.

“He liked the idea that a taste could open a door you didn’t know was there.”

They ate pears beneath the trees. The flesh was grainy and sweet. Mara tasted, without warning, the sour cherries Ada had stolen from the market, the juice darkening her sister’s fingers. Memory returned not as a story but as the exact pressure of a small hand slipping into hers.

Proust had written of involuntary memory, of time hiding inside sensation. Mara had once quoted him to patients as if remembrance were a gift. She understood now that it was also an ambush. A lost thing could return so completely that for one bright instant the loss itself seemed new.

“Does remembering help?” Jonah asked.

“Sometimes. Sometimes it only proves what isn’t here.”

He nodded. “Tomas used to say that a person could be missed in two directions: for who they were, and for who you were when they were alive.”

Mara held the pear in both hands. “I miss Ada in all the versions.”

The words came, and then the tears. She turned away out of habit. Jonah did not turn her back or offer a phrase. He remained beside her, quiet and breathing, while the orchard moved in the wind.

She had been taught to notice the signs of escalation: breath, pulse, orientation, the narrowing of thought. She noticed them now, but did not turn them into a prophecy. Her body was distressed. She was still herself. The two facts could occupy the same minute.

When she could speak, she said, “I’m afraid if I let myself feel this, I’ll never come back.”

Jonah brushed a leaf from his sleeve. “I don’t think coming back means returning unchanged.”

She leaned her forehead against his shoulder. For a while the island held them in the scent of wet bark and bruised fruit. Then a bell rang across the water, announcing a ferry. They stood and went to meet it.
