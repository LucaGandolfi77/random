# Chapter 7a — The House They Nearly Shared

Mara and Jonah had once looked at an apartment together in the south quarter. It had narrow windows, a kitchen too small for two people to pass, and a view of a brick wall that turned pink at sunset. Jonah had said the wall made the place feel private. Mara had said it made the place feel like a waiting room.

They had not moved in. The decision was never made, only postponed through a series of reasons that sounded sensible: her fellowship, his brother’s illness, the rent, the inconvenience of two commutes. Looking back, Mara could see the apartment as an unchosen future, though at the time it had been only a set of keys the landlord expected them to return.

They went there now because the new tenant had left the courtyard gate open. The windows had been replaced. From the alley, the brick wall was still visible, but the sunset could no longer reach it.

“Do you remember what we thought we’d do with the rooms?” Jonah asked.

“A desk in the bedroom. Your books in the hallway.”

“Your books in the hallway. My books would have occupied the bedroom.”

“I see you have revised the historical record.”

“I am an archivist. Revision is my profession.”

They laughed, but the laughter brought back the people they had been: not better, only less informed. Mara wondered whether love was partly the wish to inhabit a future before it had gathered evidence against you. She had loved Jonah with an eagerness that felt like relief. When Ada began to worsen, that relief became intolerable. It seemed indecent that one life could open while another was closing.

“You used to say you wanted a home where no one had to earn quiet,” Jonah said.

“I did?”

“You said it when the neighbors were arguing at two in the morning.”

She could not remember the sentence, but she remembered the night: the radiator knocking, Jonah asleep with one hand open on the sheet, the argument upstairs ending in a door slammed hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling. Perhaps memory kept not the words but the shape of the wish.

“I don’t know if I can live with someone again,” she said.

“Neither do I.”

“You make that sound hopeful.”

“I’m trying to make it sound true.”

He did not ask her to promise. He did not suggest that tenderness would undo the years. They stood in the alley as the light withdrew from the high windows. When Jonah kissed her, it was brief, cautious, and not a solution. Mara felt the familiar impulse to assign it a meaning—reconciliation, risk, mistake—and let the impulse pass without choosing for them.

They left the courtyard separately, then met again at the corner, both walking in the same direction.
