# Chapter 7b — The Letter Without a Signature

The letter from Ada’s file had been written in a hand Mara recognized before she understood why. It was not Ada’s; it was the clinician’s, small and slanted, the same hand that had filled the margins of the case note. On the back of the page, in pencil, were three words: *Call her sister.*

The instruction had never been carried out.

Mara took the sheet to Sera. For several minutes neither spoke. The pencil had faded, but the pressure of it remained in the paper. Mara thought of Proust’s madeleine, and immediately disliked the comparison. A taste that returns a childhood is not the same as a note that exposes a failure. The memory might be involuntary; the responsibility was not.

“I don’t know who wrote it,” Sera said. “It could have been a clinician, a supervisor, someone at intake.”

“Can we find out?”

“Yes. But we’ll need to ask people who may not want to remember.”

Mara felt the familiar desire to uncover every detail, as if knowledge could be a form of repair. If she could name the person, the hour, the missed call, then the event might acquire a shape she could carry. Yet the person who wrote those words might have been overwhelmed, ill, or certain someone else had acted. The institution’s failure had taken place through ordinary omissions, and no single culprit would restore the missing Sunday.

“Don’t put this in the public report as an anecdote,” Mara said.

Sera studied her. “Even if it makes the point?”

“Especially if it makes the point. Ada isn’t a useful illustration.”

They agreed to include the procedural fact: a referral had been deferred, and no follow-up call occurred. Ada’s name and the private note would remain restricted unless the family chose otherwise. Mara’s mother did not want Ada’s full case made public. She wanted the service to admit it had failed to follow through. Mara honored that distinction, though she understood Jonah’s hunger to see every hidden thing brought into the light.

That night she dreamed her sister was calling from the kitchen, asking whether the bread had burned. Mara woke before she could answer. The room was dark, the clock unlit, her mouth dry. She remembered the dream as one remembers a sentence spoken in another language: the meaning present, the words unavailable.

She wanted to believe the note meant someone had seen Ada. She wanted to believe the unmade call had been a mistake rather than indifference. Neither belief could be verified. What she knew was that Ada had asked for help; the service had not followed up; Mara had not called when she said she would. The facts were small enough to fit on a page and large enough to occupy a life.

In the morning, she called her mother at half past four, though it was only ten. She did not wait for the appointed hour. They spoke of the bread, the soup, and the hearing. When her mother said she was glad Mara had called, Mara let the words remain an affection rather than a debt.
