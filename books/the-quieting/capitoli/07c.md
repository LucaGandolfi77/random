# Chapter 7c — Supervision

Mara’s supervision with Imani took place on Friday afternoons, when the clinic began to empty and the light from the western windows made the corridors look briefly forgiving. Imani brought a list of questions. She had learned to ask them before the session ended, though she still sometimes asked the most important one last.

“Do you think a clinician can care too much?” she asked.

Mara waited. The question might have meant several things: can care become intrusive; can attachment compromise judgment; can a clinician be hurt by a patient’s suffering; can the work demand more than a person has to give. There were answers to all of them, and none would be sufficient.

“Care isn’t a quantity,” Mara said. “The problem is what we do with it.”

Imani told her she had stayed late the previous night to call Lio’s union representative, though the representative had not asked her to. She had wanted to make the accommodation happen. When Lio later said he did not want her speaking for him, she had felt embarrassed and, unexpectedly, resentful.

“I thought I was helping,” she said.

“You may have been. And you may have crossed a line.”

“I should have known better.”

“Knowing better doesn’t mean you never do the thing. It means you can notice it, take responsibility, and change what you do next.”

Mara heard her own voice and recognized the advice she had failed to give herself. She had made Ada’s life into a private examination in which any mistake proved she had not loved her sister correctly. The same merciless arithmetic could enter clinical work wearing the uniform of professional responsibility.

Imani asked whether Mara regretted becoming a psychologist.

“No,” Mara said, and then, because a simple answer felt too complete, “Sometimes I regret believing that understanding a problem gave me the right to stand outside it.”

The sentence seemed to surprise Imani. She asked what Mara meant. Mara told her, in part, about Ada: the appointments she had not attended, the Sunday she had called on Monday, the way she had used her training to explain a sister she was afraid of losing. She did not tell the whole story. Some of it belonged to her family; some of it was still changing shape inside her.

Imani listened without taking notes. Mara noticed and felt briefly exposed, then grateful. She had spent years asking patients to accept that she could witness their pain without reducing it to a record. It was stranger to be the one whose words would not be carried away as a note.

At the end of the hour Imani said, “I don’t want to become the kind of person who can only help if the outcome is good.”

“Then you’ll have to learn to stay when the outcome is unknown.”

Outside, a bus stopped at the curb and released a small crowd. The people dispersed in different directions, each carrying a private destination the street could not show. Mara watched until they were gone, then asked Imani what she wanted to do next with Lio’s case. They returned to the practical matter, which was another way of caring: not escaping into reflection, but bringing reflection back to the next appointment.
