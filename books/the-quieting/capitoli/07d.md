# Chapter 7d — The Hill Above the Cemetery

Mara went to her father’s grave after the review board announced its decision. She had not visited in months. The cemetery stood on a hill above the eastern railway, where the trains passed with a slow metallic shudder that made the ground seem to remember movement.

Her father had died when Mara was seventeen and Ada was thirteen. He had been ill for several months, but the family had preserved an idea of ordinary life until the day the hospital called. Mara remembered the drive there more clearly than the room: her mother’s hands on the steering wheel, Ada’s shoes wet from the rain, the radio playing an advertisement for a mattress. The banal details had stayed where the important ones blurred.

At the grave, Mara tried to reconstruct her father’s voice. She could remember the cadence when he read the newspaper aloud, the way he pronounced place names, the mild irritation he used when the radio failed. The actual timbre escaped her. She wondered when she had last heard it clearly. Memory did not announce the final time a thing would happen. It simply continued until, much later, someone discovered the door had closed.

Ada had been furious after the funeral. She accused Mara of behaving as if she had become their father’s substitute, though Mara had never wanted the role. Their mother had praised Mara for being helpful, and Mara had accepted the praise as a duty. She had organized the bills, remembered the hospital instructions, and made tea for visitors. Ada had refused to help with any of it until one night, months later, she sat beside Mara on the kitchen floor and said she was scared their mother would die too.

Mara had answered that their mother was fine. It was a lie intended to keep Ada from being afraid, and a failure to understand that fear did not become more dangerous merely because it was spoken.

At the grave, Mara felt an impulse to apologize to her father for a life he had not been present to witness. She resisted the fantasy that the dead were waiting to receive the right words. Instead she told him, quietly, that Ada had died, that their mother was still living in the house, that Mara had become a psychologist, that she had loved a man and hurt him and was trying again.

She did not know whether speech to the dead was prayer, habit, or a form of thinking with an audience. It did not matter. She was not asking for a reply.

On the way down the hill she passed a family carrying flowers. The youngest child held a paper cup of hot chocolate and complained that it had cooled. The parent bent to listen. Mara felt tenderness toward the child and envy of the parent, who still had the illusion that a small practical act might answer the world.

By the time she reached the station, the envy had softened into memory. She called her mother and asked if she wanted company on Sunday. Her mother said yes. Mara made no promise to fix the past. She promised to arrive.
