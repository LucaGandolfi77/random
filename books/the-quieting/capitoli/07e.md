# Chapter 7e — The Sentence in the Book

Jonah found a line in Tomas’s copy of *The Brothers Karamazov* that he had never noticed before. The page was bent at one corner. A pencil mark ran beside a paragraph, then stopped halfway down, as if Tomas had been called away or had simply decided not to finish reading.

Jonah took the book to Mara’s apartment. She was at the table with the draft report, crossing out an adjective that made a patient sound like a problem. He read the marked passage aloud. It concerned responsibility, but the translation was old and the punctuation theatrical. Neither of them was sure whether Tomas had marked the words or merely used the page as a place to rest his hand.

“I used to think the line meant we owe everyone everything,” Jonah said. “Now I think that’s an impossible standard that can make you feel guilty for being human.”

Mara put down her pen. “Maybe it means we can’t pretend suffering is someone else’s private failure.”

“Does that include my brother?”

“Yes.”

“And your sister?”

“Yes.”

“Does it make us responsible for them?”

“Not in the sense that we could control what happened.”

Jonah closed the book. He had wanted the phrase to settle the argument between responsibility and blame. It did not. It made the argument larger, drew in the city, the clinic, the family, the labor contract, the people who had looked away because they were afraid of what they would be asked to do if they looked directly.

They sat together in the kitchen while the evening light moved from one wall to another. Mara told him about the patient who had asked for a rule that would make crying safe. Jonah said he wished someone could give such a rule to everyone. She asked whether he meant protection or permission. He said he did not know.

That was the kind of answer they had learned to tolerate from one another: not evasive, not complete, an opening left unfilled because the person speaking had not yet reached the end of their thought.

Mara read a few pages from Proust while Jonah made tea. She told him how a taste could summon a vanished room, but how the memory did not restore the room or the people inside it. It only let the present touch what had gone. Jonah said he had often mistaken the intensity of remembrance for a kind of return.

“Maybe remembering is not return,” Mara said. “Maybe it’s a meeting between what’s gone and what remains.”

Jonah set the cups down. The phrase sounded like the kind of sentence one might write in a book and distrust afterward. They did not write it down. They drank the tea, which was too strong, and argued about whether the chapter should be in the report. The argument lasted longer than the tea. The book lay open between them, its marked sentence unresolved.
