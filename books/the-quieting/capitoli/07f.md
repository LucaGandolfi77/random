# Chapter 7f — The Two Reports

The internal report and the public report disagreed about the meaning of the revised measure. The internal version called the return-to-work target “an operational indicator vulnerable to misuse.” The public summary called it “one measure among several.” The second phrase was easier to defend and less honest about how much the first measure had shaped the system.

Mara found the discrepancy while reviewing the proofs. She brought it to Sera, who no longer had authority over the report but had been asked to advise on its clinical language. Sera said the wording had been softened during communications review. The editor argued that the public would misunderstand “vulnerable to misuse” as an accusation of deliberate harm.

“Then explain the difference,” Mara said.

The editor replied that the report was already too long. There were limits to what a reader could be asked to hold. Mara thought of the books that had taught her to stay with a thought past the point at which it became comfortable, and of the people who would never read a report at all. There was no format that could hold everyone’s attention without also losing something.

They argued over one sentence for nearly an hour. Sera suggested: *The indicator proved useful for tracking access to ordinary activities, but in practice it was sometimes treated as proof of recovery, even when a patient’s return was shaped by economic pressure.* The sentence was longer than the editor wanted and less accusatory than Mara had wanted. It was also accurate.

The editor asked whether the report needed to say that some staff had objected to the target. Sera said yes, because the record showed that objections had been raised. Mara asked whether the response to those objections should be described. It should. The final wording named the concern, the decision to retain the measure, and the later failure to review its effect.

When the proofs returned, the passage remained. Mara felt a satisfaction that was almost embarrassing. One paragraph had survived the pressure to become simpler. She knew it would not change the people who had already decided what the story meant. It might help someone else ask a better question.

Sera read the sentence and said, “I wish I’d insisted on that when it mattered.”

Mara said, “So do I.”

There was no comfort in the shared regret. It did prevent either woman from pretending the omission had happened beyond their reach. They had choices, not unlimited ones; they had made them, and the report now had to say what those choices were.

That evening Jonah asked Mara whether she was pleased with the final text. She said she was pleased with one paragraph and worried about the rest. He asked if that counted as pleased. She said it counted as what she felt. The distinction seemed small, but she had spent enough of her life looking for a clean feeling to know how often the search became another way of not living the mixed one.
