# Chapter 7g — The Empty Chair at the Table

The patient consultation group met in a room with eight chairs. There were usually seven people: two clinicians, three current or former patients, one family representative, and a facilitator. The eighth chair remained empty because the group did not want to turn absence into an invitation that no one had chosen.

At the meeting, they discussed whether the clinic should contact a patient after a missed appointment. One former patient said the calls had helped her return. Another said repeated calls had made her feel watched. The family representative said she had wished the service would call her son when he disappeared from care. The clinician said there were times when a call might be lifesaving. The facilitator asked what the default should be.

They did not reach one. They wrote three options into the policy: the patient could choose a follow-up preference in advance; the clinician could explain when a welfare check might be considered; and the patient could change the preference later. If there were immediate grounds for concern, the team would consult and record its reasoning. The policy would not make uncertainty vanish. It would make the person’s prior choice harder to overlook.

During a break, a former patient named Amara told Mara that she had refused follow-up for years because she did not want to become a case everyone discussed. She had not wanted to be alone. She had wanted the service to ask her which people she trusted, rather than deciding that family was automatically safe.

Mara felt the sentence settle into a place where it could alter other sentences. She thought of Ada, her mother, and the times Mara had assumed she knew whom her sister wanted contacted. The question had not occurred to her because she had believed closeness conferred knowledge. It did not.

At the end of the meeting, Amara asked whether Mara had ever been a patient.

“Yes,” Mara said, though she had not intended to tell the group. “I see my own psychiatrist.”

“Does it help?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes it’s difficult. It’s still my care.”

Amara nodded. She did not ask for details. The exchange lasted less than a minute, but Mara noticed the old hierarchy in the room had shifted. She was not only the clinician who knew how to explain the policy. She was a person who used the service and had limits on what she would share.

The empty chair remained empty. It did not stand for the people who had not come; it stood for the fact that the group could not decide on their behalf. Mara had once thought a complete table meant everyone was represented. Now she understood that an empty place could be a reminder not to confuse presence with permission.
