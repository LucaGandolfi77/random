# Chapter 7i — What the Tests Could Say

Dr. Chen did not begin Tavi’s assessment with a test. She began by asking when the wrong words happened, what else was happening at the time, and whether Tavi noticed a pattern. Tavi described the evenings after short sleep, the mornings after the medication, the conversations in which she was worried about being observed. The word substitutions were not constant. They had become more frequent when she was tired and less frequent when she was rested.

The cognitive screening showed some difficulty with attention. It did not establish a cause. Chen recommended a medical review, a medication consultation with the prescribing doctor, and a longer follow-up after Tavi’s sleep stabilized. She explained that a screening tool could identify areas to explore but could not diagnose a future decline or prove that the Quieting was underway.

Tavi listened, then asked if the result would appear in her employment record.

“No,” Chen said. “The clinic keeps it confidential. We would share information with your employer only with your consent, except in a narrow situation of immediate serious danger, which this is not.”

Tavi asked if that could be written down. Chen wrote it down. Tavi read the note and corrected one phrase that implied she had requested a medical referral. She had agreed to it, she said, but wanted the record to show that the suggestion had come from the clinician. Chen changed the sentence.

On the way out, Tavi told Mara she felt less frightened and more frightened. The test had not shown a devastating result, but it had not given her the clear reassurance she wanted. Now there was a medical appointment to attend, an employer conversation to prepare for, and a private fear that could not be assigned a number.

Mara told her that both feelings made sense. She stopped herself from adding that the test was good news. It was not exactly good news; it was information with limits.

In supervision, Imani asked whether clinicians should ever tell a patient that a test result was reassuring.

“If it is,” Mara said. “But be specific about what it reassures us about. A normal result today doesn’t promise tomorrow. An abnormal result doesn’t tell us the whole person.”

Imani said she worried that too many qualifications made care sound evasive.

“Sometimes they do. Sometimes they’re how we avoid lying.”

Mara remembered being young and believing that compassion meant reducing fear. Now she thought it might mean refusing to add a false certainty to the fear already there, while still helping the person take the next step.

Tavi returned four weeks later. Her physician had adjusted the medication, and her sleep had improved somewhat. The wrong-word episodes had become less frequent. They had not disappeared. Chen recommended monitoring and further assessment if they worsened or new symptoms appeared. Tavi asked for a copy of the results. She wanted to keep them herself, not because she intended to use them as proof, but because she wanted access to her own information.

The request was granted. Tavi put the papers in a folder and carried them home.
