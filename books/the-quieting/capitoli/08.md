# Chapter 8 — The Unfinished Map

Nell returned to the map workshop on a Thursday. She chose a table near the exit and asked for the district surveyor’s blank sheets.

“I want to map the parts people avoid,” she told Mara. “The stairs with no rail. The benches that face the wall. The clinic entrance that looks like an office where you’ll be in trouble.”

“Would you like me to help?”

“I’d like you to not make it into a recovery project.”

“Understood.”

Nell sketched the harbor quarter from memory, then added places where someone could sit without being asked to explain. The map was not accurate yet. It was useful in the way a question can be useful before it has an answer.

Her aunt had agreed to pause the residential referral. The psychiatrist met with Nell and her aunt together, then separately. She talked through sleep, panic, and the practical signs that meant they should ask for urgent help. She did not promise that a plan could prevent everything. She offered treatment and left room for refusal.

Nell said she might try a short course of therapy. She would not take medication without another conversation. The psychiatrist said that was reasonable, and they would revisit it. Nell seemed surprised that the answer could remain open.

Afterward, Mara and Nell walked to the harbor wall. A group of teenagers were feeding gulls pieces of bread, though a sign asked them not to. The birds behaved like small, unlicensed gods.

“Do you think my mum would want me to finish the map?” Nell asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Good.” Nell stared at the water. “Everyone says yes because it sounds kind.”

Mara thought of Ada’s file and all the kindnesses that had been mistaken for answers. “Sometimes the kindest thing is not to pretend we know.”

Nell looked at her. “Are you okay?”

It was a question adults rarely allowed children to ask. Mara nearly gave the practiced answer. Instead she said, “Not entirely. But I’m safe, and I’m getting help.”

“Is that enough?”

“For today.”

Nell nodded as if she respected the scale of it. She folded the map and put it in her coat pocket. A wind came off the water and pushed her hair across her cheek.

“I’ll draw the clinic door wider,” she said.

“It already opens.”

“Not wide enough.”

Mara watched her walk toward the workshop, carrying a paper map whose roads did not yet meet. For the first time, the unfinished lines did not look like failure.
