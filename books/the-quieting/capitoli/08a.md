# Chapter 8a — The Appointment

Dr. Chen’s office overlooked a schoolyard. At the hour of Mara’s appointment, the children were outside, their voices rising and falling in a way that could be mistaken for disorder if one had forgotten that play was not required to be efficient.

Mara had been seeing Chen for two years. At first she had disliked the intimacy of being asked questions without being able to redirect them. She had expected, irrationally, that professional knowledge would exempt her from needing care. Chen had never mocked that belief. She had simply continued to ask how Mara was sleeping.

“Do you think I’m at risk of the Quieting?” Mara asked.

Chen rested her pen on the table. “I think you’re frightened, and I think you’ve turned uncertainty into a diagnosis because a diagnosis might give the fear a boundary.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have. There is no validated formula that predicts this in an individual. Your panic last night is not proof that your mind is deteriorating. We can talk about the symptoms you have, what helps, what needs monitoring. We shouldn’t pretend to know more.”

Mara looked out at the schoolyard. A boy was walking the perimeter of a painted circle, stepping in and out as if testing the edge of a country.

“I’m afraid I’ll become useless,” she said.

Chen did not answer immediately. It was one of the reasons Mara trusted her: she did not confuse a quick response with a good one.

“Useless to whom?”

“To everyone.”

“Is that the same as being loved?”

Mara almost said yes. It would have been too easy, and too revealing. She spoke instead about Ada, the report, and her testimony. Chen helped her separate responsibility from omnipotence: the fact that Mara had failed her sister in particular ways did not mean she had controlled the conditions of Ada’s life; the fact that institutions had failed Ada did not make Mara’s grief a debt she could repay by destroying herself in service.

They discussed a plan. More sleep. No testimony rehearsal after midnight. A check-in with Jonah. A follow-up appointment. Chen asked about medication; Mara said she was not ready to consider it. Chen agreed they could revisit the question. Neither treated refusal as proof of illness or treatment as defeat.

When the session ended, the schoolyard had emptied. The boy was gone, and the painted circle remained, incomplete where rain had washed away the white line.

“You don’t have to become a lesson,” Chen said at the door.

Mara carried the sentence with her. It seemed to belong as much to Ada as to herself.
