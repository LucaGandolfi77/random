# Chapter 8b — The Mapmakers’ Table

The map project began to outgrow Nell’s paper. The library offered a wall; the neighborhood association offered money for printing; the harbor authority offered a digital survey platform and wanted to place its logo at the top. Nell said no to the logo. The authority withdrew its offer. The library said it could still print the maps, but only in a small run.

At the workshop, the argument lasted longer than the drawing. Mr. Daven believed the city should be grateful for the map and pay for it. A student named Yara wanted to make an online version. Nell did not want the locations of quiet spaces to become a list that could be used to identify people who needed them. The shopkeeper wanted to put the map in her window; she was proud to have been included and did not see how visibility could carry risk.

Mara listened, saying less than she wanted. Her first impulse was to mediate, to make a proposal that satisfied every party by making each desire smaller. Nell recognized the impulse.

“You keep trying to make the argument stop,” the girl said afterward.

“I thought I was helping.”

“You were helping everyone avoid being mad at one another.”

The observation stung because it was true. Mara had been trained to distinguish conflict from danger, but she had spent much of her life treating the first as a warning of the second. Perhaps because Ada’s crises had taught her that a disagreement could arrive before a collapse, she had begun to hear every raised voice as the beginning of an emergency.

Nell proposed that the map have two versions: a public map showing accessible places, and a private workshop layer where participants could mark spaces they did not want listed. The map would name no one’s reason for using a place. A small committee of residents would decide what to publish. The shopkeeper agreed that the map need not reveal which customers came to sit. The authority declined again to fund it, then offered to provide printing without a logo after the youth council intervened.

It was imperfect and slow. It required the neighbors to trust one another with a responsibility no single expert could claim. Nell did not smile when the first proof arrived. She inspected the legend, corrected a mislabeled lane, and asked the printer to make the text larger for older readers.

Jonah stood beside Mara at the library wall. “She’s making something that can outlast the crisis,” he said.

“Don’t turn her into a symbol.”

He looked at Mara and nodded. “You’re right.”

She had not meant to scold him. She had meant to protect Nell. But even protection could become a way of deciding what someone else represented. The printed map showed no heroic figure, no story of recovery, only routes, benches, toilets, public rooms, and the places where a person might sit without explaining why.

Nell pinned it to the wall herself. When the pin went in, she pressed it twice to make sure it held.
