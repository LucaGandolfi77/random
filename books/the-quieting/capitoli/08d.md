# Chapter 8d — A Vote of One

Councilman Rusk voted against the first version of the revised protocol. The public statement said he had concerns about accountability and staffing. It did not mention his daughter. It did not mention the fear that had once sent him to a hospital corridor at three in the morning, where he waited while Leona refused to speak to the clinician and asked only for her mother.

The vote was close. The proposal failed by one.

Rusk found Mara in the corridor afterward. He said he had supported the principles but could not support a system that required clinics to accept patients without a clear standard for urgent intervention. Mara told him the proposal did include an emergency threshold, but it did not allow a risk score by itself to justify removing a person’s choices.

“That distinction sounds fine in a document,” he said. “In practice, someone has to decide.”

“Yes.”

“And that someone may be wrong.”

“Yes.”

He seemed frustrated by her agreement. He had expected either reassurance or opposition. Mara said no policy could remove judgment; the question was whether the person affected could understand, challenge, and review the decision. Rusk said review came after the harm. Mara said that was true, and prevention still mattered, but prevention could not mean treating every frightened person as if the worst outcome had already occurred.

He told her Leona had called the previous night. She had said she would not return home for the holiday. He had asked whether she was still angry. She had replied that anger was not the reason she lived elsewhere. She liked her life there.

“I wanted her to say she was all right,” he said. “I wanted the answer to end my part in it.”

Mara recognized the wish. She had wanted Ada to say she was safe, and sometimes had accepted the words as proof rather than beginning another conversation.

“Did you ask what she wanted you to understand?”

“No. I asked if she was coming home.”

Rusk looked toward the chamber, where aides were gathering papers. “I thought the policy would make it possible to save her.”

“Did she need saving?”

“Sometimes, yes. And sometimes she needed to be left alone.”

The committee’s amended draft was scheduled for a vote the following week. Rusk said he would abstain unless the emergency safeguards were made clearer. Mara told him that was his decision. She did not ask him to become an ally because she understood his fear, nor did she need to hate him because he had voted against her.

That evening, Leona sent him a message: *I’ll visit in March. Please don’t arrange anything for me.* He showed it to no one. He read it several times, then replied only: *I won’t.*

The committee draft passed by two, but still required the full council’s vote after the public hearing. The amendment did not make the city safe from error. It made the error more visible, and therefore more difficult to call inevitable.
