# Chapter 8e — Reva’s Signature

Nell’s apprenticeship contract required an adult guardian to sign the renewal. Reva placed the form on the kitchen table and read every line before passing it over. Nell watched her, already irritated.

“You don’t have to inspect it like it’s a treaty,” she said.

“I’m responsible for you.”

“I’m seventeen, not seven.”

“Both things can be true.”

The argument had been waiting in the room for weeks. Nell wanted to travel to the northern survey station with her classmates for three days. Reva thought the trip was too soon after the death, too far from home, too difficult to monitor. Nell thought monitoring was the problem. Mara had been asked to attend because both of them trusted her, and because they hoped she could declare one position reasonable enough to end the other.

Mara refused to arbitrate. She asked Nell what she wanted from the trip. Nell said she wanted to see how the surveyors measured a coastline that kept changing. She wanted to go with her friends and not have the entire trip discussed as a test of whether she was getting better.

Reva said she feared Nell would panic away from home and be unable to find help. Nell replied that she had her phone, the group leader’s number, and a psychiatrist she could call. Reva said the phone might fail. Nell said home might fail too.

The room went quiet. Reva’s face tightened. Mara understood that Nell had named a truth her aunt could not make harmless. A person could be in danger at home, in a clinic, or on a field trip; safety was not a location one could guarantee by keeping someone close.

They made a plan with the survey leader: Nell would have the option to leave the trip early, would carry her own emergency contacts, and would not be required to share her medical history with the group. Reva would speak directly to the leader about travel arrangements but would not call the clinic for updates unless Nell asked. The plan did not make Reva unafraid. It let fear exist without giving it sole authority.

Reva signed the renewal form. Her hand shook slightly. Nell took it and checked that the trip had not been added as a condition of her participation.

“I’m sorry I make you worry,” Nell said.

Reva stared at her. “You don’t have to apologize for having a life.”

The sentence seemed to surprise them both. Reva had been trying to protect Nell from further pain and had come close to making the girl feel that movement itself was a threat. She did not withdraw her worry. She let it sit beside the signature.

Nell left for the survey station two weeks later. The night before, she and Reva argued about whether she had packed enough socks. In the morning they hugged at the bus stop, briefly and without saying that the trip would prove anything. When the bus pulled away, Reva stayed until it turned the corner. Then she went home and slept for three hours.
