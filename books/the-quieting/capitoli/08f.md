# Chapter 8f — The Survey Station

The northern survey station stood where the city’s seawall ended and the coast became a sequence of low dunes, marsh, and stubborn grass. Nell went with six apprentices and two instructors. Reva packed food for three days; Nell unpacked half of it and repacked only what she had asked for. They argued over the raincoat. They negotiated the route to the bus. The morning of departure was made of small decisions, none of which revealed whether the trip would be easy.

The surveyors taught the apprentices to mark the shore at high tide and return at low. A coastline was not a line, the instructor said, but a record of agreements between water and land that never remained in force for long. Nell thought of maps that claimed permanence, and of the dotted line she had drawn across the bridge to her mother’s last walk. She had spent weeks worrying that a mark would fix the event in place. The water made a different argument: a line could be true for an hour and still be worth drawing.

On the second day, after a night of poor sleep, Nell felt the beginnings of panic. Her breath shortened; her thoughts gathered around the possibility that she would lose the route back. She stepped away from the group and sat on a low bank, where the grass bent beneath the wind. The instructor asked whether she wanted company. Nell said no, then changed her mind. The instructor sat at a distance and began talking about the tide gauge, not about Nell.

Nell named what she could see: the orange flag, the gray water, a black bird working at something in the sand. She did not feel the panic disappear. She felt her attention widen enough to include the shore around it. After several minutes she stood and rejoined the group. No one praised her for enduring it. They returned to the measurements.

That evening she called Reva. Her aunt asked if everything was all right. Nell said she had had a difficult hour and was okay now. Reva was silent, and Nell could hear the fear gathering at the other end of the line.

“I don’t need you to come get me,” Nell said. “I wanted to tell you because I want you to know the whole trip, not just the part where I managed.”

“Thank you for telling me,” Reva said. She did not ask for proof or promise not to worry. They spoke about the weather, the food, and the map of the tide pools.

When Nell returned to Bellwether, she brought back a sheet of field notes. She added no mark for panic, no mark for success. At the edge of the page she wrote the time of the tide and the wind direction. The coast had changed during the afternoon. The measurement remained true because she had made it carefully, not because it would stay true forever.
