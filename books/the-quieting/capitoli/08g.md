# Chapter 8g — The Map Committee

The city offered to adopt Nell’s map as an official public resource. The offer came with a grant, a graphic designer, and a request that the map use the city’s standard categories for “wellness access.” Nell read the proposal in the workshop and said the categories sounded like places where the city expected people to become better.

A library representative said official recognition would make the map easier to distribute. Mr. Daven said a public map that no one could find was a private hobby. The youth worker wanted the grant but feared the city would turn the map into a referral tool. Nell listened until the arguments began to repeat, then asked the city officer what would happen if residents wanted to remove a location after publication.

The officer said the digital map could be updated quarterly.

“What about tomorrow?” Nell asked.

The officer admitted there was no procedure for that. The map’s information could become inaccurate between reviews, and the city had not considered who would be responsible for checking it. The grant assumed that public knowledge, once printed, remained a public good.

The committee spent two meetings rewriting the proposal. The city would pay for printing and accessible formats, but residents would retain editorial control. Locations could be removed at any time by request. The map would include its review date and a phone number for corrections. It would not be linked to patient records, clinic referrals, or individual use statistics.

The last condition surprised the officer. “We need to know whether the map works,” he said.

“You can ask people,” Nell replied. “You don’t need to track who goes where.”

The final agreement was less efficient than the city wanted and more expensive than the original. The committee approved it anyway. Nell signed as one of six contributors, not as the project’s face. She asked the printer to list the community group rather than her name first.

At the launch, a reporter asked whether the map had helped her cope with her mother’s death. Nell said the question made the map sound like medicine. The reporter asked what she would call it instead.

“A map,” Nell said. “It helps you find places. It doesn’t tell you what to feel when you get there.”

The answer appeared in the story, though the headline still called her “the grieving girl who redrew the city.” Nell clipped the article and sent it to the editor with a note asking them to correct the phrase. They did, reluctantly.

The map was distributed in libraries, buses, and community rooms. Several locations changed within the first month. A bench disappeared during construction; a café stopped serving the public; a new quiet room opened at the harbor. The version number changed. Nell kept the first print in a drawer, not as a monument to the beginning but as evidence that the map had needed to change.

When Mara visited the workshop, Nell asked whether the official version made the map feel less hers.

“It’s yours,” Mara said, “and it belongs to the people who use it. Those things can be true together.”

Nell said she was still deciding whether she believed that. She folded the new map and put it in her bag.
