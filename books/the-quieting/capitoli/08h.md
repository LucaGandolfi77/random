# Chapter 8h — The Cost of Being Found

The newspaper’s corrected headline did not undo the first one. The story about Nell circulated under several versions, some of them naming her school. The library director called Mara to ask whether the map project should be paused. Nell said no, then asked that her own name be removed from the next public announcement. She wanted the map to continue without requiring her to stand beside it every time someone wanted a story about grief.

Reva worried that refusing the publicity would make the city less likely to fund the map. Nell said that if the money required her to become a symbol, the money cost too much. She did not say this easily. The project mattered to her, and she was afraid that stepping back would allow the city to take it over. She asked the contributors to act as a committee and share responsibility for the next edition.

The reporter who had used the headline requested an interview. Nell declined. He sent a note apologizing for the wording and said he would correct the online piece. The correction arrived two days later, below the original story. It described Nell as a mapmaker and included a link to the community project. It did not describe the harm caused by making her grief publicly legible.

Mara read the correction and felt a flicker of anger at its limits. She also knew that asking the paper to publish every consequence of its story could become another demand for Nell to speak. She asked Nell whether she wanted to pursue a complaint. Nell said she wanted the school references removed and the project’s contact information placed in the correction. That was all.

The map committee received three new messages. One was from an older man who needed a route with fewer stairs. One was from a woman who wanted a quiet place open after six. The third was a threat from someone who said the city should not spend money on “people who refuse to cope.” Nell read it, then passed it to Reva without comment.

“Do you want to answer?” Reva asked.

“No.”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

Reva deleted the message from the project inbox, preserving a copy for the organizers in case the threat escalated. She did not tell Nell that she had done it for her safety. She asked permission first, and Nell gave it.

The map went forward under a rotating group of contributors. Nell remained part of the work, but no longer its public face. The city’s communications officer objected that this would make the initiative harder to promote. The group said promotion was not the same as access.

At the next workshop, Nell sat at the end of the table, correcting the route to the harbor clinic. She was quiet for most of the meeting. When someone asked for her opinion, she gave it. When no one asked, she continued drawing. Mara watched from the doorway and understood that participation did not require constant visibility. A person could matter to a project without becoming its explanation.
