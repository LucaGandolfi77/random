# Chapter 8i — The Paper That Stayed Private

When Ilyana’s maps were brought to the community archive, Nell selected one to copy and kept the rest at home. The archivist explained that the collection could be restricted for a fixed period, or held privately until the family chose to donate it. Nell asked what would happen if her aunt later changed her mind. The archivist said the family could revisit the decision.

Nell felt the same unease she had felt with the public map: the belief that a record became more valuable when more people could see it. She did not want the city to lose Ilyana’s work. She did not want every private walk converted into a piece of public memory. The original route to the footbridge was not a secret, exactly. It was a small piece of her mother’s life, written before anyone knew it would be read after her death.

Reva said they could donate the copy and keep the original. Her mother’s sister—Nell’s aunt by another branch of the family—said the archive would preserve the paper better than a kitchen drawer. Both were right. Nell asked whether the map itself could be described in the catalogue without reproducing its route.

The archivist agreed. They wrote: *Hand-drawn walking map by Ilyana Ilyan; private family copy, not for reproduction.* Nell added a note that the map could be reviewed after five years, but only with the family’s consent. The phrase “not for reproduction” felt stronger than the boundary she had first wanted and less than a final refusal.

At home, she unfolded the original. The pencil line was faint in places. She could see where Ilyana had rested her hand and smudged the graphite near the market. The paper smelled of the drawer: cedar, dust, a trace of soap. Nell thought of Proust’s attention to the involuntary return of the past, and understood that the memory did not need a public audience to become real. The map could bring her mother back to her without becoming evidence for anyone else.

She folded it along its old creases and placed it in a box with the letter Reva had written after the funeral. The box was not an archive in the official sense. It had no access rules, no conservation plan, no index. It had a place under Nell’s bed and a label she had written herself: *Things that are ours for now.*

The word *ours* troubled her. Her mother’s life had belonged to Ilyana first. Nell changed the label to *Things we have been trusted with.* It was still not exact. She left it anyway.

When she closed the box, no conclusion arrived. There was only the quiet sound of paper settling against paper, and the knowledge that remembering could be an act of care even when it was not shared.
