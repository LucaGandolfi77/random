# Chapter 8k — The Map on the Bus

The first time Nell saw the new map on a bus, it was folded into a clear plastic pocket beside the route schedule. Someone had circled the library in blue pen and written *ramp closed until Friday*. The note was not an official update. It was a passenger’s hand making the map more useful than the version the city had printed.

Nell took a photograph and sent it to the committee. The city officer worried that handwritten corrections could be inaccurate. Nell agreed. She also said the correction was true that morning, which was what a passenger needed. They agreed to add a temporary notice beside the printed map: *Check current access before traveling; report closures by phone or in person.* The warning was imperfect, but it admitted that a printed map could age faster than its maintenance schedule.

On the bus, an older man asked Nell whether the route to the harbor clinic was accessible. She showed him the longer path and explained that the lift at the station had been unreliable. He said he had known about the lift and wanted to know whether there was a place to sit halfway. The map listed a bench, but it had been removed for construction.

“I don’t know,” Nell said. “I’ll ask the committee to check.”

The man nodded and thanked her. He did not seem disappointed that she lacked the answer. He seemed relieved she had not invented one.

Nell got off at the library and walked the route herself. The missing bench had left a pale square in the pavement. She recorded it as removed and added the date. The committee debated whether this level of detail made the map too difficult to maintain. Mr. Daven said the alternative was to maintain a fiction of certainty. They kept the date field.

The map had become more cumbersome with each revision. It was also less likely to mislead. Nell understood that no map could account for every body, every broken lift, every day when a person needed to stop. Its usefulness came not from being complete but from making its incompleteness visible enough that someone could ask a question.

That evening she took the route with Reva. They stopped at the bakery and bought a pear pastry. Reva asked if Nell wanted the short way home or the longer one. Nell chose the longer route because she wanted to walk by the water. They did not discuss whether the choice meant she was doing well. They walked, ate the pastry, and let the map remain folded in Nell’s bag.
