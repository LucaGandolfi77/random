# Chapter 9 — The Brothers Karamazov

Jonah found Mara in the records room reading Tomas’s intake notes. He did not ask whether she had permission. The files had been released for review, though the public still could not see them.

“Do you think your brother knew what was happening?” she asked.

“I think he knew something was wrong and could not make the right people understand it.” Jonah sat opposite her. “He had panic. He had depression. He also had a landlord who raised the rent, a job that punished sick days, and a family that thought asking for help meant making trouble. The Quieting came later. People like to point at one cause. It makes the world feel solvable.”

Mara looked at the record. A note described Tomas as “uncooperative.” In the margin, in another hand, someone had written: *Asked for his brother. No callback arranged.*

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“Don’t be sorry on behalf of the whole city.”

“I’m sorry he asked for me and I didn’t know.”

Jonah’s face changed. He had not known that. The silence between them widened, but did not split.

“I didn’t tell you,” he said. “I was angry that you could keep saving strangers while I was losing him. It wasn’t fair. You were doing what you could.”

“Not enough.”

“Maybe. But no one can be enough for a whole system.”

On the table lay a copy of *The Brothers Karamazov*. Jonah had brought it in, a battered edition Tomas had underlined. One sentence was marked in blue: “We are all responsible for all.” The translation differed from the phrase Mara remembered; she knew the line in many forms.

“It can become a cruel sentence,” she said. “If everyone is responsible for everything, then no one is allowed to be only human.”

“Or it can mean no one gets to say the suffering belongs to somebody else.”

She met his eyes. “That’s the version I’m willing to keep.”

Jonah reached across the table. She took his hand. Their old love was not restored; restoration implied there had been an original state waiting intact. Instead, something living began among the evidence, altered by what it had learned.

“I don’t want you to be my clinician,” he said.

“I don’t want to be.”

“Good. I want you to be the person who tells me when I’m impossible.”

“I can do that.”

“And I’ll tell you when you’re hiding in competence.”

“That sounds less pleasant.”

“It is meant to be useful.”

She laughed. The sound surprised her, but did not need explaining.
