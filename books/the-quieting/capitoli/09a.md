# Chapter 9a — The Map at the Kitchen Table

Nell’s aunt, Reva, spread the residential program brochure across the kitchen table. The paper was glossy, cheerful, and illustrated with a young woman standing in a field beneath a cloudless sky. The field looked like a place where no one had ever needed to ask for directions.

“I don’t want to send you away,” Reva said. “I want to know you’ll be safe.”

Nell sat opposite her, one thumb rubbing the pencil groove in her finger. “You say ‘send’ as if I’m a parcel.”

“I’m trying not to panic.”

“Then don’t make your panic my address.”

Mara sat to one side, invited as a clinician and uncertain how much space that role permitted her. Reva wanted advice. Nell wanted an adult witness. Neither wanted Mara to decide for them, though each briefly hoped she would.

The psychiatrist joined by video. She asked what Nell wanted from the program and what Reva feared might happen without it. She explained that residential support could help some people when home was not safe or ordinary outpatient care was insufficient. It could also disrupt school, privacy, and the fragile sense of agency a grieving teenager was trying to recover. There was no single correct choice hidden inside the brochure.

Reva rubbed her eyes. Since her sister’s death, she had become responsible for a person she loved and did not understand. She had been sleeping on the sofa outside Nell’s room. She had taken unpaid leave. Her own grief had no official place in the evaluation.

“I’m afraid I’ll miss something,” she said.

Nell looked at her. “You can’t watch me all the time.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you trying?”

Reva began to cry, quietly at first, then with the exhausted helplessness of someone who had held a household upright by refusing to sit down. Nell looked alarmed. Mara felt the old impulse to intervene, to turn the moment into a manageable exercise. Instead she asked Reva whether she wanted a glass of water. Reva nodded.

They made a plan together. Nell would continue weekly therapy and attend one psychiatric follow-up. Reva would speak with a bereavement counselor and sleep in her own room. They would identify clear signs that meant they should seek urgent care, without treating every sad day as an emergency. The residential option would remain available, not as a threat but as a choice to revisit.

After the call ended, Nell unfolded her map. The lines had been drawn over and over until the paper grew soft.

“I’m not angry that she cried,” Nell said. “I’m angry that people only let adults cry when they’re supposed to be strong.”

Mara thought that perhaps the girl had already understood the thing the city was still trying to learn: that distress could be shared without being transferred, and care did not require pretending the carer was made of stone.
