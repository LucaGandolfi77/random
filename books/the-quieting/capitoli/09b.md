# Chapter 9b — The Unsent Photograph

Jonah’s colleague Elias had found a photograph of Tomas in the back of a personnel folder. It showed him standing beside a ferry window, his face turned toward the water. On the reverse someone had written the date and the word *better*. The photograph had been taken during a period when Tomas was attending therapy and working part-time. It did not show whether he was better. It showed that he had been outside, that the light had been bright, that he was looking at something beyond the glass.

Elias wanted to include it in the public exhibition on the archive. He believed the image would give the statistics a face. Jonah believed, for one impulsive hour, that it might give his brother back to the world in a form other than case notes.

Mina asked who had taken it. No one knew. She asked whether Tomas had agreed to have it displayed. No one knew that either.

The question made Jonah angry. He had spent years searching for evidence that Tomas had existed beyond the final months, and now that a piece of him had surfaced, the people around Jonah were asking him not to use it.

“He’s my brother,” he said.

“He was your brother,” Mina answered. “He was also himself.”

The sentence divided the two claims in a way Jonah had never wanted to consider. He had thought grief gave him a right to speak. Perhaps it gave him only a duty to speak carefully.

That evening he took the photograph to Mara. She did not tell him what to do. She asked what he wanted the image to accomplish. He said he wanted people to see that Tomas had been more than a failure statistic. She asked whether showing a private image without permission was the only way to accomplish that. He said no, but his anger remained.

They sat at Mara’s table. The photograph lay between them, its glossy surface catching the kitchen light. Jonah imagined every possible Tomas contained in it: the brother who had laughed at his imitations, the young man who had asked for an appointment and left before it began, the patient who had refused to let Jonah know how frightened he was. The picture could not choose among them. It could not tell him whether Tomas had wanted to be remembered publicly.

At last Jonah placed it in an archival sleeve and marked it restricted pending consent from the family. The exhibition would describe how the record had been found, not display the image.

It felt like a loss. The archive required him to accept a second one: not only that Tomas was gone, but that Jonah could not make the brother he missed available to everyone else as proof.

Mara reached across the table. Jonah let her hold his hand. He was grateful and resentful that both feelings could be true, and more grateful than resentful that she did not ask him to choose which one was more decent.
