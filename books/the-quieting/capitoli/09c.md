# Chapter 9c — The Sentence Jonah Could Not Keep

The argument began because Jonah had agreed to give an interview about the archive without telling Mara.

He had not planned to conceal it. He had simply kept moving through the day after saying yes, as if the decision belonged to a later version of himself. The journalist had promised not to identify patients and wanted to discuss how records could be altered by the institutions that produced them. Jonah believed the interview might help the public understand why the archive mattered. Mara believed he had accepted on behalf of a process they had agreed to undertake together.

“You don’t need my permission,” she said. “You needed to tell me.”

“I thought you’d ask me not to do it.”

“That’s why you didn’t tell me?”

“I thought you’d make it about the risk.”

“It is about risk. It’s also about trust.”

He said she wanted to control the story because she had spent her life inside the institution. She said he wanted to speak for the dead because the living had complicated his grief. Both sentences were unfair. Both landed where they were aimed.

For a few hours, Mara went back to the old apartment in her mind, to the years when Jonah’s attention had felt like warmth and then, later, like a light she could not bear to stand under. She remembered the first time he had asked her what she wanted and she had answered with what would be easiest. She remembered how often he had accepted the answer, until acceptance became a way of failing to ask again.

Jonah left. Mara spent the evening reading the proposed interview questions. She found that the journalist had written *Did the Service knowingly falsify evidence?* Jonah had not yet seen the draft. The question assumed an intention the archive did not establish. Mara called him and told him. He said she was right about the wording and wrong to treat that as a reason not to speak.

They met the next day. He agreed to insist on a distinction between deliberate fraud and incremental concealment. She agreed that he could speak about the archive without seeking permission from the Service. They would not share private records. If the journalist refused the distinction, he would withdraw.

“I don’t want you to protect me from making a mistake,” Jonah said.

“I don’t want to confuse protecting you with deciding for you.”

He looked tired. “And I don’t want honesty to be something we only practice when we already agree.”

“Neither do I.”

They stood at the river’s edge, with the sound of traffic moving above them. The water was too shallow to reflect the sky. Mara took his hand, not as an apology but as a request to continue. Jonah held it. The argument had not made their love more mature. It had made its unfinished parts visible.
