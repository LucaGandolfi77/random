# Chapter 9d — The Interview

Jonah’s interview was published on a Monday, beneath a photograph of the archive door. He spoke about incomplete records, the pressure to simplify outcomes, and the moral danger of letting institutions define success without asking the people who had received their care. He did not name Tomas. He did not describe the notebook or the photograph.

The headline read: **CITY PSYCHOLOGIST’S BROTHER LOST IN SYSTEM’S QUIET FAILURE.**

Jonah had not said that. He called the journalist. The editor explained that the headline had been written by a different person and would be amended online. The printed edition could not be changed. Mara heard the conversation from the kitchen. She watched Jonah press his fingers against the bridge of his nose, a gesture she had seen when he was trying not to turn anger into a verdict.

“I should have checked the headline,” he said afterward.

“You couldn’t check what they hadn’t written yet.”

“I knew how they wanted to frame it.”

“You told them what you believed. They changed it.”

“Maybe I let them.”

Mara wanted to say that he had done enough, that he had acted carefully. She also knew he was asking a harder question: how much responsibility belonged to the speaker when another person shaped the story? She did not want to comfort him by pretending he had no part in what happened, or punish him for a choice he could not have controlled.

They read the interview together. One paragraph had compressed Jonah’s account of Tomas into a single sentence about a person “lost in bureaucratic indifference.” It was not false, exactly. It was too clean. Tomas had also been loved, difficult, funny, frightened, secretive, and capable of refusing the help that Jonah thought might save him. The article contained no room for that complexity, because complexity did not fit the space between the photograph and the column break.

Jonah wrote a correction for the archive’s site. He said the service had failed Tomas in specific ways, but the story was not reducible to a single failure or a single institution. He asked the paper to link the correction. The paper did. The new link received fewer readers than the headline.

Mara felt angry at the machinery of attention and at herself for being surprised by it. She had spent her life trying to make careful distinctions in rooms where people were present to hear them. The public sphere took a sentence, separated it from its qualifications, and sent it away to live by itself.

That night Jonah said he was sorry he had not told her earlier about the interview. Mara said she was sorry she had assumed his secrecy meant he did not trust her. Neither apology erased the other’s part. They sat at the table with the corrected article between them, a page that was both theirs and not theirs.

“Would you do it again?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said. “Differently.”

She believed him because he did not claim that a better choice would have produced a better result. He had spoken, the story had been altered, and now he was responsible for the correction he could make. It was not the same as controlling the story. It was the work available to him.
