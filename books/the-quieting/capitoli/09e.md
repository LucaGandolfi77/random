# Chapter 9e — Sera’s New Office

After losing her executive role, Sera was assigned a smaller office on the clinic’s third floor. It had a window facing the brick wall of a neighboring building and enough room for two chairs if neither person needed to move. She returned to clinical work under supervision, which meant meeting patients while another clinician reviewed her plans and notes.

The first person assigned to her was a man who had been referred after a series of panic episodes. He asked whether the doctor who saw him had been involved in the public scandal. Sera said yes, she had signed the reports under review. She could tell him more about her role if he wanted, but he did not have to receive care from her.

The man said he wanted to know whether she would make him go back to work.

“I won’t make that decision alone,” Sera said. “We can discuss what you want, what your employer requires, and what your health allows. I can’t promise every institution will respect the limits we set.”

He watched her. “You sound like you’re trying not to promise too much.”

“I am.”

“Because of what happened?”

“Yes.”

He chose to continue the appointment. He described the episodes, the shift changes, the fear that his manager would see the referral in his file. Sera listened. She did not tell him that she had once believed a good institution could protect its patients simply by wanting to. She knew wanting had not been enough. She made a plan with him and asked permission before recording each detail.

Afterward, her supervisor, Dr. Chen, said the session had been careful but overly constrained. Sera had asked permission so often that the patient had begun doing the work of reassuring her. The observation hurt because it was accurate. Accountability could become self-absorption if the person who had done harm made every new interaction about proving they had changed.

Sera thought of the letter from the former patient who did not want a meeting, and of the man who had chosen to stay. There was no single gesture that demonstrated repair. The same humility could serve one person and burden another. She would have to let each patient’s needs be specific, even when specificity deprived her of a general rule.

At the end of the day she walked past the original Room of Return. A child sat on the floor drawing a blue house. The door was open. Sera felt an urge to enter and ask whether the child needed anything. She did not. The child was with a counselor, and the counselor had not asked for help.

Sera went to her smaller office, closed the file on her desk, and waited for the next appointment. She was not restored to the person she had been before the report. She was not meant to be. The question was whether she could continue to work without turning continuation into proof of innocence.
