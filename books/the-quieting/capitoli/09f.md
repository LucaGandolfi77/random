# Chapter 9f — The Last Ordinary Visit

Jonah remembered the last time he saw Tomas before the hospital as an ordinary afternoon, which was why he had distrusted the memory. It did not contain the signs he had later learned to look for. Tomas had asked for a ride to the market, complained about the price of coffee, and stopped to watch a child feed crumbs to a pigeon. He had forgotten why they had gone out, then remembered when Jonah pointed to the shopping list.

Nothing in the afternoon announced itself as last. The mind, afterward, returned to the scene carrying knowledge the people inside it did not possess. It placed a shadow beneath every gesture and asked why no one had seen it.

Tomas had not been well. He had also been capable of an ordinary afternoon. Jonah had spent years revising the memory so that it would explain the death: perhaps Tomas had been unusually quiet; perhaps he had looked tired; perhaps Jonah should have asked more directly whether he was safe. Each version made the past seem more legible and Jonah more culpable.

Mina had been with them that day. When Jonah told her his memory, she said Tomas had been impatient and had laughed at a pigeon that stole the entire crust from a child’s hand. Jonah did not remember the laugh. He remembered Tomas buying coffee. Their accounts differed not because one of them had failed to love him but because memory had kept different things.

Jonah asked Mina whether Tomas had seemed in danger.

“I can’t answer what I didn’t know then,” she said. “I can tell you what I saw. He was tired. He was distracted. He wanted the coffee. He joked with the child. He said he’d call me the next day.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

Jonah had always regarded the unreturned call as a clue. Mina regarded it as a fact without a known meaning. The two of them stood by the archive window, looking at the ferries move through gray water.

Jonah thought of Tolstoy’s attention to the moral life hidden inside ordinary acts, then wondered whether literature had trained him to suspect every ordinary moment of carrying a future verdict. Perhaps the truth was less shaped. Perhaps people simply lived through afternoons that later became important because they had ended.

He entered Mina’s account in the oral history catalogue with her approval, noting that the memory differed from his. He did not reconcile them. The record allowed both versions to sit beside each other, not as competing claims but as two people’s ways of keeping the same man present.

That evening Jonah told Mara about the market. She asked what kind of coffee Tomas had bought. Jonah remembered it was too bitter and Tomas had added sugar. The detail returned so clearly that, for a moment, he could almost hear his brother complain. Almost was not nothing. It was not the same as having him there.
