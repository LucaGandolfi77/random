# Chapter 9g — The Story of Tavi’s File

A reporter asked whether Tavi’s case could be used in the annual report. The request arrived through the communications office, which described her as an example of early intervention. Tavi had not consented to be an example. The office had not used her name, but the dates and the description of the fire could identify her to anyone in the building.

Mara objected. The communications director said no clinical details would be published and that the story could demonstrate the new process working as intended. Mara said the process had not produced a resolved outcome; Tavi still had symptoms and was being monitored. The director said that was precisely why the story was honest.

Mara was not convinced. The report wanted a narrative: a person notices a problem, enters care, receives support, and improves. Tavi’s actual experience had no such clean arc. Her sleep had improved after a medication review. The fire remained. The building’s accessibility complaint was still pending. She had found a way to talk with Luca that was imperfect and ongoing. To select the improving symptoms and omit the rest would not make the report false. It would make it misleading.

She asked Tavi whether she wanted to contribute. Tavi said no. She did not want her case discussed publicly, even without a name. She wanted the clinic to use the procedural lesson—that the patient should control release of a case narrative—without using her story to teach it.

The communications director argued that removing the case would weaken the report. Mara said a report was not entitled to a patient’s life because it needed a compelling example. They settled on an aggregate account of the process with no details that could identify Tavi. The report described the clinic’s policy on confidentiality and recorded that individual case stories would require explicit consent.

Tavi reviewed the language and asked for one sentence to be changed. The draft said “clients may choose to share their stories.” She said it made sharing sound like the expected choice. They changed it to “clients may decline to share their stories without affecting their care.”

The sentence took up less than one line. Mara felt the familiar temptation to praise Tavi for improving the policy. She stopped herself. Tavi had asked for a correction because the wording mattered to her; she did not owe the service a lesson in return.

When the report was published, the communications office ran a photograph of an empty room. It was not Tavi’s room. The caption said the image was illustrative. The service’s numbers and limitations appeared beside it, without a personal narrative to soften them.

The story was less vivid. It was also more honest about who had consented to be seen.
