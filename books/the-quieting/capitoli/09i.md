# Chapter 9i — The Archive’s First Visitor

The first person to request the recordings was not a journalist or a family member. She was a former clinician who had been present at the meeting where the recovery measure was challenged. She wanted to hear her own voice. She said she could not remember whether she had spoken or only intended to.

Jonah played the recording in the reading room. The woman sat with her hands clasped, listening to a younger version of herself say that return to work could be a poor measure of recovery if the patient had no paid leave. The sentence was brief. After it, the chair moved to the next speaker.

The woman covered her mouth. “I thought I’d said more.”

“Do you remember what you wanted to say?” Jonah asked.

“I remember being angry. I remember believing someone else would carry the point forward.”

She did not ask for a copy. She wanted the transcript corrected to show that she had raised the concern. The record already did. She asked whether the minutes had included it. Jonah checked. They had not.

The woman said she had not followed up at the time because she was caring for a parent and believed the issue would be addressed by the committee. She had left the Service two years later. She had not thought about the meeting until Jonah’s public interview made the omissions visible.

“Do you feel responsible?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said. “Not responsible for the whole thing. Responsible for my part. I had a voice in that room, and then I let the minutes speak for me.”

Jonah understood the feeling. He had spent years accusing institutions of silence and had only recently begun to notice where he had remained silent himself. He did not tell the woman this. Her visit was not an occasion for his confession.

She asked that her account be added to the archive as an oral history, but not used in the report without a second request. Jonah explained that the archive could preserve the recording under a temporary restriction and contact her before any public use. She agreed. Before leaving, she asked to hear the sentence once more.

They played it again. This time she did not cover her mouth. She listened to the younger voice as if it belonged to a colleague she had not seen in years, someone whose warning had been correct and insufficient.

After she left, Jonah updated the catalogue. He added that the speaker had returned to the record and requested that her contribution be acknowledged. He did not label the visit reconciliation. The archive could preserve that she had spoken, then failed to follow through, and later returned. It could not say whether she had forgiven herself, or whether the return had changed what she would do in another room.

Jonah closed the recording file. The voice remained in the archive, neither erased nor made complete.
