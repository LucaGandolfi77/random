# Chapter 10a — The City’s First Report

The first public report on the Stillness Protocol had been written in a converted railway office. Its authors slept on folding cots during the week the document was due. They had no proper data system, no stable funding, and no agreed definition of recovery. They had stories, handwritten notes, and the conviction that a city ought to do more than tell a frightened person to go home and try not to be frightened.

Sera had been one of them. She remembered the windows rattling when freight trains passed, the paper cups of bad coffee, the feeling that the work was too urgent to be elegant. The report described failures as honestly as it could. It said that the service might reduce harm; it said the evidence was preliminary. A council member called it weak. Another called it unscientific. The funding was cut by a third.

Then a young man died after being turned away from an emergency clinic. His family spoke to the press. The council asked for a system that could promise it would not happen again. Sera and her colleagues built the protocol: referral thresholds, follow-up intervals, priority classifications. Some of it helped. Some of it gave overworked staff a way to decide who needed attention first. The categories were meant to be provisional, but a provisional category is often a permanent category seen from the other side of a desk.

The archive Jonah uncovered did not reveal a conspiracy in the theatrical sense. It showed a succession of choices, each defended by urgency. A note was removed because it could identify a patient. Then the number of removed cases was not reported. Then the missing number made the program look more successful. The deception had not begun as a plan. It had become one by being repeated.

Aven Rusk read the first report after Mara requested it. He had not known it existed. When he asked why it had never been released, Sera told him the truth.

“I was trying to keep the service alive,” she said.

Rusk looked at the bound pages. “You kept alive the service’s right to define what survival meant.”

Sera did not answer. The sentence was not entirely fair, but fairness was not a door one could insist on before entering a difficult room.

At home that night, she wrote a letter to the former patients whose records had been used without consent. She did not know whether it would reach them or whether an apology from the institution would feel like another demand placed on the injured. She wrote anyway, then left the letter unsigned until morning.

In the morning, she signed it with her own name.
