# Chapter 10c — The Budget Room

The city’s finance committee met in a room where every chair faced a screen. The committee’s report showed the cost of the Civic Resilience Service, the number of completed appointments, the percentage of clients who returned to work within six weeks, and the proposed expense of the new safeguards. It did not show the people who had waited on hold, or the clinicians who had stayed late, or the families who had learned to ask a question without demanding an answer.

Mara attended with Sera, Imani, and a patient representative. The committee chair, Oran Pell, said the city could not fund a service that refused to define success. He was not hostile. He had three departments asking for money and a public asking why their taxes seemed to buy fewer visible results each year.

Mara explained that success had to include more than speed of return. A patient might remain away from work and still be safer, more supported, or better able to make choices. A person might return quickly because they feared losing their job. The same outcome—back at work—could indicate recovery, coercion, necessity, or all three.

Pell asked how the committee could tell the difference.

“By asking the person,” Mara said. “By recording the conditions around the return. By tracking whether the support continues. There won’t be one number that answers everything.”

“Then the public cannot compare us to other programs.”

“The public will be comparing a simplified number to another simplified number.”

The chair leaned back. He looked not offended but tired. “Doctor, I am not asking for perfection. I’m asking for a decision.”

Mara thought of Ada’s deferred appointment and of the clinician’s penciled note, *Call her sister*. The phrase had been a small human effort caught inside a system that did not complete it. A measure could reveal such failures, if it was designed to look at the spaces between intention and action. But a measure could also bury a person under a count and call the resulting silence evidence.

Imani spoke next. She proposed a public dashboard with several measures, including waiting times, patient-reported agency, follow-up completion, and staff capacity. It would be more difficult to read than a single success rate. She acknowledged that it could not eliminate judgment. The patient representative asked for a way to challenge inaccurate records.

The committee did not approve the full budget. It approved a six-month pilot and required the service to return with evidence of its effects. The clinics would continue, but the staff rota would remain thin. No one left believing they had won.

In the corridor, Imani asked Mara whether the meeting had gone badly.

“It went.”

“Is that good?”

“It’s what happened.” Mara heard the weakness of the answer and tried again. “We kept the service open, and we accepted a short pilot that may fail. We’ll have to watch what it does.”

The decision was not a resolution. It was another uncertain interval in which people would either receive care or not receive it. Mara understood why the city wanted one number: the number could be carried into a meeting, repeated without the weight of all the people it left out. She understood, too, why refusing the number entirely would leave power to those who already knew how to count.
