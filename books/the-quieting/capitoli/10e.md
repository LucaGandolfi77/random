# Chapter 10e — The Form with Two Boxes

The revised intake form had two boxes where the old one had demanded a single classification. One asked what the clinician was concerned about. The other asked what the patient was concerned about. The distinction seemed obvious until people began using it.

A patient wrote *I don’t want to lose my job* in the second box. The clinician wrote *sleep deprivation and memory lapses* in the first. Neither answer canceled the other. At review, the clinic had to consider whether it had addressed the concern the patient named or merely the one it could treat.

Imani helped train staff on the form. She explained that the two boxes were not a contest and not a way to make a patient responsible for diagnosing themselves. They were a reminder that expertise did not erase the person’s knowledge of their own life. One clinician asked what to do when the boxes contradicted each other. Imani said they should ask the patient what the contradiction meant before deciding it was a problem to solve.

The room became quiet. A senior doctor said that was easy in theory and difficult in a crisis. Imani agreed. The form did not supply more time, more staff, or more certainty. It only made the difference visible when those resources were absent.

Mara watched from the back. She remembered when Imani had asked whether she should make a record consistent. Now the young psychologist was explaining the value of keeping two answers side by side. Mara felt pride, but she kept it to herself. Imani did not need a mentor’s approval to make her own argument.

At the end of the training, a clinician approached with a question about an older patient who had written *I don’t want my daughter contacted* in the second box, while the clinician worried the patient could not manage alone. The old procedure would have turned the clinician’s concern into a reason to call the daughter. The new procedure required an assessment, a conversation about capacity and immediate safety, and documentation of the patient’s preference. It did not dictate the outcome.

The clinician wanted a rule that would protect them from being blamed. Imani recognized the wish. She had wanted the same thing when she first arrived at the clinic: a correct sequence that would make no one suffer from her uncertainty. There was no such sequence. There were obligations, consultation, the possibility of error, and the requirement to explain what had been done.

Mara later asked Imani whether she thought the new form would change the service.

“Some,” Imani said. “Not enough by itself.”

“Good answer.”

“It’s not good. It’s the answer.”

Mara smiled. She heard in the reply not a quotation of her own caution but an idea Imani had made her own. The form did not resolve the old disagreement between protection and agency. It gave the disagreement a place in the record, where it might be argued rather than hidden beneath a score.
