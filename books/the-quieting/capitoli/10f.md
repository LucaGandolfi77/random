# Chapter 10f — The Complaint Box

The clinic installed a locked box for patient complaints after the review panel found that the existing online form required a login. The box was placed near reception, then moved when patients said it could be seen from the waiting area. It was moved again after a wheelchair user pointed out that the slot was too high. Every adjustment made the box more usable and more difficult to pretend the first design had been neutral.

The first note said the evening receptionist had been kind. The second said the waiting room clock made the writer feel they were being timed. The third accused a clinician of sharing information with a family member without permission. The review officer, Nessa, had to determine whether the allegation involved an immediate safety issue, a misunderstanding, or a breach of confidentiality. She could not simply file it under dissatisfaction and move on.

Mara was asked to respond to the complaint because the clinician named was on her team. She knew the clinician, Amel, to be careful. She also knew that good intentions did not prove the complaint was false. The patient had said they did not want their brother contacted. The chart showed a phone call to the brother, with the notation *support plan discussed.*

Amel remembered the call. The patient had been disoriented after an overnight crisis and could not remember the brother’s number, so Amel had called using contact information already listed. She believed the patient had agreed. The patient remembered refusing. There was no recorded consent.

The facts did not fit a clean verdict. The patient had experienced the call as a violation. Amel had believed she was preventing the patient from being left alone. The procedure allowed emergency contact when a clinician judged the risk immediate, but did not require the patient to be incapable of making that choice. The policy’s ambiguity had given professional concern more authority than the patient’s refusal.

The review found that Amel had acted outside the service’s consent standard. She received supervision and retraining. The patient was offered an apology and the option to speak with Amel; they declined both and requested that the contact note be corrected. The note was amended to clarify that consent had not been documented and the patient disputed the call.

Amel was hurt by the finding. She had stayed late that night and believed she had helped. Mara let her be hurt without turning the review into a condemnation of her character. They discussed what the patient had asked for, what Amel had done, and what the service needed to change so that the same uncertainty did not automatically favor the clinician.

Afterward, Mara stood by the complaint box. It looked too small to hold the weight of the process around it. That was perhaps its value. It did not promise justice. It made a place where dissatisfaction could enter the institution without first becoming a crisis, a headline, or a story the institution chose to tell about itself.
