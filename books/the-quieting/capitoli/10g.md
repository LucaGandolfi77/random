# Chapter 10g — The Petition

The petition to restore the old risk categories reached the council with six thousand signatures. Its organizers said the new policy had made it harder for families to get help. They described nights when a relative had refused treatment, the fear of being asked to wait while someone deteriorated, and the knowledge that a missed warning could not be repaired afterward. Some of their stories were true. Some were told without the consent of the people they described.

Rusk read the petition in his office. He did not dismiss the signers as frightened or uninformed. He had felt their fear himself. He also recognized in the proposed categories the old promise that a number could save the person whose life refused to be reduced to one.

A family member testified that the service had once turned her brother away because he did not meet the threshold. She wanted the threshold lowered. Mara said the threshold had been designed to prioritize limited care, and had sometimes become a reason to deny care. Lowering it might bring more people in; it could also classify more people as urgent and leave clinicians with no additional staff.

The family member said, “So what are we supposed to do while you debate the number?”

Mara did not have an answer that could carry the weight of the question. She said the city should fund an after-hours team and allow families to request consultation without automatically overriding the patient’s choices. She said the proposal could not guarantee that no one would be missed. She hated that the statement sounded inadequate. It was inadequate. A truth could be necessary and still fail to comfort the person who needed more.

Lio spoke as a worker who had experienced both the relief of being assessed and the pressure of being categorized. He said a protocol should help people get care, not decide in advance what kind of person they were. The organizer asked whether he believed families should have no say.

“No,” Lio said. “I believe family should have a way to raise a concern. I also believe the patient should be in the room whenever possible. Those aren’t opposite things unless the system makes them opposite.”

The council agreed to hold a public consultation and rejected the petition’s demand to reinstate the old categories. It approved additional funding for an after-hours consultation line, but not a full mobile team. The compromise satisfied no one. The family group said the city had left people waiting. The patient board said the city had preserved consent while failing to fund enough care for consent to mean much.

Mara left the hearing with the sense of having defended a principle that could not answer a telephone at two in the morning. She told Jonah she feared that the new policy was more ethical on paper than in practice.

“Then the practice has to catch up,” he said.

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then the record has to say it didn’t.”

She wanted a stronger promise. Jonah offered the one he could keep: he would not call the gap success.
