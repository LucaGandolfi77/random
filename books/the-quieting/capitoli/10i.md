# Chapter 10i — The Form Tavi Changed

Tavi was invited to a patient consultation meeting because the service wanted feedback on its consent policy. She asked whether the invitation was based on her file. Imani said she had been invited as a former patient, but the group did not need to discuss any part of her treatment. Tavi asked if she could leave whenever she wanted. Imani said yes.

At the meeting, the clinicians presented a draft that allowed patients to choose whether family could be contacted. The options were “yes,” “no,” and “in emergencies.” Tavi said the last option was too vague. Who defined an emergency? Would the clinic notify the patient afterward? Could the patient specify which family member? Could a person change the answer between appointments?

The policy lead explained that a document could not anticipate every situation. Tavi said she was not asking for it to. She wanted the policy to admit that the decision involved judgment and to require the clinician to explain that judgment afterward. She also wanted patients to be able to choose someone outside their family: a friend, a neighbor, a colleague, or no one.

The group spent the hour revising the form. The new version included a blank line for the patient to name a contact, a separate line for situations in which the clinic might act without permission, and a requirement to record why. It did not make every choice easy. It made the choice specific enough to be discussed.

During the break, Tavi told Imani she would not speak about the fire or the wrong words she had used. She would help with the policy but did not want her case turned into an example. Imani said she understood. Tavi asked if the meeting minutes would identify her as a former patient. Imani said they could list her as a community adviser instead. Tavi chose that description.

The policy team adopted several of the changes. One administrator worried the blank line could make staff feel responsible for managing a patient’s complicated relationships. Tavi said they already were responsible; the blank line only made the patient part of the decision. The administrator did not agree, but the point entered the minutes.

On the way home, Tavi stopped at the building office to ask about the evacuation plan. The management company had installed a new ramp and added the night porter’s number. The repairs had taken months. She did not feel triumphant. She felt that one part of the place where she lived had become easier to navigate, and that she had spent more time than she wished making it happen.

Luca met her at home. He asked whether the meeting had been tiring. She said yes. He asked if she wanted to talk about it. She said not yet. He nodded and began making dinner.

The space between asking and insisting had not become effortless. It had become familiar enough for both of them to practice.
