# Chapter 10j — The Night the Line Was Busy

The after-hours line received four calls at once during a storm, and for eleven minutes the fourth caller heard only a busy signal.

The caller was a school caretaker named Orin, whose daughter had been unable to remember the way home from the bus stop. She was standing beneath a shop awning two streets from their apartment, frightened and embarrassed. Orin found her by retracing the route with a neighbor. By the time the line called him back, they were both home.

No one had been injured. The delay had still mattered. Orin said the line’s recorded message promised a clinician would answer immediately. The message was inaccurate. The administrator said callers in crisis should use emergency services if the line was busy. Orin said that did not answer why the service had advertised a capacity it did not have.

The clinic reviewed the call. The after-hours team had been understaffed; two clinicians were already supporting other callers. The service had assumed call volume would remain below the threshold. It had not. The line’s model had counted completed calls, not callers who could not get through.

Mara met with Orin and his daughter, Sima, at their request. Sima was nineteen and had been under treatment for a neurological condition diagnosed before the Quieting became part of the family’s vocabulary. Her recent memory difficulties had several possible explanations, and her medical team was reviewing them. Orin worried that the service would attribute every lapse to her existing diagnosis. Sima worried that her father would answer questions on her behalf.

They agreed that Sima would speak first in appointments. Orin could add information if she asked. The after-hours plan would list a backup contact and the nearest open clinic. Sima said she did not want to be tracked by a city app. The counselor agreed to provide a paper card instead.

Mara asked whether the family wanted to file a complaint about the busy signal. Orin said yes. Sima said she wanted to be included in the wording. The complaint stated that the line’s promise exceeded its staffing and that the return call came after the immediate need had passed. It did not say the line had failed completely. It had eventually reached them; the delay had nonetheless exposed a weakness.

The service changed its recorded message to explain that calls could be queued and list an emergency alternative. It added a callback estimate and published the number of unanswered calls alongside completed consultations. The city funded another clinician for evenings, but not enough to guarantee no future delays.

Orin said the new message was more honest. Sima said she still hoped not to need it. They left with the paper card in her pocket.

Mara later thought about the danger of treating every unreturned call as a moral failure by the person who did not answer, and every answered call as proof the system worked. There were limits imposed by resources, limits imposed by the body, and limits no one had yet understood. A city could not abolish them. It could stop hiding them behind a promise that made the caller responsible for believing it.
