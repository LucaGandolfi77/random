# Chapter 11 — All Happy Cities

The council chamber had been built for the age when cities believed marble could make decisions sound permanent. The room smelled of polish and old paper. Behind the public benches, a screen displayed the hearing title: **THE STILLNESS PROTOCOL: SAFETY, EVIDENCE, AND TRUST.**

Jonah sat beside Mara. Sera sat alone in the first row reserved for Service staff. Nell came with her aunt and carried the unfinished map folded in her coat.

“Are you frightened?” Mara whispered to Jonah.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He glanced at her. “That’s a strange thing to say.”

“I mean I’m glad you can tell me.”

He squeezed her hand once beneath the table.

The council heard from a psychiatrist who explained the limits of current evidence, from a former patient who described the help that had saved her, and from a union representative who said the protocol had been used to pressure employees back to work. The contradictions did not cancel one another. They made a truer picture.

A councilwoman asked Mara whether repeated emotional crises could damage a person’s brain.

“Some people experience lasting cognitive impairment after repeated severe distress,” Mara said. “We do not understand all causes, and we cannot predict with certainty who will be affected. Emotion itself is not an illness. Crying is not evidence of damage. Panic is not a character flaw. The danger is real; so is the danger of treating every distressed person as already broken.”

“Then why have a protocol?”

“Because people need care before they reach a breaking point. Not because they need to be made convenient.”

She spoke about the rooms, the clinicians, the nights when someone stayed alive because a stranger answered. Then she spoke about the falsified summary and Ada’s deferred appointment. Her voice shook once. She did not mistake the shaking for failure.

The councilwoman asked if she believed Sera Okafor should be dismissed.

“I believe Dr. Okafor must answer for what she signed. I also believe the city should not turn accountability into a way to avoid looking at the policies it rewarded.”

The room stirred. Behind the council, rain moved across the windows in long gray lines.

Tolstoy begins *Anna Karenina* with a sentence about happy and unhappy families, each unhappy in its own way. Mara thought cities might be like that too: every system appearing successful in the same way, every failure particular, intimate, stubbornly human.

Nell was called next. She did not use Mara’s language. She described the map she was making and the places where she could not sit without being watched. She said that when adults called her “high risk,” she felt as if they had started grieving her while she was still there.

“I want help,” she said. “I don’t want to be a warning.”

No one applauded. The sentence was too serious for applause. But the clerk wrote it down.
