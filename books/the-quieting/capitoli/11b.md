# Chapter 11b — The Work of Saying I

Imani was not invited to speak at the hearing. She had no authority, and her testimony would have been treated as an anecdote from a junior employee. She was asked to submit a written statement to the internal review panel, which she began three times and abandoned twice.

The first draft sounded like a defense of Mara. The second sounded like an indictment of the clinic. In the third she tried to describe what it felt like to sit beside a patient and understand that the form she was filling out might determine how long the patient waited for help. She wrote about Lio, but removed his identifying details. She wrote about the woman who called at two in the morning and asked whether her husband’s crying meant he was becoming stupid. She wrote about the sentence she had learned in training: *A person may refuse one kind of help and still be asking for help.*

She worried the sentence was too polished, as if it could conceal that she had once thought refusal meant resistance. She left it in.

The review panel asked her to attend a private session with the patient representatives. Mina was there. So was a former patient named Dena, whose husband had been the caller. Dena said the crisis team had arrived quickly and treated her husband gently. She also said the follow-up system had called her six times at work after she asked them to call after six in the evening.

“I am grateful,” she said, “and I was angry. I don’t want the gratitude to erase the anger, or the anger to erase the gratitude.”

Imani recognized the sentence as the shape of the book she had not read yet, the thought Mara had been trying to teach her without making it a lesson: that contradiction was not always confusion. Sometimes it was the most accurate account available.

When her turn came, Imani said she had believed that consistency meant fairness. She now understood that the same question asked in the same order could make one person feel safe and another feel inspected. She said she wanted the clinic to keep measuring outcomes, but to include patients in deciding which outcomes mattered. She said staff needed enough time to ask questions that did not fit into the registry.

One of the panel members asked whether she believed the protocol had harmed people.

“Yes,” Imani said.

“Do you believe it has helped people?”

“Yes.”

“Which answer should the public hear?”

“Both. Otherwise we are asking them to trust a story instead of a service.”

The panel asked her to help design a new training module on consent and uncertainty. She accepted, then asked that she not be the only junior clinician involved. It was the first time she had asked for a role without assuming she must be grateful to receive it.

Afterward she went to the clinic. Lio was waiting for his appointment and reading the harbor union bulletin. He had returned to work on reduced shifts. The memory lapses had eased with sleep, though he still had nights when a call sign arrived late and his stomach clenched. He told Imani he did not want to give a testimony about being rescued.

“I don’t think you have to,” she said.

“Good. I’m not a story with a tidy ending.”

“No,” she said. “You’re a person whose next appointment is on Thursday.”

Lio nodded. It was not poetic, but it was true.
