# Chapter 11c — The Steps Outside

After the hearing, the crowd divided on the stone steps into those who wanted to speak and those who wanted to leave before anyone asked them to. The rain had stopped but the air still held it. Reporters stood beneath the council’s columns, microphones lifted like small instruments of accusation.

Lio had come as a witness to the patient advisory group, not as a speaker. When a journalist asked whether he believed the protocol had failed him, he looked toward the harbor and said, “That isn’t the whole question.” The journalist asked what the whole question was. Lio said he wanted to keep his job without having to prove that he was either perfectly well or completely incapable. He wanted his employer to change the schedule that had helped make him ill. He wanted the clinic to treat him without deciding what his story meant for the city.

The journalist asked whether he feared permanent damage.

“I fear losing my livelihood,” Lio said. “I fear making a mistake at work. I fear being asked to talk about fear as if it’s the only thing I am.”

The reporter’s next question arrived more slowly. Lio answered that he had received useful care, and that care did not erase the conditions he returned to. He said the clinic had helped him sleep; the union had helped him keep his hours; his employer still had not hired the missing night workers. He refused the invitation to name himself a success story.

Imani watched from the steps. She had seen Lio struggle to put words in order when he was exhausted. Today he spoke with no difficulty, but the difference did not mean he had been cured, any more than a stumble meant he was lost. The public wanted a visible arc: crisis, help, transformation, gratitude. Real people had to live the weeks that came after the arc ended.

A woman from the patient group asked the journalists not to photograph Nell. Nell had not attended; she was at the map workshop, where the library’s printer had misplaced the large-type proof. Someone had nevertheless printed her name in a morning headline. Jonah had already asked the paper to remove the reference to her school. They would correct it, the editor said, but the story had been copied elsewhere.

Mara felt the familiar rush of responsibility, the sense that each failure had become hers because she could see it. She wanted to run back into the chamber and demand a public correction. Instead, she asked the patient group what they wanted done. They agreed to issue a statement about consent and minors’ privacy. Imani offered to draft it; Mina insisted that a patient representative review every sentence.

The statement was plain and unsensational. It did not describe Nell’s grief. It said only that a young person had the right to speak for herself or not to speak, and that public concern did not cancel privacy.

By the time they finished, the reporters had moved on. The city’s attention was already searching for another story. Mara stood beside Jonah at the foot of the steps. Neither mistook the dispersing crowd for an ending. The hearing had opened a door; it had also exposed who might be pushed through it without consent.
