# Chapter 11d — Dena’s Testimony

Dena did not want to testify. She came to the public hearing because the patient advisory group had asked what she wanted the city to hear, and she had said she wanted the city to stop confusing gratitude with consent. When a council clerk offered to read her statement, Dena said she would read it herself, provided she could sit rather than stand at the lectern.

She spoke about the week her infant was in hospital, about her husband’s panic, and about the clinic team that came to their apartment. The clinicians had been kind. One had sat on the kitchen floor with her husband while Dena warmed a bottle. The psychiatrist had explained the treatment options without insisting on one. Dena said that care had mattered. Then she described the follow-up calls at the wrong hour, the notes that referred to her as “supporting spouse,” and the assumption that because she was present, she could be treated as part of the plan without being asked.

“I was grateful,” she said, “and I was also exhausted. I wanted someone to ask me what I needed, not only whether I could keep helping him.”

A council member asked if the clinic had failed her.

Dena paused. She looked down at the paper, though she had memorized the sentence. “The people who came to our home helped. The system around them did not always remember that I was a person too. I don’t know how to answer with one word.”

The room shifted. The council member tried to reformulate the question. Dena said she had answered it already.

Mara watched from the witness row. She felt pride, then recognized that pride could make Dena’s act into something Mara wanted to claim. Dena had not come to validate the service or condemn it. She had come to say what happened in the shape she remembered it. That shape did not fit the categories the committee had prepared.

After her testimony, Dena asked the clerk whether the written transcript would include pauses. The clerk said it would record spoken words but not silence unless requested. Dena asked that one pause be marked: the moment after she said she had been grateful. It had taken her time to continue because people tended to hear gratitude as the end of the story.

The clerk added the notation.

Outside the chamber, Dena’s husband waited with the baby, who was now walking unsteadily between them. He looked tired and well in the limited ways a person could be both. Dena did not ask him how her statement had sounded. She lifted the child and said they were going to get something warm to eat.

Mara watched them descend the steps. She thought of Tolstoy’s insistence that a life could turn on ordinary particulars, and of how no great principle could substitute for the person who had to carry the groceries home. The hearing would discuss policy. Dena would go on living inside what the policy had done.
