# Chapter 11e — The Question That Would Not Narrow

After Mara testified, Councilman Rusk asked her whether the city should intervene when a person’s distress seemed likely to compromise their judgment.

The question was not a trick. It was the question every clinician feared and every family hoped someone could answer before it became urgent. Mara asked him what he meant by “likely.” Rusk said a person might have repeated crises, might forget instructions, might refuse help despite appearing unable to care for themselves.

“Those are different situations,” Mara said. “A person may refuse care and still understand the choice. A person may be confused for reasons we can assess and treat. A person may be in immediate danger. We should not use one measure to decide all three.”

Rusk asked if delay could cost a life.

“Yes.”

“Can intervention save one?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you so concerned about the authority to intervene?”

“Because an intervention can also cause harm, and because the authority becomes dangerous when we stop reviewing how it is used. The fact that a decision is difficult does not make it safer to hide the decision inside a score.”

She knew the answer would not satisfy the people who wanted a rule that made no one responsible for judgment. Rusk himself looked dissatisfied, but he nodded. He asked how a clinician should tell the difference between a patient who needed protection and a patient who was being denied agency. Mara said they should involve the patient as much as possible, seek independent review when possible, record the reasons, and revisit the decision. She said none of those steps guaranteed a right outcome.

A member of the public asked whether repeated panic could make someone permanently stupid. The question came from a man who had once been a patient at the clinic. His voice shook with anger.

“No,” Mara said. “That is not a responsible way to describe it. Some people experience lasting cognitive difficulties after repeated severe distress, but we do not know enough to predict that for an individual. Panic does not make a person stupid. Distress does not erase their worth or their right to be heard.”

The man said he had been told he would become a burden if he did not comply with treatment.

Mara felt the room turn toward her. “That should not have been said to you.”

“Can you promise it won’t be said to somebody else?”

“No,” she said. “I can promise we will make it easier to report and review, and that I will not pretend the promise is the same as prevention.”

It was an unsatisfactory answer. It was also the only one she could give without converting a hope into a guarantee. In the gallery, Imani watched the people listening to Mara. She saw disappointment, fear, and, in a few faces, the relief of hearing uncertainty acknowledged rather than concealed.

After the hearing, Rusk told Mara that he had hoped she would offer a clearer rule.

“I wish I could.”

“Would you trust someone who did?”

Mara considered. “Not without asking what they left out.”

Rusk looked back toward the chamber. He did not thank her. He did not accuse her of evasion. The question remained between them, large enough to require another hearing.
