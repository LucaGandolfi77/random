# Chapter 11f — Sera Gives Her Account

Sera testified before the full council after the independent review released its findings. She sat at the same table where Mara had spoken, but without the Service badge she once wore. The chair introduced her as a former director, a phrase that seemed both accurate and insufficient. Sera had not stopped being a psychiatrist. She had stopped being the person who could decide what the institution said about itself.

The council chair asked whether she had knowingly misled the public.

“I knew the summaries were incomplete,” Sera said. “I did not know how the omissions would be used later, though I should have understood that a number without its exclusions becomes a claim.”

“Are you saying you were not responsible?”

“No. I am saying responsibility has more than one level. I signed. The council demanded proof of success. The service adopted a measure that rewarded quick return to work. Staff objections were recorded and not acted on. The patients who were most affected were not part of the review. I am responsible for my choices. I am not the only cause of the system.”

The answer angered some council members because it refused both the defense of an individual scapegoat and the comfort of a single culprit. Sera spoke about the early years, the crisis that had led to the protocol, the patients who had received timely help. She also spoke about the people whose care had been delayed, whose requests for privacy were overridden, whose recovery was defined by an employer’s schedule.

A former patient in the gallery asked why Sera had not resigned sooner.

Sera looked toward the person. “Because I believed I could repair the service from inside. I was afraid that leaving would make it easier for the council to close it. I also feared losing the work I had spent my life building. I told myself the first reason was the only one.”

The admission made Mara uncomfortable. It did not sound noble, and it was not offered as an absolution. Sera had been protective of the clinic and of her own place within it. The motives had braided together until even she could not separate them cleanly.

The council asked what she would change. Sera said the service should publish its exclusions, allow patient representatives to review outcome measures, provide a way to challenge records, and fund enough staff to make consent more than a sentence said while someone was already in crisis. She said no protocol could guarantee the absence of harm.

“Then how will we know whether the new one is better?” a council member asked.

“By asking people affected by it,” Sera replied. “And by being willing to hear answers that make us change it.”

After the testimony, she walked out without speaking to the press. Mara met her in the corridor. Sera asked whether she had sounded defensive. Mara said she had sounded responsible and still frightened. Sera said that was probably true.

They did not embrace. They stood together for a moment, then went in separate directions, carrying a shared history that had no final ruling.
