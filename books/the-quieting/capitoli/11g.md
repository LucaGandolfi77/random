# Chapter 11g — The Recess

The council recessed for twenty minutes after Mara’s testimony. The pause was longer than any interruption she had taken during the morning, and she spent it in a corridor where no one seemed to know whether she was a witness or a colleague. A clerk offered water. A reporter asked if she was worried that the clinic would lose funding. A patient representative asked whether she wanted to sit down. Each person meant well. Each offered a different version of what she was supposed to need.

Mara declined the interview and accepted the chair. The room beyond the corridor filled with voices, then quieted. She could hear the harbor bells through the high windows. She thought of Ada’s file, not as a document but as the several versions of her sister that had been compressed into it: the girl with cherries on her fingers, the young woman who asked if a mind could grow too tired to recognize its own life, the patient whose appointment was deferred, the person whose death could be made to serve arguments she had never heard.

Mara wanted the city to know what had happened. She also wanted her sister’s life to remain larger than the report. The wants did not fit neatly together. Perhaps that was the reason she had hesitated before agreeing to testify: not fear of exposure alone, but fear that the exposure would create a version of Ada that could travel without her.

Jonah appeared at the end of the corridor. He did not ask if she was all right. He asked if she wanted him to sit with her or go back inside. Mara said she wanted him to sit. He did. They did not touch. Their shoulders were close enough that she could feel warmth through their coats.

“Do you think I said enough?” she asked.

“I think you said what you knew.”

“Is that enough?”

“I don’t know.”

She smiled faintly. It was the answer she trusted most.

When the recess ended, Mara returned to the chamber. The committee chair resumed the questioning, and the city continued to ask for a conclusion. She gave no promise that reform would prevent every future failure. She said the service could make its failures easier to see and less likely to repeat. The distinction was small in language and large in consequence: a promise of perfection would make the first error an excuse to abandon the entire project; a promise of scrutiny required the institution to keep returning to the error.

At the end of the day, she walked down the council steps with Jonah. The reporters called after them. Neither turned. The city had heard her, though she could not know what it had understood.
