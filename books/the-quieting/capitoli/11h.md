# Chapter 11h — The Relative in the Gallery

A woman named Salma came to the public hearing carrying a folder of hospital letters. Her brother had experienced a long period of distress and had eventually received inpatient treatment. She had spent months trying to persuade him to accept help, then weeks trying to persuade the system to listen when he did. The letters showed appointments, referrals, and cancellations. They also showed that Salma had called repeatedly on his behalf.

She had permission to speak about the facts. She did not have permission to reveal his name, diagnosis, or the things he had told her in confidence. Before her turn, she asked the clerk to confirm that the transcript would not include the folder’s identifying details. The clerk said they would redact them. Salma asked who would review the redaction. The clerk named a legal officer and a patient representative.

When Salma spoke, she praised one psychiatrist who had taken her call and criticized the service that had left her brother waiting. She said she had wanted an emergency intervention, and that her brother had refused. She had been terrified that respecting his refusal would leave him alone; she had been equally terrified that overriding him would destroy the trust between them.

“I needed someone to help me understand what I could do,” she said. “Instead, every person I called asked me to decide whether he was safe. I am his sister. I am not a clinician, and I could not carry that decision by myself.”

Rusk asked whether she believed the city should have intervened sooner.

“I believe I should not have been made the only person deciding whether it was time.”

The answer shifted the question. The committee began discussing family consultation, clinical responsibility, and after-hours support. Salma’s testimony did not resolve the conflict between the patient’s autonomy and the relative’s fear. It showed that leaving families outside the room did not make them less involved; it made them involved without guidance.

Afterward, Mara asked Salma if she wanted to speak privately with the service’s family liaison. Salma said she would consider it, then asked whether her brother could access the account she had given. Mara explained the transcript and review process. Salma requested a copy before it became public.

The request delayed publication by a week. The legal officer found two details that could identify her brother and removed them. A patient representative reviewed the changes. Salma approved the final version. It contained less than she had said, but did not change the substance of her account.

Mara thought of the way stories lost their texture as they passed through institutions, and of the way they could also expose the person who had trusted the institution with them. Accuracy was not only the amount of detail preserved. It was also the care taken not to reveal what had not been offered.

Salma left the chamber with her folder. She had not been asked to declare whether the service was good or bad. She had described what it had been like to need help alongside someone who might not accept it. The record held her words, with the silences she had chosen intact.
