# Chapter 11i — The Word They Chose

The patient advisory board argued over whether to call its members “consultants,” “representatives,” or “people with experience of the service.” Each phrase carried a different claim. “Consultant” sounded professional but could hide the fact that members were unpaid. “Representative” suggested they spoke for more people than had elected them. “People with experience” sounded accurate and faintly diminutive.

Mina wanted to keep the title open. Lio said that a changing name would make it harder for the public to know what the board did. Imani suggested they describe the role in a sentence instead of looking for a word that could carry everything.

Tavi, attending her second meeting, said she wanted the title to distinguish them from current patients. She did not want every disagreement treated as disclosure of her medical history. They chose “community advisers,” with a note that advisers could have personal or professional experience with the service but were not required to discuss it.

The decision was small, yet it changed the tone of the next meeting. A council clerk introduced Tavi as a former patient, having read the old attendance sheet. Tavi corrected her: “I’m here as a community adviser.” The clerk apologized and amended the record. Tavi did not give a speech about privacy. She returned to the question on the agenda: how the service should describe risk to a family member when the patient had not agreed to share details.

Mara watched from the staff side of the room. She had not known Tavi long enough to feel entitled to pride in her. She felt respect, and an urge to make the moment proof that the new policy worked. She resisted both the urge and the conclusion. Tavi’s correction did not prove anything about the service. It was a correction made by a person who had noticed a boundary had been crossed and chosen to name it.

After the meeting, Tavi told Imani that she might not attend next month. The meetings were useful but long. She had a work deadline and wanted her evenings back. Imani said the board could arrange childcare or adjust the schedule. Tavi said she did not want a solution before deciding whether she wanted to continue.

They agreed to send her the next agenda and ask once. Tavi could decline without explaining. Imani wrote the arrangement in the minutes, then noticed how strange it felt to record a person’s permission not to participate. The service had often documented consent to enter, disclose, or receive treatment. It had rarely recorded the right to stop.

When Tavi left, she took no papers with her. She had not become an emblem of patient voice. She was a woman who had corrected a title, contributed to a policy, and wanted an evening without another meeting. The board’s responsibility was to make room for that choice, even if her absence left an empty chair.
