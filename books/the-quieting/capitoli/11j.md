# Chapter 11j — The Transcript

The transcript of the hearing arrived with one hundred and twelve pages and a notice inviting corrections within ten days. Mara read it at home. The printed words had none of the room’s air in them: no hesitation before an answer, no glass of water lifted and set down, no movement in the gallery when Dena spoke about being grateful and angry.

The transcript had rendered one of Mara’s pauses as an em dash. It had also recorded her saying, “We cannot know,” when she had said, “We cannot know yet.” The missing word changed the sentence from a limit into a final refusal.

She requested the correction. The clerk asked for audio verification. The audio confirmed that the word had been spoken. The transcript would be amended in the public record, though printed copies already distributed could not be recalled. The correction would appear as a note at the back.

Jonah said the note mattered. Mara said it would be read by almost no one. He replied that the point was not the number of readers; it was that the record should not claim she had said something she had not said.

They disagreed about another passage. The transcript included a patient’s description of an earlier clinic visit, identifying enough details that the patient could be recognized by a former colleague. The legal office had classified the testimony as public because the person had spoken at an open hearing. The patient had not asked for the details to be redacted. They had not been told what might identify them.

Mina contacted the person and asked what they wanted. The patient asked for the identifying details to be removed, even if the transcript had to be republished. The council’s legal adviser said the transcript was a public record and could not simply be altered. The patient board argued that the record could preserve the fact of the testimony while protecting details not relevant to the policy discussion.

For two weeks, the question moved between offices. The city eventually added a privacy addendum and replaced the online transcript with a redacted version, preserving the earlier copy under restricted access. The official record now included a note that the original had contained identifying details removed at the speaker’s request.

The process was cumbersome, and it did not undo the copies already downloaded. The patient said they were satisfied with the correction but still wished they had understood the risks before speaking. The council added a consent explanation to the witness registration, including the right to ask about privacy before testimony.

Mara read the corrected transcript from the beginning. It remained unlike the hearing. The paper could not reproduce the way a voice had trembled or the long time a person had taken to answer. But it could avoid claiming certainty where the speaker had said “yet.”

That small word mattered to her. It left a future open, not as a promise but as a fact about what they did not know at the time. The transcript had begun as a record of speech. It had become another place where the city had to decide whether accuracy included the right to revise itself.
