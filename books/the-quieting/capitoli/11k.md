# Chapter 11k — The Draft That Named No One

The press obtained an early draft of the oversight report and published a column claiming the Service had knowingly suppressed a “wave of Quieting cases.” The phrase was not in the report. It was not supported by the data. The article quoted a paragraph about missing follow-ups and transformed the gaps into a dramatic rise in cognitive injury.

The city’s communications office wanted Mara to issue a categorical denial. She refused. They did not know whether the underlying number of people affected had been undercounted; that was one of the questions the independent review had been created to answer. She could say the article’s phrase was unsupported. She could not say the risk did not exist.

The reporter called her. He said the public deserved to know whether Bellwether had hidden a public-health catastrophe. Mara explained that the Quieting was not an epidemic with a known case definition. It was a name used in the city for persistent cognitive effects that might follow repeated severe distress in some people, with uncertain causation and no predictive threshold. She said that uncertainty did not make the people affected imaginary, and did not justify inventing a wave.

The reporter said the answer was too complicated to fit the column. Mara said the complexity was the point. He asked if she was protecting the city. She said she was trying not to turn people’s distress into a statistic the evidence could not support.

The council released a statement correcting the claim and published the report’s limitations. The article remained online, updated with a note. Its original headline continued to circulate. The correction received less attention than the fear.

Mina asked the advisory board whether to respond publicly. Some members wanted to describe their experiences to counter the claim. Others did not want the city’s correction to require patients to disclose themselves. They drafted a statement that said no person’s history should be used to prove or disprove the existence of a population-level risk without consent. It also said the city should fund research and care without exaggeration.

The statement was not dramatic. It was shared by a few local organizations and ignored by most of the national press. Mara found it more trustworthy than the article because it did not ask suffering to become spectacle in order to count.

At home, Jonah asked if the coverage made her regret testifying. She said no, then admitted she regretted how little control she had over what followed. He said he felt the same about his interview. They had both chosen to speak, and both had watched their words travel beyond their intentions.

“Would you speak again?” Jonah asked.

“Yes. But I’d want the record to include what I can’t know.”

“Would anyone read that?”

“Someone might need to.”

She thought of Proust’s long sentences, how a thought could carry its own qualifications without losing its direction. Public language rarely had the patience for such movement. She would have to make the sentences as careful as she could and accept that they might be cut short.
