# Chapter 11l — The Last Question

The final question at the public hearing came from a boy in the gallery who looked younger than Nell. He stood with a folded piece of paper and asked Mara whether she thought people should be afraid of crying.

The room waited. Mara had been speaking for hours. Her mouth felt dry; her notes had acquired soft edges from being handled. She wanted to say no, because she did not want a child to learn fear from the hearing. She wanted to say yes, because the city’s story included real people who had suffered lasting changes after repeated distress. She wanted to explain the difference without making the boy responsible for understanding an entire field of uncertain knowledge.

“Crying is not a danger by itself,” she said. “You don’t have to be afraid of having feelings. If you or someone you love is having a hard time, you can ask for help before it becomes an emergency. And if someone tells you that crying means you are broken, that person is wrong.”

The boy looked at the paper in his hand. He asked if panic could make a person forget things.

“It can make it harder to think while it’s happening,” Mara said. “If someone keeps having problems with memory, they should speak to a clinician and have it assessed. But a panic attack doesn’t mean a person is becoming stupid. It doesn’t tell us the future.”

He nodded. He did not say whether the answer was for himself or someone else. Mara did not ask. The chair thanked him and moved to close the hearing.

Afterward, a reporter asked whether Mara’s answer had been too reassuring. Another asked whether it had been too alarming. She said it was the answer she believed the evidence allowed. Both reporters wrote something down. She did not watch to see which phrase they would keep.

Outside, she found Imani beside the steps. The younger clinician said she had been thinking about the boy’s question. She wanted to know whether a child should hear all the uncertainty or only the part that helped.

“Tell them what they need to know in words they can use,” Mara said. “Don’t make uncertainty the child’s burden. Don’t hide it from them either. There’s a difference.”

“What is it?”

“I’m still learning.”

Imani smiled. “I thought you’d say that.”

They walked down the steps together. A reporter called Mara’s name. She turned once and said she had no further comment. The boy had already left with the adult who had brought him. The folded paper was gone. She hoped the answer had been enough for the question he had asked, while knowing the question might have been several questions at once.

That night she dreamed of the hearing room as a beach. The witnesses stood at different distances from the water, each speaking in a voice that could not be heard above the tide. When she woke, she remembered not the words but the fact that she had been listening. The city’s questions remained. So did the people who had asked them.
