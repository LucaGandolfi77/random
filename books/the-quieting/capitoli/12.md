# Chapter 12 — The Open Record

The hearing lasted six hours. By the end, the council had not become wise. It had become less certain, which was a beginning.

The first vote suspended the risk-tier form. The second ordered an independent review of the concealed records, with patient representatives and privacy protections. The third renewed funding for the clinics, but tied it to voluntary care, paid leave, and a public report that would count long-term outcomes without reducing them to speed of return.

Sera was placed on leave pending review. Before leaving the chamber, she crossed to Mara.

“I wanted the work to survive,” she said.

“I know.”

“I should have trusted the work to survive the truth.”

Mara wished she could offer a sentence that repaired what the older woman had done. She did not. They stood together in the thin light from the high windows, two people who had wanted to protect the same thing and had confused protection with concealment.

Outside, the city had begun its ordinary evening. Buses sighed at the curb. A florist carried buckets inside. On the steps, Jonah waited with two cups of coffee.

“Is it over?” he asked.

“No.”

“Good. I’d hate to miss the next part.”

She laughed, then leaned her forehead against his. It was not a grand reunion. No music rose from the street. The city had not been healed by a vote, and neither of them had become unafraid. Jonah’s brother remained dead. Ada remained dead. Nell’s mother remained dead. The record could be opened; no archive could return the living.

“Do you want to come home with me?” Jonah asked.

Mara knew the question carried no promise of rescue. She answered anyway. “Yes.”

They walked toward the harbor. The tide had turned, and small waves climbed the steps before sliding back. At the seawall, Nell was showing her map to her aunt. A line ran from the clinic to the water, then doubled inland toward the old market.

“Does it go somewhere?” Jonah asked.

“Not yet,” Mara said.

“Does it have to?”

“No.”

They stood until the city lamps came on, one by one, not as a cure but as a way to see each other in the dark.
