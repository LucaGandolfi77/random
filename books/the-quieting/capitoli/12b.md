# Chapter 12b — The Archive’s Open Door

After the hearing, the archive opened a reading room for families who wished to review the records of relatives who had died. The procedure was slow and careful. A person could bring a support worker. They could stop at any point. They could request corrections to errors of fact, though not every disagreement could be resolved. The archive promised not to turn access into an obligation: no family had to read what had been written about someone they loved.

Jonah helped design the room. He insisted on ordinary chairs, good light, and a separate exit so a person would not have to pass the waiting area afterward. Mina said the room should have a kettle. Elias wanted a secure terminal for the digital records. The three of them argued about the position of the clock. In the end they put one on the wall and left the second hand intact.

The first person to use the room was a man whose wife had died twelve years earlier. He read for two hours, then asked Jonah whether the phrase “limited engagement” meant she had refused treatment. Jonah explained that it could mean several things, and that the note did not make the meaning clear. The man said he had spent a decade believing she had rejected help. He did not know whether the record changed that belief. He asked for a photocopy of the page and went outside alone.

Jonah watched through the glass until the man crossed the courtyard. He had expected access to be a kind of answer. Instead it had given the man another uncertainty to carry. The archive had not returned the dead; it had only changed the shape of what the living did not know.

Mina told Jonah this was still worth doing. “A closed file can become a false certainty,” she said. “An open one can give you the truth that it isn’t enough.”

He thought of the photograph of Tomas, sealed away, and of the words he had wanted to place beside it. He had wanted the archive to bear witness. He had not understood that witness could mean protecting what could not be shared.

That evening he told Mara about the man. She listened from the floor, where she was sorting old books. She had taken to doing ordinary tasks while Jonah spoke, perhaps because the hands needed something to do when the mind had no answer to offer.

“Do you regret opening it?” she asked.

“No. I regret thinking it would be simple.”

She put a book on the shelf. “Maybe that’s the work. Not making the truth simple enough to survive.”

He sat beside her. Their shoulders touched. The intimacy between them was not the absence of fear but the repeated discovery that fear had not made them strangers. He told her he missed Tomas. She told him she missed Ada. They did not compare the losses, or try to make them equivalent. For a while they sat among the books and let the names remain names.
