# Chapter 12c — The Pilot Room

The first pilot Room of Return opened in the harbor district, in a former ticket office with cracked blue tile and windows that faced the water. The city had removed the lock from the inner door. It had not yet removed the old sign instructing visitors to check in at reception, and the sign caused three people to turn away before the staff noticed.

Nell came to inspect the space with the map committee. She disliked the fluorescent lights and liked the broad sill where someone could sit without blocking the entrance. Mr. Daven tested the route from the bus stop. The library sent books that did not require concentration to follow. A local café agreed to provide tea, but asked not to be listed as a mental-health service. The distinction mattered to the owner: she wanted people to sit without making every cup an act of treatment.

The room’s first visitor was a woman who had been directed there by a nurse after a difficult appointment. She stood by the door for a long time. The staff member on duty, a counselor named Paz, did not call her inside. She said the room was open, and the woman could use it or keep walking. The woman sat on the sill and looked out at the harbor. She stayed for twelve minutes, then left without speaking.

The visitor count recorded one use. The staff note recorded no intervention. The patient advisory group objected that the two facts made the visit sound empty. They asked for a field that could record “came and sat” without requiring the person to be categorized as treated.

The registry engineers said the requested field would complicate the data model. Imani answered that human beings had already complicated it. She was surprised to hear herself speak so sharply. The engineer was not the enemy; he had been asked to make an old system accommodate a new idea without additional time. She softened the tone, not the request.

They added the field a week later. A small change, almost invisible to anyone outside the room. For the person who had sat there, it meant the record could acknowledge a presence without pretending it knew what that presence meant.

Mara visited on the first rainy afternoon. The harbor water was dark and ridged by wind. She sat with Nell on the sill. Neither spoke for several minutes. Mara thought of the rooms where she had learned to ask what a person wanted, and the rooms where a form had asked first. She wondered how often the difference was not the furniture but the willingness of the person in authority to let a silence remain undecided.

“Does it feel different?” Nell asked.

“What?”

“The room.”

Mara looked around. The chairs were ordinary. The kettle was too loud. Someone had left a stack of maps on the table, each one marked with a different route to the clinic.

“It feels like it’s trying,” she said.

Nell accepted the answer. Outside, a ferry eased away from the dock, leaving behind a line of foam that broke apart before it reached the wall.
