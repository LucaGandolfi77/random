# Chapter 12d — The Quarterly Review

The pilot’s first quarterly review lasted two days. On the first day, the registry team presented numbers: visits, waiting times, referrals, departures before assessment, follow-ups completed. On the second, patients described what the numbers had not captured.

A man said the room helped because he could leave without being asked why. A woman said the open door made her feel watched. A teenager said the staff had asked too many questions. Another said they had asked too few. One person wanted the clinic to call their family; another had spent years trying to stop the clinic from doing exactly that.

The city’s evaluator asked how the service could build a consistent standard from experiences that contradicted one another.

Mina answered, “You can make the standard about the choices you offer, not the feeling you require.”

The evaluator wrote the sentence down but did not promise to use it.

The review found that people who used the room were not, as a group, returning to work faster. Some were not working at all. A member of the finance committee called that evidence that the pilot had failed. Lio asked whether the city had measured whether their workplaces had changed. It had not. The question required another field and another source of information, which meant time and money the city had not allocated.

Imani presented the revised intake form. It included a line for the patient’s own goal and a line for what the service could not provide. The second line had been controversial among administrators, who feared it would make the clinic appear inadequate. Imani said people already knew when the clinic was inadequate; the form only determined whether the institution admitted it.

Mara watched Imani speak. She remembered the young clinician who had asked whether records should be made consistent, and felt tenderness toward her—not because Imani had become right, but because she was learning to stay answerable to what she did not know.

The council extended the pilot for another six months, with a budget for staff training and a patient review board. It did not fund all the requested night shifts. Lio’s workplace changes remained incomplete. The policy had improved the conditions for care without making care plentiful.

After the meeting, Jonah asked Mara whether the review felt like progress.

“It feels like a record of what happened.”

“Do you ever want to say more than that?”

“Of course.” She took his hand. “I want to say it was worth it. I want to say it wasn’t enough. I want to say those things without using one to silence the other.”

He looked toward the building, where the lights were still on in the meeting room. “Then say both.”

She did not speak. The words were already in the room, and neither required her to choose a single one.
