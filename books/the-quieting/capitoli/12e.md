# Chapter 12e — The Night Rota

The six-month staffing pilot ended with a spreadsheet and a meeting at which every participant had arrived tired. The second dispatcher on the harbor’s busiest nights had prevented two routing errors. The authority counted both as safety improvements. It also reported that the new shifts had increased overtime because there were not enough workers to fill them.

The union argued that the pilot showed the need to hire. The finance office argued that it showed the model was too expensive. Havel said the rota could not be made safe by rearranging the same exhausted people. Lio, who had returned to most of his duties, said he did not want his case used as the argument for either side.

The committee chair asked what Lio wanted the report to say.

“That the change helped,” he said. “That it didn’t fix the staffing shortage. That I still have bad nights. Don’t write that I’m back to normal. I was never sure what you meant by normal.”

The chair asked whether he considered himself recovered.

Lio looked at the table. He had been asked the question by clinicians, by managers, by his mother, and by himself. Each time he had tried to answer as if the word described one state. It described a succession of days, some easier, some not. The harbor had changed one procedure. His sleep had improved. He still sometimes woke thinking he had entered the wrong berth number.

“I’m doing better than I was,” he said. “I don’t know if that answers you.”

It did not answer the committee’s budget question. The pilot was extended for another quarter, not long enough to guarantee permanent positions. Lio’s shift remained adjusted. The report included his phrase, though he worried that the city would use “doing better” as proof it could stop investing.

Afterward, Havel offered him a permanent daytime role in training. It paid slightly less and would take him away from the dispatch room. Lio considered it for several days. He liked the harbor at night. He did not want to leave because someone had decided the night was too dangerous for him. He also did not want to keep proving that he could manage it.

He accepted the training role for six months, with a review date and the option to return to dispatch. The choice felt neither like retreat nor triumph. It was a job he had selected under conditions that were still imperfect.

At the clinic, he told Imani he had taken the offer. She asked whether he felt relieved.

“Some.”

“Regretful?”

“Some.”

“Do you want to talk about it?”

“Not today.”

Imani said all right. She did not add the refusal to a risk score. She made a note that Lio had chosen a follow-up in two weeks, then asked whether he needed the appointment card printed in large type. He said yes. The small request pleased him more than he expected. It was a question about what would make the next appointment possible, not about whether he was becoming a success.
