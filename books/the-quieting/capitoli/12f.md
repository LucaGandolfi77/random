# Chapter 12f — The First Complaint Read Aloud

The patient advisory board read one complaint aloud at the first public review of the new protocol. The writer had given permission, on the condition that no identifying details be included. The complaint concerned a room that had been closed during a fire drill, a clinician who had continued asking questions while the writer wanted to leave, and a form that marked the departure as “unable to complete assessment.”

The board had asked permission to read the complaint because the meeting’s agenda referred to early successes. The city’s report showed fewer missed appointments and shorter waiting times. The numbers were real. The complaint was also real, and the people who had written it had asked the city not to make the review sound like a celebration.

After the account was read, there was a long silence. The committee chair asked whether the board wanted to recommend a change. Mina said they wanted the record amended, the fire-drill procedure revised, and the staff retrained. They did not want the patient brought back to explain the injury again.

The chair said the service needed to understand the context. Mina answered that the patient had already supplied it. Requiring another account would make the person pay again for the institution’s understanding.

Imani watched the council staff write down the request. She knew the process would be slower than a press release and less satisfying than a verdict. The city would have to determine who could amend the clinical note, how to preserve the original entry, and how to record the patient’s account without turning the correction into a second dispute. The work was clerical, legal, and intimate in ways the meeting room did not acknowledge.

The board’s recommendation was accepted. The note was preserved with an addendum that the patient had disputed the description of their departure. The fire-drill procedure was changed so that visitors would receive notice when possible and be offered a choice of exits. Staff training included the difference between asking for consent and assuming consent because a person had entered the room.

The city report included the complaint and the changes made in response. It did not include the patient’s name, the date, or the language that might identify them to people who had been present. The account became part of the public record without becoming a public possession.

After the meeting, Imani asked Mina whether she thought the change was enough.

“No,” Mina said. “But enough is not the only reason to do it.”

Imani understood. The answer did not excuse incompletion. It refused the idea that one must wait for a perfect solution before making an imperfect correction. She wrote the sentence in her notebook, then put the notebook away. The work now required a phone call to the clinic, where someone was waiting for the new instructions to be explained.
