# Chapter 12g — The City Keeps Score

The first year’s public report arrived in a blue folder with a chart on the cover. The chart showed a reduction in average waiting time and a rise in patient-reported control over treatment choices. It also showed that the night clinic had missed its staffing target, that the new complaint procedure had produced more recorded disputes, and that the harbor accommodation pilot had not been extended to every department.

A council member said the increase in complaints suggested the revised service was making people less satisfied. Mina said it might also mean people had discovered a way to complain. A statistician said the data could not distinguish between those explanations. The room disliked the answer because it could not be converted into a slogan.

The report recorded both the improved and worsened measures. It did not rank them in a single score. The finance committee demanded an overall rating. Imani explained that the service could not responsibly assign one without deciding in advance which harms mattered most. The committee asked whether the public would understand. Imani said the public was already living with the consequences of decisions that had been simplified for them.

Lio attended the review as a worker representative. He reported that the second-person check had reduced ambiguity in berth changes, but the day shift created its own understaffing problem. He had moved into training and liked teaching new dispatchers, though he missed the night water and the sense of the harbor waking beyond the windows. He asked the city to include staff fatigue in the safety review rather than classifying it as an individual health concern.

The committee agreed to extend the pilot for one year and to publish a report on staffing across all shifts. It did not agree to permanent funding. The decision left the work open to the next budget fight.

Outside the meeting, a reporter asked Mara whether the service had succeeded. She said that depended on what one meant by success. The reporter asked if that was an evasion.

“No,” Mara said. “It’s a refusal to pretend the question is simpler than it is.”

She knew the answer would be framed as uncertainty, perhaps even weakness. She had spent years fearing uncertainty because it seemed to leave people unprotected. Now she understood that false certainty could be a more polished form of abandonment: it let institutions stop looking once they had named the result.

That evening, she read the report at home. Jonah was cooking; the sauce had begun to stick to the pan. She read the sections on complaints and waiting times aloud. He asked whether the report included the patient who had left the room after the fire alarm. It did, without a name. The service had changed the procedure and preserved the person’s refusal to participate further.

“Would they say it worked?” he asked.

“I don’t know if they would say that.”

“Would you?”

“I’d say the room changed. The person may still have had a bad experience.”

Jonah turned down the flame. “That’s not a satisfying answer.”

“It’s the one that keeps them both true.”

They ate the sauce, slightly burnt at the bottom. Neither called it a success. Neither threw it away.
