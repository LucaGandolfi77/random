# Chapter 12h — The Night the Line Stayed Open

The after-hours consultation line received its first call before the service had completed the training. A woman called because her partner had locked himself in the bathroom after an argument. She said he was crying and refusing to answer. She did not know whether she should call emergency services, break the door, or wait.

The clinician who answered had worked in crisis care for eight years. She asked if anyone was injured, whether there were weapons, whether the partner had threatened to harm himself or someone else. The woman answered no, no, and not that she knew. The clinician asked what the caller herself wanted. She said she wanted someone else to make the decision.

The line’s new protocol did not tell the clinician what to do. It gave her a senior consultant to call, a clear explanation of emergency thresholds, and the option to send a mobile team if the caller agreed. They spoke for nearly half an hour. The partner eventually opened the door. He was not in immediate danger, but he wanted the clinician to speak to him. The caller stayed in the hallway and was not asked to interpret his answers.

The next morning, the call appeared in the service log as a “resolved non-emergency.” The clinician objected to the label. The couple had not resolved their argument. The immediate uncertainty had changed; the underlying distress remained. The log was revised to say that the line had provided consultation and arranged follow-up.

At the weekly review, the city manager asked whether the call had been a success. The senior clinician said the caller had received help without an unnecessary emergency intervention, and the partner had agreed to follow-up. She also said the line might have failed if the consultant had not been available. The pilot had one trained senior person on call; the service needed a rota with more than one name.

Mara listened to the recording with consent from the clinician, not the callers. She was assessing the protocol, not the family. The conversation was full of pauses. The clinician asked the caller to describe the bathroom door, the sound of the fan, the place where she was standing. She did not instruct her to calm down. She helped her orient to the facts she could actually know.

Mara remembered the first Room of Return and the man waiting for a bus with tea in his hands. The scale had changed. The essential act had not: do not assume the feeling tells you everything; do not assume the feeling tells you nothing. Ask what is happening, what the person wants, what risk is immediate, what support exists, and what remains uncertain.

The service hired two more clinicians for the line, though one position stayed vacant. The city described this as a successful pilot. Mara did not object to the phrase. She asked that the report include the staffing gap beside it.

The call had gone well. The system had been short-staffed. Both belonged in the same account.
