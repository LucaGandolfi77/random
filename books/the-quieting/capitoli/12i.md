# Chapter 12i — The Last Follow-Up

Tavi’s final scheduled follow-up was not called a discharge. Dr. Chen asked whether she wanted to continue appointments or return if new concerns arose. Tavi said she wanted one more check in three months, then no standing appointment. She had improved in sleep and concentration after reviewing the medication. She still had occasional word substitutions, usually when tired. They had not worsened. The assessment did not determine whether they had been caused by the fire, the medication, sleep loss, anxiety, or a combination.

Tavi asked Chen whether she should be worried.

“I think you should notice changes and seek care if they affect you,” Chen said. “I don’t think you need to treat every lapse as evidence of decline.”

“Can you say that in the letter?”

Chen wrote: *Current findings do not establish a progressive cognitive condition. Continue ordinary medical follow-up as needed; return for reassessment if symptoms change.* Tavi read the line and asked that “ordinary” be removed. It implied that she had to know what counted as ordinary. Chen changed it to *routine medical follow-up*.

The distinction mattered to Tavi. She did not want the clinic’s uncertainty to become a new standard she had to monitor alone. She wanted a clear description of what the assessment did and did not show, and a way back if the situation changed.

At the last appointment, she told Mara that the fire still frightened her. She had not returned to the apartment’s stairwell after dark. The building had fixed the ramp and changed the evacuation notice. Luca had stopped testing her memory at breakfast, though he still sometimes asked if she wanted help after a difficult night. Their agreement held imperfectly. Tavi had told him when she was upset; he had listened without immediately searching for a solution.

“Do you feel better?” Mara asked.

“Some days. Other days I feel like myself, which is not always the same thing.”

Mara understood. Tavi was not a conclusion. Her symptoms had changed, her life had changed, and the question of cause remained open. The care had not made uncertainty disappear. It had made uncertainty less solitary and less easily used against her.

At reception, Tavi asked for copies of her notes. The clerk printed them and placed them in a folder. Tavi checked the pages, then asked that one entry be corrected: it said she had “declined family support,” though she had declined contact with her partner during the first appointment. The clerk changed the wording through the formal amendment process.

When Tavi left the clinic, she carried the folder under her arm. She had not been cured of fear, and the service had not cured itself through her case. She had a record she could read, a doctor she could return to, a home whose alarm plan had improved, and the right not to turn any of it into a story for strangers. The future remained open, which was not the same as being safe from it. It was, for now, a future she could enter.
