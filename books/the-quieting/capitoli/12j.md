# Chapter 12j — The Forecast

The service’s annual forecast predicted that the new after-hours line would prevent a number of emergency admissions. The prediction was based on a model built from calls, referral patterns, and historical outcomes. It was presented to the council as a reason to fund another clinician.

Mara read the report and found that the model treated all calls that ended without emergency transport as successful de-escalations. The line’s first year included callers who had chosen not to go to hospital, callers who had needed a hospital but could not reach one, and callers whose situation had changed because a friend arrived. The model had counted them together.

The analyst said this was standard practice. The categories were determined by the available data. Mara asked whether the report could distinguish a person choosing not to go from a person unable to go. The analyst said not with the current records.

The city had asked for a forecast before it funded the staffing to improve the records. It wanted a confident estimate of savings based on an incomplete system. Mara recognized the old bargain: simplify the evidence now, promise to make it accurate later, and hope the service survived long enough to fulfill the promise.

She asked Imani and the patient advisers to review the wording. They recommended that the forecast be described as provisional and that the report state plainly what the model could not distinguish. The finance office objected that the caveat weakened the case for funding. Imani replied that a prediction without its limits was not a stronger case; it was a more fragile one.

The committee funded one additional clinician, not the two requested. The report included the caveat and a commitment to improve the call outcomes. It also projected cost savings that could not yet be confirmed. The city had moved forward and kept some of its old habits.

Mara wondered whether the system could ever become honest without becoming impossible to fund. Every public service had to translate human needs into budgets. Translation altered what was said. The answer could not be to refuse all translation, since refusal left the figures to those least troubled by what was lost. It had to be to show the seams: who had chosen the categories, what they could measure, and which lives they made difficult to count.

At the next meeting, the analyst brought a revised model. It separated “no transport requested,” “transport unavailable,” and “situation resolved before arrival,” though the categories still did not capture every circumstance. Mara thanked him. He said he had not expected the work to become a discussion of language.

“It was always a discussion of language,” she said. “We’re only making the language visible.”

He looked at the chart, then nodded. The line of numbers remained. Beside it, for the first time, stood an account of what the line left out.
