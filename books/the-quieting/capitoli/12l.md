# Chapter 12l — The Annual Audit

The first annual audit under the new protocol found that most clinicians were following the consent requirements. It also found that patients were rarely given a copy of their contact preference, that family calls were still more likely to be documented than refused calls, and that the after-hours line had no consistent way to record when a caller had been unable to get through.

The report described these as implementation gaps. Mina objected to the phrase. A gap sounded like an empty space in a plan. These were actions that had affected people. The audit team agreed to use “unmet requirements” instead.

The director asked whether the wording mattered if the corrections were made. Mina said words shaped whether the institution understood itself as having forgotten a task or having failed a person. The distinction would not restore anyone’s trust, but it could change what the staff looked for next time.

The clinic introduced a printed copy of each patient’s contact preference, a log for unanswered calls, and a quarterly review of family-contact decisions. The changes required additional administrative time. The council funded a clerk for six months. After six months, the position would be reviewed. Everyone understood that a temporary resource could disappear as soon as the numbers looked better.

Tavi attended the audit meeting as a community adviser. She did not discuss her own case. She asked whether the new follow-up form would make it possible to state “contact me once, then wait.” The director said yes. Tavi asked whether that instruction would be visible to after-hours staff. The answer was no; it appeared only in the daytime record. The audit group added a field to the night system.

The change took three weeks to implement. During that time, one caller received two follow-up calls after requesting one. The service apologized and amended the record. It did not call the caller again.

Mara read the incident report. The error was smaller than Ada’s missed follow-up and still made her stomach tighten. She wanted to believe that reforms would prevent repetition; she had to accept that a rule could be improved and violated in the same month. The purpose of an audit was not to declare the service safe. It was to discover where the promise had failed while there was still time to alter what happened next.

At the next staff meeting, the director read the report aloud. No one applauded. Amel said she was tired of every mistake becoming evidence that the service should not exist. Mina said the people harmed by the mistakes were tired of being asked to prove the service should exist. The room held both statements without resolving them.

The director asked what they would do. They reviewed the call process, changed the night screen, and agreed to check the field again in three months. It was not a satisfying ending. It was a procedure that would have to be repeated.
