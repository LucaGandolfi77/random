# Chapter 12m — The Clinician on the Other Side

After a night shift, Mara received a call from Dr. Chen. Chen had been involved in a bicycle collision on her way home and was physically unharmed, but she had begun shaking once she reached her apartment. She asked whether Mara could speak with her as a colleague, not as a clinician.

Mara said yes, then noticed how quickly the old roles tried to arrange themselves. She asked whether Chen was safe, whether she needed medical attention, whether someone was with her. Chen answered. She had spoken with an urgent-care doctor, called her sister, and was sitting at home with the door unlocked. She did not want a psychological assessment. She wanted to tell someone that she had been afraid and that the fear had embarrassed her.

Mara listened. She did not offer a breathing exercise. Chen had taught Mara that care could begin by asking what the person wanted; it was easy to remember the lesson when the other person was a patient. It was harder when the person was a colleague whose steadiness Mara had relied on.

“I thought I’d be better at this,” Chen said. “I know what panic is.”

“Knowing what it is doesn’t mean you don’t feel it.”

“I know that too.”

They both laughed, a little. Chen said the collision had reminded her of a patient whose care she had once mishandled. She had not been able to sleep for much of the night afterward. The memory did not prove she was unfit to work. It did mean she needed to discuss the old case in supervision rather than keep carrying it alone.

Mara asked if she wanted help finding someone to cover her morning appointments. Chen said yes. Mara made the call. She did not ask Chen for more details about the collision or the old patient.

Afterward, Mara sat with the phone on the table. She understood that clinicians could be both knowledgeable and frightened, that expertise gave no exemption from needing another person. The city’s reverence for psychologists and psychiatrists had sometimes turned them into public instruments, as if their knowledge meant they should function without remainder. The clinicians themselves had helped sustain that illusion by presenting care as a skill they could provide without needing it.

At the next staff meeting, Chen told the team she had taken a day off after the collision. She did not describe the patient or the details. She said the service needed a coverage plan that did not depend on clinicians being invulnerable. The director agreed to revise the rota so staff could ask for relief without disclosing private medical information to their manager.

It was a small policy change born from a private fear. Mara wondered how many changes began this way, with someone discovering that the rule they had designed for others did not fit the body they lived in.
