# Chapter 14 — A Room for Two

In spring, Jonah left a toothbrush at Mara’s apartment. He did not announce the act. She found it in the glass beside her own and felt, first, tenderness; then alarm; then irritation at the alarm for arriving so quickly.

She had spent years imagining intimacy as a room one entered only after the danger had passed. But danger did not pass in the clean way she had expected. Ada’s absence remained. Jonah’s grief for Tomas remained. Mara still sometimes woke from dreams with the sense that she had forgotten an appointment on which someone’s life depended. If she waited for a future without those things, she would be waiting for a world that did not exist.

She told Jonah the toothbrush frightened her.

“Because you think I’m moving in?”

“Because I thought I’d feel only happy.”

He leaned on the kitchen counter. “That sounds like a very demanding form of happiness.”

“I know.”

He did not offer to take the toothbrush away. He asked whether she wanted him to. The question itself loosened something in her chest. They talked about the difference between a guest and a resident, about keys, about nights when either of them might want solitude. It was an awkward conversation, and because it was awkward they trusted it more than they would have trusted an effortless promise.

Later they ate soup at the table. Jonah told her he had been afraid that loving Mara again would mean returning to the years when he measured every silence for signs that she was leaving. Mara told him she was afraid that being loved would become another duty at which she could fail. They did not solve the fears. They learned their outlines.

When Jonah kissed her, she let herself attend to the kiss without asking what it predicted. There was warmth, a faint taste of coffee, the pressure of his thumb near her wrist. Her mind attempted to turn sensation into evidence, then drifted away from the task. The moment did not promise that they would never hurt each other. It promised only that, now, neither was pretending not to be there.

In the morning the toothbrush remained in its glass. Mara looked at it while brushing her teeth. She thought of Proust’s long patience with time, with the way a present moment could contain a vanished one without becoming identical to it. The thought did not make her feel wiser. It made the morning larger, filled with more than its own small actions.

She went to work. Jonah went to the archive. In the evening they would decide whether to cook or order food. A life was not made intimate by a single declaration, she began to understand, but by the repeated permission to be ordinary beside another person.
