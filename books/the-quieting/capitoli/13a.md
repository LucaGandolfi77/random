# Chapter 13a — Two Keys

Jonah gave Mara a key in June. He did not make a ceremony of it. He placed it beside the sugar bowl while she was cutting bread and said, “This is for the archive office. I keep forgetting mine.”

Mara looked at the key. It was not a key to his apartment, and she knew that was why he had chosen it. The gift offered intimacy while leaving her the task of deciding what intimacy meant.

She asked whether he wanted her to have a key to his home.

“I do,” he said. “I didn’t want to make you feel as if saying yes to this meant saying yes to everything.”

The care in the distinction was touching, and it also made her tired. She was tired of being protected from the implications of her own choices. “You can ask me directly,” she said. “I might still be frightened. But let me be frightened in the room where the question is.”

Jonah apologized. He explained that he had learned, after Tomas’s death, to offer people only what they could accept without feeling trapped. He had mistaken her uncertainty for a request to be spared the question. Mara said she had learned to treat every offered need as another obligation she might fail. They had each built a method of care out of the part of grief they understood, and each method had sometimes kept the other at a distance.

Jonah took a key from his pocket. It was old, scratched, and belonged to the apartment he rented above the archive. He put it on the table beside the other one.

“You can have it,” he said. “You can also not use it.”

Mara did not take it immediately. She watched the afternoon light move across the metal. The key appeared to contain a promise, but she knew the promise was not that nothing would change. It was that a door could open without demanding that she be certain about every room beyond it.

She took the key.

That evening she went to Jonah’s apartment while he was still at work. She let herself in and stood in the hallway, listening. There was a radiator knocking, a clock, the neighbor’s radio playing through a wall. The room smelled faintly of dust and coffee. She felt the old impulse to search for signs of permanence, then understood that she was alone and did not have to decide anything about the future before Jonah came home.

She put the key on the table, beside a bowl of pears. When Jonah arrived, they cooked badly, argued about the salt, and ate at the window. Later he asked whether she wanted to stay. She said yes, not because the answer was easy but because she had made it in the present, with the whole of her uncertainty included.
