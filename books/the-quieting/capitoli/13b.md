# Chapter 13b — The Guest Room

Jonah had a small guest room with a narrow bed and a shelf of books he had not read since Tomas died. He had called it a guest room because “the room where I keep things I cannot decide about” sounded too candid for a rental agreement.

Mara stayed there the night after the quarterly review. Jonah had asked if she wanted to sleep beside him; she had said she wanted to be near him but not held. He said the guest room was hers. She did not want the offer to become a test of whether she could accept care, so she accepted it without explaining herself.

At two in the morning she woke with a sentence in her mind: *I should have called.* It was not new. It was the old Sunday with Ada, returning as if time had been waiting for a moment when Mara was too tired to defend herself. She sat up. The guest room smelled of paper and cedar. Through the wall she heard Jonah move, then stop.

He did not come in. A minute later he asked from the hallway, “Do you want company?”

“Yes,” she said.

He sat in the chair by the door. He did not ask what had happened. Mara told him. She said she had begun to fear that her whole life was a series of missed opportunities disguised afterward as unavoidable choices. Jonah listened, then said he had spent years imagining that if he had taken Tomas to a different doctor, or called one more time, his brother might still be alive.

“Do you believe that?” Mara asked.

“I believe I can imagine it. I don’t know if I believe it.”

She felt the difference. Imagination made a road and then punished the traveler for not having taken it, even when the road had not existed at the time. The mind could create an alternate past with the ease of drawing a line across a map. It could not prove the line would have led anywhere safe.

Mara asked whether Jonah thought they were using one another’s grief as a place to meet.

“Sometimes,” he said. “But not only that.”

“Does that worry you?”

“Yes.”

They sat quietly. He did not move closer until she held out her hand. When he took it, the contact was light enough to leave her choice intact. The sentence about Ada remained in her mind. It did not grow smaller. It ceased, briefly, to be the only sentence.

In the morning, Mara made coffee. Jonah found her in the kitchen reading the spine of a book without opening it. He asked whether she wanted breakfast. She said yes. They ate toast at the counter, discussing a broken lamp and a letter from the archive. Nothing was resolved. The ordinary morning did not correct the night; it existed beside it, which was not nothing.
