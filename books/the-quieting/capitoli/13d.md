# Chapter 13d — The Door Between Homes

Mara’s mother asked if Jonah would help move the cabinet from the upstairs room. The request was practical, though the cabinet had been in the house since before Mara was born and moving it meant admitting that the house was changing around the body of the person who remained.

Jonah came on a Saturday. Mara’s mother gave instructions from the doorway while he and Mara lifted the cabinet down the stairs. They had to stop twice. The wood caught on the railing; Mara’s mother said to tilt it left; Jonah said there was no left once they were halfway down. In the end they set it in the front room, where the light was better and the doors opened without catching.

Afterward, the three of them drank tea. Her mother asked Jonah whether he intended to keep seeing Mara. The question was so blunt that Mara nearly choked. Jonah said he hoped to. Her mother asked whether he understood that Mara sometimes needed to be alone. He said he did, then added that he did not always understand it in the moment.

“Neither do I,” Mara said.

Her mother seemed pleased by this answer. She had never liked men who claimed to understand women completely. The conversation moved to the weather, the cabinet, and whether the neighbor’s dog had begun to recognize the postman.

On the drive home, Jonah asked whether the questions had embarrassed her.

“A little.”

“Did you want me to answer differently?”

“I wanted to know what you actually thought.”

“I do want to stay in your life. I don’t know what form that will take.”

Mara watched the road. The sentence produced both relief and sadness. She had wanted love to become a room with a clear entrance; instead it seemed to be a house with several doors, some belonging to her mother, some to Jonah, some to the life she had not yet decided to build. The door could be open without being an invitation to occupy every room.

At Jonah’s apartment she used her key. Her scarf was on the shelf, her book on the chair. These objects no longer looked like the beginning of an irreversible claim. They looked like the evidence of visits that had happened and could happen again.

She thought of Tolstoy’s attention to the daily arrangements by which a life declared itself—not through its grand intentions but through the plate washed, the chair moved, the question asked at the wrong time and then asked again more gently. She did not quote the thought aloud. She wanted it to remain an observation, not a promise.

That night she and Jonah made a list of repairs the apartment needed. They argued about the order. The list was not a plan for forever. It was a plan for Saturday.
