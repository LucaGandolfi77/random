# Chapter 13e — The Evening They Did Not Discuss the Future

On a warm evening in August, Jonah and Mara walked to the harbor without discussing the future. They had agreed, after several conversations, not to make every quiet hour an opportunity to review the state of their relationship. The agreement lasted until the harbor bells rang, when Jonah asked whether she had ever imagined having children.

Mara had not. Or she had, briefly, in the way one imagines lives one is not sure one wants. She said she had no answer that had survived the question for long. Jonah said he had once assumed he would have a family, then stopped assuming after Tomas died. Neither knew whether the assumption had disappeared because of grief, circumstance, or a choice they had not made consciously.

The conversation began to acquire the old weight of a decision. Mara felt herself preparing to say something definitive so Jonah would not have to wait. She recognized the reflex and stopped.

“I don’t want to decide tonight,” she said.

“I wasn’t asking you to.”

“I know. I’m telling you what happens in me when we talk about it.”

Jonah said he sometimes heard uncertainty as a quiet refusal, because he had spent years trying not to ask for more than Mara could give. He did not want to make her responsible for that fear. He also did not want to pretend that the future was irrelevant to him.

They sat on the seawall. The water carried the lights in broken lines. Mara thought of Proust, whose long attention to time made it possible to see how a present could hold the pressure of what had gone before without being determined by it. She had once wanted memory to teach her what to do. Now she understood that memory could illuminate a question and still leave it unanswered.

“I love you,” she said. “I can say that without knowing every form it will take.”

Jonah looked at her. “I love you too. I can say that without asking it to answer the other questions.”

They kissed, then watched a ferry come in. No resolution followed. The evening did not become a promise, and neither of them needed it to. They went home separately, as they had planned, because Mara wanted to sleep in her own apartment and Jonah wanted to finish the archive report. On the way she felt both the sadness of leaving and the relief of choosing to leave.

The next morning she called him. They spoke about a missing citation in the report and the weather. The love remained. So did the unmade decisions. For once, the presence of the second fact did not invalidate the first.
