# Chapter 13f — The Name on the Lease

When Jonah’s lease came up for renewal, he asked Mara whether she wanted her name added. He did not phrase it as a test. He did not say that the landlord needed an answer that week, though the landlord did. He told her the practical facts, then asked what the question brought up for her.

It brought up a memory of the apartment they had nearly shared, the brick wall that turned pink at sunset, the months in which they had deferred the decision until the future seemed to make it for them. It brought up the fear that living together would expose them to an everyday intimacy they could not retreat from. It brought up the possibility that retreat had been what she was protecting all along.

Mara said she did not want to move in yet. She wanted to keep her own apartment and stay at Jonah’s several nights a week. She wanted them to revisit the question in six months. She feared the arrangement sounded like a refusal disguised as a schedule.

Jonah listened. “I would like to live with you,” he said. “I don’t want you to agree before you want it. I also don’t want to pretend I have no preference.”

The honesty was harder than unconditional patience. It gave Mara something to answer. She said she understood, and that she wanted him to keep wanting it even if she was not ready now. He said he could do that, as long as they continued to speak honestly about what waiting cost him.

They did not sign a lease together. Jonah renewed his apartment. Mara kept hers. On the day he received the new keys, he gave her one without a speech. She placed it on her ring with her own key, then carried it home in her pocket.

That evening they moved a bookshelf away from the window because the paint had begun to peel behind it. The task took longer than expected. They found a postcard from Tomas wedged at the back, a receipt from a dinner they had shared before the separation, and a folded note in Mara’s handwriting listing things she meant to discuss with Jonah. The note was dated a week before he left. She did not remember writing it.

She asked whether he wanted to read it. He said yes, then changed his mind. They put the note in the archive box that held their shared history, a box neither had agreed to create but both had kept adding to.

The room looked bare without the shelf. Jonah asked if she wanted to put it back. Mara said they could decide tomorrow. They ate sitting on the floor among the books, laughing at the inconvenience and the cold tea.

The lease was not a metaphor, though they kept trying to make it one. It was a document with an end date and a fee for late renewal. Mara found comfort in its practical limits. It gave their feelings a question that could wait six months without becoming eternal.
