# Chapter 13g — The Orchard in September

Mara and Jonah returned to the island orchard in September. The pear trees had begun to drop fruit. Some pears were bruised, some split open at the stem, and several had been taken by birds. Jonah picked one from the ground and asked if she wanted it. Mara said not that one. He asked what was wrong with it. She said she did not know; it looked like it had already begun to belong to the earth.

He laughed. “You’ve become a poet.”

“No. I’ve become cautious about fruit.”

They walked the paths where the trees had been planted from cuttings saved after storms. Jonah told her the archive had received a box of Tomas’s school essays. Mina had found them in a cupboard and asked whether they should be kept. One essay described the orchard’s first trees, though Tomas had written it at twelve, before the city had opened the place to the public.

Mara asked whether Jonah had read it. He said yes. Tomas had complained that the trees were planted in rows and therefore looked “like children lined up for inspection.” The sentence had made Jonah laugh, then cry, then feel embarrassed by both responses. The essay contained the boy he remembered and a boy he had not known: observant, impatient, already suspicious of anything arranged too neatly.

“Will you put it in the archive?” Mara asked.

“If Mina agrees. Maybe it should stay with her.”

“You don’t have to decide what it means because it mentions this place.”

“I know. I just keep thinking that an archive is a way of refusing disappearance.”

“Sometimes. It can also make a thing visible in a way the person never chose.”

He nodded. They had said versions of the same thing many times, but the repetition was not empty. Each time it arrived from a different direction, shaped by a new decision.

They sat beneath a tree. Mara remembered the orchard as it had been when she first came with Jonah: pears, Proust’s book, the taste of grief suddenly returned. She had believed the memory itself had changed her. Now she suspected she had been changed in small ways before she could name them, and the orchard had merely given those changes a scene in which to appear.

Jonah rested his hand on hers. They were not living together. They were not engaged, nor were they waiting for a future moment when their love would become free of uncertainty. They had begun to speak of an apartment they might share when her mother no longer needed the ground-floor room, but no one had set a date. The future had acquired a shape without becoming an obligation.

On the path back, Mara picked up a fallen pear. It was bruised but not split. She put it in her bag. At home, she cut it open. The flesh was sweet near the core and tart at the skin. Nothing returned exactly. Something answered.
