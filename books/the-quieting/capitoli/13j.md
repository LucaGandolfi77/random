# Chapter 13j — The Shared Shelf

The shelf in the new flat did not hold the books in any order. Jonah put the archive references together. Mara arranged her novels by height, then gave up when the books slid sideways. Proust leaned against a volume of city plans; Tolstoy sat between a book on clinical ethics and a manual for repairing the print shop’s old press. Dostoevsky occupied the only shelf with enough depth for the larger editions.

They argued about whether the books should be sorted. Jonah wanted to find them. Mara wanted to see them. The disagreement grew into a conversation about the difference between living together and merging. Jonah said he had imagined that sharing a home would make their separate histories feel less separate. Mara said she did not want the shelves to become a proof that they had successfully combined their lives.

“I want a place for my books,” he said.

“You have several.”

“I mean a place where I don’t have to ask whether they’re in the way.”

The sentence struck her as a small version of a larger fear. She had been so concerned with preserving her independence that she had made Jonah’s needs sound like a threat. He had been so careful not to impose that he sometimes withheld the request until it arrived as resentment.

They cleared one shelf. Jonah put his books there. Mara left the rest of the bookcase unsettled. The empty space beside his row was not a message; it was simply room.

The discussion continued while they cooked. Mara said she did not want to become responsible for managing Jonah’s grief. He said he did not want her to become responsible for managing his grief either. He wanted to be allowed to miss Tomas without having to make the feeling instructive. She wanted to be allowed to miss Ada without fearing that every tear would become a crisis or every silence an avoidance.

They had spoken about these things before, but living together changed the scale. Grief no longer arrived as a subject on a particular evening; it entered through objects, a book left open, a coat on a chair, a date circled on the calendar. They could not schedule all of it, and neither wanted to try.

After dinner, Jonah placed a book on the shared shelf. Mara put another beside it. They did not sort them by author or subject. In the morning, she found one had fallen forward and opened to a page marked by an old receipt. The receipt belonged to a bakery that had closed. Neither remembered when they had been there.

Mara left the book open. The shelf could remain partly ordered and partly accidental. It did not need to tell the story of their relationship. It only needed to hold the books they had chosen to bring.
