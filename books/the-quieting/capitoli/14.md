# Chapter 13 — The First Winter After

The first winter after the council vote was colder than the city had predicted. The sea wind came through the old window frames, and the clinics ran out of blankets for the waiting rooms. The new policy had not yet become a habit. Staff still used the old risk form when the registry crashed, which it did twice in December. One administrator argued that the temporary form was harmless. Imani asked whose definition of harmless they were using.

The patient advisory group met every other week. It was not harmonious. Some members wanted the city to record every symptom because invisibility had kept them from receiving care. Others feared that the same record would be used by employers, schools, or families who believed concern gave them a right to know. Mina attended, as did Lio, who said little until the question of return-to-work measures arose.

“Stop measuring recovery by the day somebody gets back behind a desk,” he said. “Measure whether the workplace changed what made them sick.”

The labor representative objected that the clinic could not evaluate every employer. Lio replied that the clinic could at least stop calling the patient’s return a success when the shift was still unsafe. The meeting ran late. No one left satisfied. They agreed to add a question about working conditions to the review form and revisit the issue in three months.

Nell’s map went up at the library, printed in a larger format. The town council removed two benches during construction and promised to replace them. Nell wrote to the council herself. She did not ask Mara to edit the letter. It was blunt, correctly addressed, and contained one sentence that made Mara laugh: *A bench that exists only in the minutes is not somewhere a person can sit.*

Mara and Jonah spent one weekend in January at her mother’s house, fixing the back door. They worked badly together at first. Jonah held the frame while Mara read the instructions. They switched roles, then switched back. Her mother made tea and criticized the screws. For a brief hour, the three of them became a household in the old sense: not a place without sorrow, but a place where sorrow had to share the table with tools and a kettle.

That evening, Mara found her mother asleep in the chair. The lamplight lay across her face, deepening the lines around her mouth. Mara remembered being a girl and watching her mother sleep after the funeral, afraid that if she woke her the family would have to resume being a family. Now she placed a blanket over her and let the old memory coexist with the new one.

On the train home Jonah rested his head against the window. Mara watched the dark countryside pass, each lit house briefly visible and then gone. She thought of the way memory made a life seem continuous after the fact, smoothing over the stretches where one had only endured the next hour. Perhaps this, too, was a kind of narrative mercy: not that suffering had a design, but that the living could look back and discover they had been accompanied by more than they knew.
