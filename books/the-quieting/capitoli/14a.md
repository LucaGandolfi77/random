# Chapter 14a — The Day Tomas Was Born

Jonah visited the cemetery on Tomas’s birthday. He had not gone every year. In the early years he had believed that the date required a particular action: flowers, a visit, a period of remembrance solemn enough to match the loss. When the ritual failed to make the day bearable, he had stopped going and felt guilty for the relief.

This year he brought no flowers. He brought the notebook, though he did not open it. The grave was marked with Tomas’s name and two dates, a fact so complete that it seemed almost rude. A person had been more than the space between those numbers, and yet the stone had no room for anything else.

Mina joined him. She had known Tomas before the loss, and had once left the city without telling Jonah. She did not apologize for it. She had needed to leave, she said, and Tomas had known where to find her if he wanted. Jonah had spent years imagining that everyone who loved his brother had owed him a report. He was beginning to see how grief could turn the bereaved into a kind of authority, granting them rights they might not have earned.

They spoke about the ferry sounds in Tomas’s notebook, about the terrible film he had insisted they watch twice, about a day he had been kind to a stranger and then denied it because he did not want kindness counted as a virtue. Mina remembered Tomas’s impatience with stories in which suffering made a person more beautiful.

“He’d have hated the way we talk about him now,” she said.

“Probably.”

“Would you rather not talk about him?”

“No. I just don’t want every memory to be evidence.”

Mina understood. They sat for a while without speaking. Jonah thought of Dostoevsky’s insistence on the moral entanglement of human lives, and of how easily a large moral truth could be used to demand too much from one person. He had believed that responsibility meant carrying Tomas’s story wherever it could do good. Now he wondered whether responsibility also meant accepting that Tomas’s life belonged partly to the silence around it.

That evening Jonah told Mara where he had been. She asked whether it had helped. He said he did not know. She asked if he wanted to talk about Tomas. He said yes, then told her about the film. Mara listened as if the story mattered for no reason beyond the fact that it had happened.

When he finished, they sat together. Jonah felt no release, no transformed grief. He felt tired, and loved, and still bereaved. The combination was not elegant, but it was the day he had been given.
