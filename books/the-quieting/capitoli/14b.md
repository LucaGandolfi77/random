# Chapter 14b — The Second Sunday

At half past four on the second Sunday of March, Mara, her mother, Nell, and Reva sat around the old kitchen table. No one had planned a gathering. Mara’s mother had invited her for soup; Nell had come to collect a book of Ilyana’s maps; Reva had driven her and stayed because the rain was heavy.

The table was too small for four adults and the maps. One sheet slid beneath the soup pot and acquired a damp circle. Nell rescued it, dried it with a dish towel, and scolded everyone for placing things on top of the paper. Mara’s mother laughed. It was the first time Mara had heard her laugh without immediately wondering whether she would begin to cry.

They spoke about Ilyana. Reva told a story about the sisters getting lost on a walk because Ilyana had insisted that a lane “must go somewhere interesting.” It ended at a fence. Ilyana had climbed over; Reva had walked back the way they came. They had argued for years about which one of them had chosen the sensible route.

Nell asked which one was right.

“Neither,” Reva said. “We both got home.”

Mara’s mother said the same thing had happened to her and Ada in a department store, though the girls had been children and the store had been closing. They had found one another by the escalators. Ada had pretended she had never been afraid. Mara remembered being angry that her sister could lie so convincingly, then, years later, learning that children often mistook silence for courage because the adults around them needed to believe it.

The conversation did not become a memorial service. They ate soup, passed bread, corrected each other’s memories. Nell asked if she could copy one of Ilyana’s routes for the private archive. Reva said yes, then asked that the original remain with the family. Mara’s mother offered to scan it. They negotiated the question without making any one person the owner of the whole story.

When Nell left, she carried the copied map in a folder. Reva went with her to the bus stop. Mara and her mother remained at the table, collecting bowls.

“I used to think a family stayed together because someone remembered everything,” her mother said.

“And now?”

“Now I think we stay because we keep making room for what we forgot.”

Mara looked at the damp ring on the paper, at the route that disappeared beneath it and reappeared on the other side. She thought of Proust, of time held in objects and tastes; then she let the thought pass. Her mother was not a literary illustration. She was a woman rinsing bowls, tired from the stairs, alive beside her daughter.

Outside, Reva and Nell reached the bus stop. Through the window Mara saw Nell unfolding the map to show her aunt something. They leaned together beneath the shelter while the rain moved over the street in fine silver lines.
