# Chapter 14c — Leona Comes in March

Leona came to Bellwether in March, as she had promised. She told her father she would arrive at noon and leave by four. Rusk asked if she wanted lunch. She said yes, provided he did not invite anyone else.

They met at a small restaurant near the station. Rusk had chosen it because it was quiet. Leona asked if he had chosen it because he thought she could not manage noise. He admitted he had. She said she appreciated the thought and did not want him making that choice for her next time.

For the first half hour they spoke about her work. She had designed a public housing project with a courtyard that flooded whenever the drains were blocked. Rusk asked how the city planned to fix it. Leona said the city had not planned to fix it; the maintenance budget had been cut. He recognized the argument and almost turned it into a discussion of municipal policy. Then he stopped himself and asked if she wanted to talk about the project or something else.

“Something else,” she said. “I want you to know I remember what happened when I was seventeen. I also remember you bringing me tea every morning when I wouldn’t leave my room.”

He looked down. “I didn’t know what else to do.”

“You kept asking if I was ready to go back to school. I wanted you to sit down and stop asking.”

“I thought if I stopped asking, you’d think I’d given up.”

“I thought if I answered, you’d turn it into a plan.”

They sat in the restaurant while the server poured water. Neither version canceled the other. Rusk had been frightened and loving; Leona had been ill and still entitled to anger. They spoke about the crisis team, the psychiatrist, the months of treatment. Leona said the service had helped her and had sometimes made her feel watched. Rusk said he had believed the watchfulness meant protection.

“I know,” she said. “That’s why I’m telling you, not asking you to feel guilty forever.”

He did not know what to say. She did not rescue him from the silence. They finished lunch and walked to the station. At the platform, Rusk asked when she would come again. Leona said she did not know, and that the next visit would not be a test of whether they had repaired everything.

“I understand,” he said.

She looked at him. “Do you?”

“Not completely.”

“Good. Then we can start there.”

The train arrived. Rusk watched her board, but this time he did not call after her with another question. He waited until the doors closed, then went back to the council building. The meeting on the amended protocol began in an hour. He had no new certainty to bring it. He had, perhaps, a better understanding of the cost of pretending that love gave him the right to decide.
