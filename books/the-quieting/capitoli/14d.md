# Chapter 14d — The Box Marked Keep

Mara and her mother spent another afternoon sorting papers. The box they labeled KEEP was smaller than the box they had begun with and heavier than either expected. It contained the letters from Mara’s father, Ada’s maps, two photographs whose dates they disagreed about, and the school report in which Ada had been described as “highly imaginative, occasionally disruptive.”

Her mother wanted to discard the report. Mara wanted to keep it because she remembered the teacher and the year. They argued about whether keeping it meant preserving the school’s judgment or preserving evidence that Ada had been a child before she became a patient. The argument was not about paper. It was about who had the right to define the terms by which a life could be remembered.

In the end, they kept the report with a note in the margin: *This is how the school described her. It is not the whole of her.* Her mother wrote the note. Mara recognized that her mother had always known more than Mara credited her with; the difficulty was that knowledge did not prevent her from making mistakes, and mistakes had often become the only part Mara could see.

They found a photograph of Ada at sixteen, standing beside the sea wall with one shoe untied. She was looking away from the camera, toward something beyond the frame. Mara tried to guess what it had been. Her mother said it might have been a boat. Mara thought Ada had been listening to someone behind them. They could not know.

The uncertainty did not make the photograph less precious. It made the image more honest. Ada had been looking somewhere the camera did not record. The family could love her and still fail to know what she saw.

They placed the picture in a sleeve and wrote the date on the back. Mara’s mother asked if the photograph should go in the archive. Mara said that was her choice. Her mother said she wanted it to stay in the family, at least for now. The phrase *for now* seemed to leave a path open without promising that the path would be taken.

At the end of the afternoon, Mara carried the KEEP box to the front room. Her mother sat by the moved cabinet. The house looked unfamiliar in its rearrangement, yet every object seemed to retain the impression of the room in which it had once lived.

“Do you think we’re remembering her properly?” her mother asked.

“No.”

Her mother smiled, sad and unsurprised. “Good.”

“We’re remembering her as we can.”

“That’s what I meant.”

Mara left with no papers. She had come to help sort the archive and gone home with nothing she could file. The afternoon remained with her anyway: her mother’s hand on the photograph, the soft sound of cardboard closing, the word *for now* making a small room around what neither of them could decide.
