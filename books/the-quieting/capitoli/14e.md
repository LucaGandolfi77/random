# Chapter 14e — The Map’s New Edition

Nell’s second edition of the city map was printed in late autumn. It included a note that the routes were reviewed by residents and could change, a date beside each accessibility update, and a way to request a correction without giving a name. The paper was thicker than the first run. The colors were less attractive and easier to distinguish for people with limited vision. Nell had insisted on both changes.

At the library launch, the city officer asked whether she would speak about her mother. Nell said no. The officer offered to remove the question from the program. Nell said it could remain on the printed list if they also included a question about the map’s maintenance. The officer agreed.

The first question came from a child who wanted to know why the map did not show the shortest route to the clinic. Nell explained that the shortest path had a steep stair and no rail. The child said his grandmother could not use it. Nell showed him the alternate route, which took six minutes longer and passed the bakery. He said his grandmother liked the bakery.

The next question came from a reporter who asked whether Nell had found mapping therapeutic. She said she liked making maps. Sometimes the work helped her think; sometimes it was only work. The reporter asked whether she had “overcome” her grief. Nell said she did not know what that would mean and did not think the word belonged to the map.

Mara stood near the back with Reva. She felt the old pull to protect Nell from a story that would reduce her, and the newer understanding that Nell could answer for herself. Reva’s hand rested on the chair beside her, not on Nell’s shoulder. Their restraint was not distance. It was trust practiced in public.

After the launch, Nell found Mara looking at the updated route near the footbridge. The bridge was still marked, but without a special symbol. The map did not erase what had happened there. It also did not insist that every person who crossed it carry the same story.

“Do you like it?” Nell asked.

“I like that it tells me where the ramp is.”

“That’s a very practical answer.”

“I’m trying to respect the map.”

Nell laughed. They stood together while people came and went. The map was not a memorial, a treatment, or a promise. It was a public object whose accuracy depended on people continuing to look at the city and tell one another when it had changed.

When Mara left, Nell stayed to answer questions. She did not look like a girl who had finished grieving. She looked like someone who had work to do and a bus to catch afterward. The distinction mattered to Mara. The future did not need to announce itself as hope in order to be lived.
