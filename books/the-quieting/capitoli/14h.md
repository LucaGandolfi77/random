# Chapter 14h — The Date in the Margin

On the anniversary of Ada’s death, Mara did not go to work. She had asked for the day off a month earlier and had told the clinic only that it was personal leave. In the morning she visited her mother. They did not go to the cemetery. They sat in the kitchen and read the old map of the market, where Ada had once stolen cherries and hidden the pits on the windowsill.

Her mother remembered the exact price of the cherries. Mara remembered the stained fingers. Neither remembered what they had eaten that night. The details made a small, incomplete room in which Ada could appear without being asked to explain her whole life.

Jonah came in the afternoon. He did not bring flowers. He brought the archive copy of the letter Tomas had written on his birthday, which Mina had approved for the family to keep. The letter was brief and mostly concerned with a broken radio. Mara’s mother asked if it was all right to read it aloud. Jonah said yes. When she reached a sentence about how “the static sounded like rain in a room with no windows,” she stopped, unable to continue.

Mara took the page and read the rest. The words did not make Tomas present. They let his attention enter the kitchen for a moment: the radio, the weather, the sound of a room. The two families sat around the table with their losses neither equal nor separate.

Afterward, Mara walked alone to the market. The cherry seller had gone. A new shop sold oranges, pears, and imported fruit with small labels. She bought a paper bag of cherries anyway. They were more expensive than her mother remembered. She carried them home without opening the bag.

At Jonah’s flat, she set the cherries on the counter. He asked if she wanted to eat them. She said later. They made tea and stood by the window. The anniversary had not behaved as she feared. It had not broken her, nor had it passed without pain. It had been a day with a beginning, several hours, and an evening she did not want to spend alone.

She thought of the madeleine, of the way Proust’s narrator had found time folded into taste. She understood that her own memory did not need to be involuntary to be real; sometimes she went looking for the past and found only the present waiting there. The cherries would not open a door to Ada exactly as she had been. They were fruit on a counter, and Mara’s sister had once stolen them, and Mara could hold both facts without asking one to prove the other.

Before bed, she placed the bag in the refrigerator. In the morning she and Jonah ate the cherries at the table, spitting the pits into a bowl. Some were sweet, some sour. Neither of them said what they meant.
