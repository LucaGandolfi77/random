# Chapter 14i — The Other Side of the Report

The year after the report, the city’s public library began keeping a copy beside the map of the harbor. Some people read the executive summary. Others used the thick report to prop open the reading-room window when the latch jammed. The librarian put a note on the cover asking that the volume not be moved without telling staff.

Mara found it one afternoon when she went to the library with her mother. Her mother had come to return a book and wanted to see the map. She read the report’s title, then asked whether Ada was in it.

“Not by name.”

“Does it say what happened?”

“It says there was a delayed follow-up and no call.”

“Does it say you were going to call her?”

“No.”

Her mother looked at the report, then at Mara. “Would you want it to?”

Mara considered the question. She had once wanted the report to contain every piece of the failure, including her own. She had wanted the public record to become a place where she could confess. But the report was not her diary, and Ada’s case was not a vessel for Mara’s remorse. Her mother had chosen what the family could share. Mara had to let that limit remain.

“No,” she said. “That belongs to us.”

They sat by the window. The library’s copy of the report lay closed on the table, its weight holding down a stack of loose papers. Mara watched a child trace the route on Nell’s map with one finger. The child paused at the marked bench, then looked toward the door as if deciding whether to ask someone where it was.

Her mother reached for Mara’s hand. It was an unusual gesture between them, less habitual than the practical acts that had defined their care. Mara held it. The contact did not make them understand each other completely. It marked a moment in which neither needed to be understood in order to remain together.

On the way home, her mother asked if Mara was happy living with Jonah. Mara said yes, sometimes, and afraid, sometimes. Her mother said that sounded more like a life than a verdict. Mara laughed.

That evening she read the report again, not to find Ada but to see whether the public words still held what they had promised. They did not hold everything. They recorded a failure, its context, the decision to hide some outcomes, the voices that had objected, and the changes the city had attempted. The missing things remained missing. The record was neither absolution nor accusation enough to end the matter.

Mara closed the report and returned it to the library the next day. She did not take a copy home. She had learned that not every memory needed to be carried in her hands in order to remain hers.
