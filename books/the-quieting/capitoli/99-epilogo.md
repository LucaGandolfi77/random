# Epilogue — What Remains

A year later, the Room of Return at Bellwether Secondary had a wider door.

Nell’s map was printed on the wall beside it. The route included three benches, a public garden, and the bakery that sold pear pastries on wet days. She had finished her apprenticeship and was studying urban design. She still had difficult mornings. She had learned which people to call, which treatments helped, and that asking for help did not sign away the authorship of her life.

Mara worked four days a week at the clinic and one day with the independent review. The public report had named the failures without calling the whole service a fraud. Some people accused the city of weakness; others accused it of moving too slowly. Both were partly right. Sera returned to practice under supervision after the review. She and Mara had not become friends again, but they had learned to speak without pretending the past was finished.

Imani helped train the next group of clinicians. She still made mistakes, and had become less likely to mistake that fact for proof that she did not belong in the work. Lio trained new harbor dispatchers and kept asking the authority to fund the second-person check. The change had prevented errors; it had not solved the understaffing. Tavi no longer had regular appointments, but kept a copy of her assessment and knew how to return if her symptoms changed. She had helped revise the family-contact policy without making her private history public. None of them had become a success story in the simple sense. They had become people whose choices and difficulties continued after the reports stopped counting.

Nell’s map had gone through another revision. Reva was sleeping in her own room. Rusk and Leona had not repaired every old injury, but they had begun to meet without treating each visit as a verdict on the past.

Mara and Jonah shared the flat above the print shop. They had given up the temporary plan of keeping her old apartment, though Mara still kept its key on a ring in the kitchen drawer, beside the spare key to the new place. Sometimes she took a day alone. Sometimes Jonah did. They had learned the difference between space and departure, though not without having to ask again.

They were learning the difference between commitment and evidence of commitment.

One evening, Mara found him reading Proust at the kitchen table. A pear sat between them, bruised on one side.

“Does it taste like anything?” he asked.

She cut it in half. The flesh was sweet, then a little bitter near the skin. It reminded her of Ada. It reminded her of nothing. Both were true.

“Not everything has to be a doorway,” she said.

Jonah smiled. “You’re sure?”

“No. But I’m learning to leave some doors closed without calling them locked.”

They ate at the table. Through the open window came the harbor bells, and below them the city carried on: people crying in clinics, in kitchens, in the quiet rooms of buses; people laughing too loudly; clinicians staying late; friends learning not to fix what they could first witness.

The city’s new protocol was not a cure for grief, nor a guarantee against cognitive injury. It was a more honest agreement about what the city would try to do: notice distress without treating it as destiny; offer psychologists and psychiatrists before the crisis became an emergency; ask permission where it could; explain when it could not; review its own decisions afterward. The words did not make the rooms safe by themselves. People had to keep returning to them, correcting them, and funding them.

Mara sometimes still feared that her own emotions would make her less able to think. When the fear came, she told Dr. Chen, or Jonah, or herself; she did not treat the feeling as prophecy. She had learned that a mind could be distressed and still belong to the person living in it. The lesson did not remove the risk the city had named. It refused to turn the risk into a sentence passed in advance.

Mara rested her hand on Jonah’s. Outside, the tide moved in its old direction, and the lamps held their small circles against the dark.

For tonight, they were enough.
