## Draft

The ninth floor was an open plan of forty desks, and the forty desks were forty people with a screen and a counter, and the counter was the first thing I saw every morning. The claims came in from all over the country: the accidents, the losses, the fires. I read the claims all day, and the claims were the stories of the people who had lost something: the flood in the cellar, the fire in the kitchen, the crash on the roundabout. The claims were stories, and I read them like stories — and the reading, I learned later, was the only part of the job that was mine.

The claim of that week was Mrs. Whitfield's, from Hull: the flood in the cellar, the one that comes every winter now, the water that rises in the cellar and doesn't stop. Joan Whitfield, sixty-eight, a widow, forty years in the same house — the house her husband had built, and the husband was gone, and the water was rising. The claim was forty pages: the photographs of the water, the inventory of the things lost — the sofa of the wedding, the photographs of the children, the sewing machine. The sewing machine was in the inventory, and the sewing machine was the thing I read twice: the machine of the things that are made, and the machine of the things that are lost. The claim was a story, and the story was Mrs. Whitfield's, and the story didn't say the number: the number was mine. The number was the thing I wrote — the settlement, the excess, the exclusion — and the number was the thing the firm called the claim, and the claim was a story, and nobody asked. The firm didn't ask. The counter didn't ask. Nobody asked.

The measure was 1,600 hours a year, billable. On the screen, in the top right corner, the counter: hours logged, hours to log, variance. The measure of a modern life: it is measured in hours, and the hours are measured in money, and the money is measured in what is left. At the end of the month, of my salary — four thousand two hundred net — not much was left: the flat in Islington took sixteen hundred, the school run took two hundred, the tube took a hundred and forty, the food took three hundred, the bills took a hundred. What was left was the counter. The counter was what I kept: the hours.

The flat was at Islington, on the third floor, with the window that gives on the inner courtyard, and the inner courtyard, in September, was the courtyard of the bins and the bicycles: the courtyard that doesn't count. The flat was the flat of the woman who counts: the rent, the tube, the school — and the counting, at thirty-eight, was the thing I did instead of the living. The living was the thing the flat didn't have: the flat had the rent.

Ruth, at the desk next to mine, was forty-five and had twenty years of claims. Ruth had a theory of the machine, that she repeated every morning with the seriousness of someone who had read the newsletters: — They say it takes — she said. — Not next year. In two years. You'll see. — Ruth had a theory of the counter, a theory of the newsletters, and a theory of the firm, which she said was a machine that didn't know it was one. She didn't have a theory of the hours. She said there was no material for one. On her desk there were the things people keep: a photo of a dog, a cactus, the coffee always half full.

Sam, two desks down, was twenty-four and had the counter of the man who is learning: 800 hours, and the 800, at twenty-four, were the counter of the man who asks. Sam asked the question — not the question of the counter: the other question. The question nobody asked. He asked it at the coffee, in the afternoon, with the cup in his hand:

— What are the hours for? —

And the question, at twenty-four, was the question the floor didn't ask: the floor didn't ask, the firm didn't ask, the counter didn't ask. Sam asked. And the asking, at twenty-four, was the asking of the man who is learning: the learning of the question. I didn't answer. The answer was the thing I didn't have: I had the counter. The counter was the answer the floor gave, and the floor's answer, at thirty-eight, was the answer that ate the hours.

The machine was the thing the floor feared, and the fear was the thing the floor shared: we shared the fear like we shared the printer. The fear was communal, and the communal, in an office, is the thing you keep: you keep it because it is cheaper than the question. The question was the one Sam asked, and the question was the one Ruth didn't ask: what are the hours for? The not asking, I learned later, was the whole of the fear: you don't fear losing the hours; you fear finding out what they were for.

Polly was seven, and the school run was mine: seven fifty every morning, the gate at eight ten, and the run, in the morning, was the run of the mother who counts. Polly talked about the school play — the school play was in March, and she was the second shepherd — and the school play, in March, was the thing I put in the calendar with the care of someone who knows the calendar is a sieve. Miss Rowe was the teacher who directs, and Miss Rowe said the thing the teachers say: — The second shepherd doesn't have lines. The second shepherd has the crook. — And the crook, at seven, was the thing you keep: you keep it, and the keeping, at seven, is the whole of the play.

The Sunday call was Edward's. Edward was seventy-two, a widower in Yorkshire, with a garden and a hedge and a greenhouse, and the Sunday call was at five, and the call was the call of the daughter who counts. Edward talked about the garden — the roses, the hedge, the tomatoes — and the garden, in Yorkshire, was the thing he kept: he kept it fifty years, and the garden didn't count. Nobody counted Edward's garden. The garden was the thing you keep: you don't count it.

— How are you? — said Edward, every Sunday, with the voice of a father who asks.

— I'm fine — I said.

It was the first thing I said that wasn't true.
