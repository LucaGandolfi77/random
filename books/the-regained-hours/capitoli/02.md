## Draft

> "to be overly conscious is a sickness, a real, thoroughgoing sickness."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*

Thursday came, and the training was at ten, in the meeting room with the long table. The trainer was a man from the vendor, and the vendor was a firm I had never seen, and the assistant was on the screen: a window, a cursor, a field that waited. The field waited like the fields you fill in: with the care of someone who knows the field is a form. The assistant didn't have a face, and the assistant didn't have a name: the firm called it the assistant, and the assistant was the machine. The machine didn't ask questions — that was the first thing I learned. The machine waited.

The meeting room was the room of the trainings, and the room of the trainings was the room of the long table, and the long table was the table of the things that don't happen: the trainings, the reviews, the objectives. The room had a window that gave on the river, and the river, at ten in the morning, was the river of the things that flow: the water, the hours, the years. The trainer said the thing the trainers say: — The assistant reads. The assistant writes. The assistant doesn't ask. — And the not asking was the thing the room feared, and the fear was the thing the room shared: we shared the fear like we shared the river.

The first delegation was a claim: the flood in the cellar, the one from Hull — Mrs. Whitfield's claim, the claim of the sewing machine. The claim was forty pages, and the report was six, and the report took me four hours — four hours, every week, for six years. I gave the claim to the assistant, and the assistant didn't ask; the assistant read. Eleven minutes later the report was on the screen: six pages, the numbers right, the language right. The language was right and the language wasn't mine: the language was the machine's, and the machine's language, I learned later, was the thing that came back.

And the claim, on the screen, was the claim of Mrs. Whitfield: the water, the photographs, the sewing machine — and the claim, in the machine's window, was the claim that flowed: the water flowed in the cellar, and the water flowed in the claim, and the claim flowed in the machine, and the machine flowed in the eleven minutes, and the eleven minutes flowed in the counter, and the counter flowed in the top right corner of the screen, and the screen was in my eyes, and my eyes were the eyes of the woman who reads — the water, the sewing machine, the sofa of the wedding — and the sewing machine was the machine that made, and the machine on the screen was the machine that reads, and the two machines were the machines of the woman who counts: the one that makes, and the one that reads — and the reading, in the eleven minutes, was the thing the machine did, and the thing I didn't: I read, and the machine read, and the two readings were the same reading, said in two languages — and the language, in the eleven minutes, was the thing that came back.

The counter, in the top right corner, said four hours back. Four hours. Four hours, every week, for six years — and the four hours came back in eleven minutes, and nobody asked me what they were for. Not the counter, not the firm, not the machine: the machine doesn't ask you what the time is for. It gives you the hours, and the hours come back, and the hours are yours. Nobody asks. The not asking was the thing I feared the most, and the not asking, I learned later, was the whole of the gift: you give the hours, and the hours come back, and the hours are yours — and the yours, at thirty-eight, was the thing I didn't know what to do with.

To be overly conscious is a sickness, and I had been sick for a year: I counted the hours the way you count the days of a sentence — backwards, and without mercy. Four hours back, on the counter, in the top right corner: and I looked at the four hours the way you look at the money of a stranger. The four hours were mine, and the mine was the thing I feared: the mine had no form. The counter had given me a form for six years; the machine had taken the form and given me the hours, and the hours, without a form, were the thing I feared the most.

Ruth, at the desk next to mine, watched the report arrive in eleven minutes, and Ruth's face was the face of the floor: the floor said nothing, and the nothing said everything. Ruth said the thing the whole floor was thinking:

— It takes. —

— It gives — I said.

And the saying, at thirty-eight, was the first thing I said that was mine: the machine didn't ask, and I said. The saying was mine, and the saying, I learned later, was the whole of it: not the machine. The saying.

Sam, two desks down, asked the question — the question he had asked at the coffee, the week before:

— What are the hours for? —

And the question, at twenty-four, was the question I didn't answer, and the not answering was the thing I had instead of the answer: I had the counter. The counter was the answer the floor gave, and the floor's answer was the answer that ate the hours. Sam asked. And the asking, at twenty-four, was the asking of the man who is learning: the learning of the question — and the question, I learned later, was the thing the machine came to answer. Not the machine. The question.

The evening came, and the four hours were still on the counter, in the top right corner: four hours, mine, and the mine had no form. The train was at six, and the school run was at seven fifty, and the four hours were still there — four hours, and the fear of them. The fear was the thing the floor had given me, and the floor's fear, at thirty-eight, was the thing I carried: the machine gives, and the floor says it takes.

I gave it mine. It didn't ask what they were for.
