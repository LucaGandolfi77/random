## Draft

> "Above all, don't lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*

The counter, in the top right corner, said 2,400: 2,400 hours a year, and the 2,400 were the thing the firm loved. The top biller was me, and the top biller, at thirty-eight, was the thing the floor said: the floor said it at the printer, at the coffee, at the counter — the top biller, and the saying was the saying of the floor, and the saying was the thing I kept. The floor's saying, at thirty-eight, was the thing I had instead of the question: the question — what are the hours for? — was the thing nobody asked, and the not asking, at thirty-eight, was the thing I kept.

Nobody asked — except Sam. Sam, two desks down, was twenty-four and had the counter of the man who is learning, and Sam asked the question at the coffee, in the afternoon, with the cup in his hand: the question he had asked in September, and the question he asked again, in May, because the question was the thing the young ones do: they ask. The old ones don't ask. The old ones keep the not asking.

— What are the hours for? — said Sam. — You're the top biller. You must know. —

And the knowing, at thirty-eight, was the thing I didn't have: I had the counter. The counter was the answer the floor gave, and the floor's answer, at thirty-eight, was the answer that ate the hours. — I don't know — I said, and the saying was the first true thing I said on the floor: the not knowing, said out loud, at thirty-eight, was the thing I had instead of the counter. The counter was the thing I had instead of the answer, and the answer was the thing Sam had: the question. The young ones have the question. The old ones have the counter — and the counter, at twenty-four, was the thing Sam was learning to have. The learning, at twenty-four, was the thing Sam was doing instead of the asking: and the asking, at twenty-four, was the thing that would be eaten. The floor eats the asking. The floor eats the question — and the eating of the question, at twenty-four, was the thing I watched, and the watching was the thing I did instead of the teaching. The teaching, I learned later, was the thing I owed Sam: the teaching of the keeping.

Dana said the thing the firm says at the review: — The numbers are extraordinary. You are the future of this floor. — And the future of the floor, at thirty-eight, was the thing I had: the hours, and the hours' hours, and the machine's hours. The future was the thing the firm loved, and the firm's future, at thirty-eight, was the thing that ate the play — the play, in March, was the thing the counter ate, and the counter, in May, was the thing the firm loved. The firm's love was the thing the floor feared, and the floor feared the machine. The floor should have feared the love: the love of the firm, at thirty-eight, was the thing that eats the hours twice — once to the machine, once to the firm.

Above all, don't lie to yourself — the books say it (Dostoevsky), and the books say it about the man who lies. I was a woman who lied, and the lie was the lie of the floor: *I'm fine*. I said *I'm fine* every morning, at the coffee, at the counter, at the gate — and the saying, at thirty-eight, was the saying that wasn't true. The truth was the one I kept: the hours were not mine, the play was not attended, the garden was not visited. The truth was the thing I kept, and the keeping of the truth, at thirty-eight, was the thing that cost me the most: not the machine. The keeping.

And the counter, in the top right corner, was the thing that flowed: 2,400 hours, billable, and the hours flowed the way the water flows in the pipes of the building — down, always down, into the cellars of the firm — and the cellars of the firm were the cellars of the top biller, and the top biller was in the cellars, and the cellars were in the counter, and the counter was in the top right corner of the screen, and the screen was in my eyes, and my eyes were the eyes of the woman who won — the winning, the winning, the winning of the floor — and the winning was the thing the firm loved, and the firm's love was the thing that flowed with the hours, down, into the cellars, where the play wasn't, where the garden wasn't, where the Sundays weren't — and the Sundays, in the cellars of the firm, were the Sundays of the top biller: the top biller's Sundays were the Sundays at the office, and the office was the thing I won, and the winning was the thing I lost. The winning was the thing I lost.

A woman like any other, living as she should — the books say it (Tolstoy), and the books say it about the man who lived for appearances. I was a woman like any other, living as I should: the counter, the objective, the numbers. And the living as I should, at thirty-eight, was the living of the appearances: the appearances were the thing the firm loved, and the appearances, I learned later, were the thing the machine came to take. Not the hours. The appearances. The machine doesn't take the hours: it takes the appearances, and the appearances were the thing I had instead of the life.

Ruth, at the desk next to mine, said the thing the floor was saying: — You're the top biller. You won. — And the winning, at thirty-eight, was the winning of the floor: the floor said the winning, and the saying was the thing I kept. The floor's winning, at thirty-eight, was the thing the firm loved: the firm loved the winners, and the winners, I learned later, were the thing the machine came to make. The machine doesn't make the winners: the machine makes the hours, and the hours are yours. And the yours, at thirty-eight, was the thing I didn't have: I had the winning, and the winning was the firm's.

The evening came, and the counter was in the top right corner: 2,400, and the 2,400 were the thing the firm loved. I was the top biller, and the top biller, at thirty-eight, was the thing the firm loved. And the firm's love, I learned later, was the thing that ate the hours twice.
