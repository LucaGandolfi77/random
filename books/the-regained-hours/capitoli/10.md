## Draft

The last Sunday in the ward was the Sunday of the garden: Edward was moved to the rehabilitation unit, and the rehabilitation unit had a window that gave on the garden — the real garden, the hospital's garden, with the hedge and the roses. The hedge was the hedge of the books, and the hedge, at seventy-two, was the hedge that measured: the hedge was the measure that made the infinite possible. Edward looked at the hedge from the window, and the looking, at seventy-two, was the looking of the man who keeps: the garden was the thing he kept, and the keeping was the thing the stroke had touched. The hand kept, and the keeping, at seventy-two, was the thing the doctors counted: the doctors counted the hand, and the hand kept.

The math I did on the train back, on the phone, in the top right corner: the counter, and the counter's hours. Six hundred hours a year, returned by the machine — twelve a week, and the twelve were the hours of the claims, the reports, the spreadsheets. Six hundred hours a year, returned, and the six hundred were the thing I had been given: and the six hundred, I had given away. To the firm — the objective, the numbers, the top biller. The math said one thing: six hundred hours given, and zero kept. Zero. The zero, at thirty-eight, was the thing the counter couldn't say: the counter counted, and the zero didn't count. The zero was the thing I had instead of the life.

Pleasure dies when desire is fulfilled — the books say it (Leopardi), and the books say it about the desire of people. The firm's desire renewed itself: the more hours I gave it, the more hours it wanted, and the wanting was the thing that ate the play, the garden, the Sundays. The desire that is born there, in the firm, is the desire that doesn't die: it is born there, and the being born there, at thirty-eight, was the thing the machine had given me. The machine gave the hours; the firm gave the desire; and the desire, at thirty-eight, was the thing that ate the hours. The machine doesn't ask what the time is for. The firm asks, and the firm's asking is the asking that eats: the objective, the numbers, the counter.

To be overly conscious is a sickness — the books say it (Dostoevsky), and I had been sick for a year: the counting was the sickness, and the sickness was the thing I had instead of the keeping. The counting didn't keep: the counting ate. And the eating of the counting, at thirty-eight, was the thing the machine came to stop: the machine gives the hours, and the hours are yours, and the yours is the thing you keep — or the thing you give away. The keeping was the thing I hadn't done.

Joan Whitfield was the thing the phone had, that afternoon: the claim — the flood in the cellar, the one from Hull — was the claim of Joan Whitfield, and the claim was the thing I had read in September, and the reading was the thing the machine did now. The machine read the claims. But the claim was a story, and the story was the thing I called: I called the number — the number on the claim, the number that wasn't the counter — and the calling, at thirty-eight, was the calling of the woman who reads: the reading of the story.

— Mrs. Whitfield? — I said. — It's Iris, from Meridian. The insurance. I read your claim. —

— The claim — said Joan, and the saying was the saying of the woman who had lost: the losing, at sixty-eight, was the losing of the things that don't count. — They took the sewing machine. My husband's mother's. Forty years in the kitchen. —

And the sewing machine, on the phone, was the machine of the things that are made: the machine that made, and the machine that was lost, and the losing was the thing the claim said — the claim said the number, and the number was the thing I wrote in September: the settlement, the excess, the exclusion. And the number, on the phone, was the thing that didn't say: the number didn't say the sewing machine. The sewing machine said the forty years — and the forty years, at sixty-eight, were the years of the kitchen: the kitchen of the bread — the bread of my mother, the bread of Margaret — and the bread, on the phone, was the thing that didn't count: the bread is planted.

— The water comes every winter now — said Joan. — They say it's the rain. It isn't the rain. It's the sea. —

— The sea — I said.

— The sea is coming back — said Joan. — The sea doesn't ask. It comes back. —

And the sea, on the phone, was the sea of the books: the sea that comes back, and the coming back, at sixty-eight, was the coming back of the things that don't ask — and the not asking was the thing the sea and the machine had: the machine doesn't ask; the sea doesn't ask. The things that don't ask are the things that give: the sea gives the water, the machine gives the hours — and the giving, at thirty-eight, was the thing the books say: the things you keep, you don't count. The sea doesn't count. The machine doesn't count. And the keeping of the giving, at thirty-eight, was the thing I hadn't done.

The evening came, and the train was at seven, and the train back was the train of the daughter who counts. The counter was on the phone, in the top right corner, and the counter said the hours, and the hours said 2,400. The 2,400 were the thing I had instead of the life, and the life was the thing I had instead of nothing: the life was the thing the machine had given me, and I had given it away. The math said one thing, and the thing was the thing the books say: the things you keep, you don't count — you keep them. And the keeping, at thirty-eight, was the thing I hadn't done.

I had the hours, and I had given them away. The keeping was the thing I hadn't done: and the keeping, at thirty-eight, was the whole of it.
