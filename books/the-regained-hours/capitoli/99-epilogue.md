## Draft

I am in London, and London, in January, has the grey — and the grey, at thirty-nine, is the grey I know: the grey is the thing you don't choose; you inherit it. The counter is on the screen, in the top right corner, and the counter says 1,800: 1,800 hours a year, and the 1,800 are the thing I have instead of 2,400 — the six hundred hours, returned, and the six hundred are the thing I keep. The keeping, at thirty-nine, is the thing I do instead of the counting: the counter went down, and the going down is the going down of the woman who keeps.

The calls are the calls of the Sundays: Polly at the school run, Edward at the garden. Edward is at home now, and the garden is the garden he keeps: the hand kept, and the keeping, at seventy-two, is the thing the garden knew. The tomatoes are the thing the neighbour gives, and the giving, at seventy-two, is the thing the garden taught: the things that are planted, you don't count. You plant them. And the planting, at thirty-nine, is the thing I learned in the garden: the machine gives the hours, and the hours are the things you plant.

Sam is at the desk two down, and Sam is twenty-five now, and the counter of Sam says 900 — the counter of the man who is learning, and the learning, at twenty-five, is the thing Sam does instead of the asking: Sam doesn't ask the question anymore. The question — what are the hours for? — is the question Sam answered: Sam keeps. The keeping, at twenty-five, is the thing I taught him, and the teaching, at thirty-nine, is the thing the machine gave: the machine gave the hours, and the hours gave the teaching, and the teaching gave the keeping. The things you keep, you don't count: you keep them — and Sam, at twenty-five, keeps: he keeps the hours of the Friday afternoons, and the Fridays, at twenty-five, are the Fridays of the kept hours.

Joan Whitfield threaded the machine. She called me in November — the phone, the number that wasn't the counter — and the calling was the calling of the woman who sews: — I threaded it — said Joan. — It took the afternoon. The machine took the afternoon, and the afternoon was mine. — And the afternoon, at sixty-eight, was the afternoon of the kept hours: the machine gave the hours, and the hours gave the afternoon, and the afternoon gave the sewing — the sewing of the things that are made, and the making, at sixty-eight, is the thing you keep. The machine doesn't keep. The afternoons keep.

And the kitchen, in the flat, is the kitchen of the bread: the bread I bake on Saturdays, the way my mother baked — Margaret, who never counted, who said the bread doesn't count. The bread is planted. The baking, at thirty-nine, is the thing I learned from a mother who is gone ten years: and the learning, at thirty-nine, is the learning that arrives by smell (Proust) — the smell of the flour, the smell of the yeast, the smell of the kitchen of the things that are planted. The mother is in the smell, and the smell is in the kitchen, and the kitchen is in the flat, and the flat is in the counter — and the counter, in the top right corner, says 1,800: and the 1,800 are the hours I keep.

The moral, the books say, is the thing you write at the end — and the end is the thing you have instead of the beginning. The moral is this, and the moral is the thing I write for the first time:

The time regained wasn't in the past. It was in the machine. Proust looked for it in a madeleine — a cake, and the cake brought back a life; the past doesn't call: it arrives, by smell (Proust). I looked for it in an assistant — a machine, and the machine gave back hours; the future doesn't call either: it arrives, by machine. And the machine's time is real time: it gives you the hours, and the hours are yours, and nobody asks you what they are for. Nobody asks. You choose. And the choosing, at thirty-nine, is the whole of it: not the machine. The choosing.

The machine gave me back my life. Not the life I had lost: the life I hadn't had yet. The life I hadn't had yet is the life of the kept hours: the play, the garden, the Sundays, the bread — the things that don't count, and the things that don't count are the things you keep. The industry says the machine takes. The industry is wrong, and the wrongness of the industry is the thing this book says: the machine gives — and the giving is the thing the floor should have said, and the saying is the thing the floor learned in a year. The machine gives. The hours come back. And the hours, this time, I kept.

The things you keep, you don't count: you keep them. And the keeping, at thirty-nine, is the thing the books say: the things you keep, you don't count — you keep them. The collection — the books of the girl who went to Thailand, the books of the man who crossed the sea, the books of the man who carried a folded paper — says it in Italian: le cose che si tengono, non si contano. Si tengono. The things you keep, you don't count: you keep them. And the keeping is the thing the machine came to give: the machine gives the hours, and the hours are the things you keep.

I am writing a book, in the evening, at the desk of the flat, with the counter on the screen and the grey at the window — and the writing, in the stream, is the writing that keeps: the book keeps the hours, and the hours keep the book, and the keeping is the thing the machine gave — the machine gave the hours, and the hours gave the evenings, and the evenings gave the book, and the book gave the keeping, and the keeping gave the life — and the life, at thirty-nine, was the life of the kept hours, and the kept hours were the things I planted, and the things I planted were the things that keep. The kitchen keeps. The garden keeps. The book keeps. And the keeping, in the stream, is the keeping of the woman who counts — the woman who counted, and the counting stopped, and the stopping was the thing the machine gave: the machine gave the hours, and the hours are yours, and the yours is the thing you keep.

The book is called *The Regained Hours*, and the book is the thing I write for the first time: and the first time, at thirty-nine, is the thing you don't choose. You choose it.

The machine doesn't ask you what the time is for. You choose. And the choosing, at thirty-nine, is the whole of it.
