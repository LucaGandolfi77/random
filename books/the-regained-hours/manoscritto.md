# Prologue — The Hours Counter


> "the only true paradise is always the paradise that we have lost."
>
> — Marcel Proust, *Time Regained*

The counter sat in the top right corner of the screen, and the counter was the first thing I saw every morning: 1,600 hours a year, billable. The measure of a modern life, and the measure was mine. I looked at the counter the way you look at a test result: with the care of someone who knows the numbers inside are the numbers of her own life, and that the numbers are never good. The counter didn't say the hours; the counter said me. One thousand six hundred: that was my name, spelled in hours.

The ninth floor was an open plan of forty desks, and the forty desks were forty people with a screen and a counter, and the light was the light of the fluorescents — the light that doesn't say day or night, and it's fine, because in offices the time isn't needed: the hours are. The windows gave on the city — the city that doesn't look back, the city of the cranes and the rain — and the light of the city was the light of the screens: blue, flat, the light of the things you count. Sam, the junior, sat two desks down, and Sam was twenty-four and had a counter of his own — a smaller counter, a counter that said 800 — and the smaller counter, at twenty-four, was the counter of the man who is learning to count.

The announcement email came on a Monday in September. It said the firm had deployed an assistant — the machine — and that the machine would take the hours. The industry said the same thing, all year, in the newsletters and the panels and the headlines: the machine takes. The machine takes your hours, your job, your profession; the machine takes, and you are taken. I read the email the way you read a verdict: with the hand that was looking for the coffee. The email said *onboarding on Thursday*. Onboarding. The word was right and it wasn't true: after Thursday I wouldn't be onboarded; I would be replaced. That was the word the industry used, and the industry used it for all of us.

Ruth, at the desk next to mine, read the email twice and said the thing the whole floor was saying:

— They say it takes. —

They say it takes. The floor said it, the newsletters said it, the panels said it: the machine takes, and you are taken. I said it too, for a year — I said it so loudly that I believed it. And the saying, I learned later, was the thing that cost me the most: not the machine. The saying. You can live inside a sentence for a year, and the sentence doesn't open: you do.

The counter sat in the top right corner, and I looked at it every morning for a year, and the counter was the only thing I kept: the hours. One thousand six hundred a year, and not one of them mine. The firm's, all of them — and the firm's hours, I learned later, were the thing the machine came to return.

And the counter, in the mornings, was the thing that flowed: one thousand six hundred hours, billable, and the hours flowed the way the water flows in the pipes of the building — down, always down, into the cellars of the firm — and the cellars of the firm were the cellars where my mother had kept the bread, the bread she baked on Saturdays, and the smell of the bread was in the pipes, and the pipes were in the walls, and the walls were in the counter, and the counter was in the top right corner of the screen, and the screen was in my eyes, and my eyes were the eyes of the woman who counts — one thousand six hundred, billable, and the years flowed with the hours, ten years since the kitchen, ten years since the bread, and the bread was the thing I didn't bake anymore, and the baking was the thing I didn't do, and the doing was the thing the counter ate — and the counter, in the mornings, flowed, and I flowed with it, down, into the cellars of the firm, where the bread wasn't.

The machine doesn't ask you what the time is for. That is the thing the industry doesn't say, and the thing this book says: the machine doesn't ask. It gives you the hours, and the hours come back, and nobody asks you what they are for — not the firm, not the counter, not the machine. Nobody asks. You choose. And the choosing, I learned later, was the whole of it: not the machine. The choosing.

This is the story of a year, and of how a woman gives the hours of her office to a machine and the hours come back — and of how the hours that came back were the life she hadn't had yet. The paradise, the books say, is the paradise that we have lost (Proust). I hadn't lost a paradise. I was about to find one: it was in the machine, and it was made of hours.

I begin at the beginning, which was an office, and the office cost me 1,600 hours a year.

# Chapter 1 — The Claims

— What are the hours for? —

And the asking, at twenty-four, was the asking of the man who is learning: the learning of the question. I didn't answer. The answer was the thing I didn't have: I had the counter. The counter was the answer the floor gave, and the floor's answer, at thirty-eight, was the answer that ate the hours.

The machine was the thing the floor feared, and the fear was the thing the floor shared: we shared the fear like we shared the printer. The fear was communal, and the communal, in an office, is the thing you keep: you keep it because it is cheaper than the question. The question was the one Sam asked, and the question was the one Ruth didn't ask: what are the hours for? The not asking, I learned later, was the whole of the fear: you don't fear losing the hours; you fear finding out what they were for.

Polly was seven, and the school run was mine: seven fifty every morning, the gate at eight ten, and the run, in the morning, was the run of the mother who counts. Polly talked about the school play — the school play was in March, and she was the second shepherd — and the school play, in March, was the thing I put in the calendar with the care of someone who knows the calendar is a sieve. The second shepherd has the crook. — And the crook, at seven, was the thing you keep: you keep it, and the keeping, at seven, is the whole of the play.

The Sunday call was Edward's. Edward talked about the garden — the roses, the hedge, the tomatoes — and the garden, in Yorkshire, was the thing he kept: he kept it fifty years, and the garden didn't count. — How are you? — said Edward, every Sunday, with the voice of a father who asks.

— I'm fine — I said.

# Chapter 2 — The Assistant

# Chapter 3 — The Hours Return


The weeks passed, and the assistant took the hours the way the counter had taken them: one at a time, without asking. Twelve a week, in the end: the claims, the reports, the spreadsheets — the hours of the office, given, and the hours came back. Twelve a week, six hundred a year: six hundred hours, and the six hundred were mine, and the mine had no form. The form was the thing the counter had given me, and the counter's form, at thirty-eight, was the thing I missed: the hours, without a form, were the thing I filled the fastest way I knew.

I filled them with work. The first reaction, and the reaction was the reaction of the floor: more claims, more reports, more hours. The firm loved it. Dana, the head of department, said the thing the firm says: — The assistant works for you. You work for the firm. That's the deal. — And the deal, at thirty-eight, was the deal I had: the machine gives, and the firm takes. I gave the six hundred hours back to the firm, and the firm ate them, and the counter went up.

Pleasure dies when desire is fulfilled — the books say it (Leopardi), and the books say it about the desire of people. The machine's desire was different: the machine's desire renewed itself. The more claims I gave it, the more claims it wanted: the desire of the machine didn't die when the desire was fulfilled. It was born there. And the desire that is born there, in the machine, is the desire of the firm: the firm's desire renewed itself in the machine, and the machine's desire was mine to fill. I filled it. The counter went up, and the going up, at thirty-eight, was the going up I had learned: the hours don't count, they go up.

The saying of the floor — they say it takes — was still on the floor, and the saying was wrong, and the wrongness of the saying was the thing I didn't see: the machine didn't take. And the hours, in the evenings, were the hours that flowed — one thousand six hundred, billable, and the hours flowed the way the water flows in the pipes of the building, down, always down, into the cellars of the firm — and the cellars of the firm were the cellars where the bread was, my mother's bread, the bread she baked on Saturdays, and the bread was in the pipes, and the pipes were in the counter, and the counter was in the top right corner of the screen, and the screen was in my eyes, and my eyes were the eyes of the woman who fills — twelve a week, six hundred a year, and the filling was the thing I did instead of the keeping, and the keeping was the thing I didn't do, and the not doing was the thing the counter ate — and the counter, in the evenings, flowed, and I flowed with it, down, into the cellars of the firm, where the bread wasn't, where the kitchen wasn't, where the mother wasn't — and the mother, in the cellars of the firm, was the mother of the bread, and the bread was the thing I didn't bake anymore, and the baking was the thing I didn't do.

The counter didn't say the six hundred; the counter said 1,900. The six hundred were the thing I didn't keep, and the keeping, I learned later, was the whole of it: not the machine. The keeping.

I thought the hours were mine. They were the firm's — and the firm was eating them twice.

# Chapter 6 — The Garden

## A note on the allusions

The protagonist's reflections contain subtle allusions to Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Proust and Leopardi, marked in parentheses: they are not quotations — they are ways of thinking borrowed, the way languages are borrowed. The epigraphs at the start of the chapters are real quotations: Marcel Proust (*Time Regained*), Fyodor Dostoevsky (*Notes from Underground*, *The Brothers Karamazov*), Leo Tolstoy (*The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, *War and Peace*), Giacomo Leopardi (*A Silvia*). The streams of consciousness — the flowing passages where the dead mother lives — are the register of the expansion.
